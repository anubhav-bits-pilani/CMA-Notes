Roadmap topic: Liquidity & Solvency Ratios: Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, Cash Ratio, Net Debt/EBITDA, Interest Coverage (EBIT/Interest), and Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

Overview

Liquidity ratios ask whether a company can meet obligations falling due over the next operating cycle; solvency ratios ask whether its capital structure and earnings can carry its debt over the long run. Together they are the creditor's view of the balance sheet — the counterpart to the shareholder's view in DuPont ROE analysis (t2). A company can post a high ROE precisely because it runs high leverage; this topic gives you the tools to judge whether that leverage is safe. For FP&A and modelling roles in India, these ratios appear in three recurring deliverables: the covenant compliance certificate sent to lenders each quarter, the Schedule III "Additional Regulatory Information" ratio note in the annual report, and the covenant-test block at the bottom of every 3-statement model's debt schedule.

The topic is built as a ladder. First, short-term liquidity (s01): current, quick and cash ratios, and the reclassification of current maturities of long-term debt that mechanically moves them. Second, leverage stocks (s02): debt/equity for reporting, net debt/EBITDA for covenants and rating agencies, and TOL/TNW for Indian bank appraisal. Third, coverage flows (s03): interest cover on EBIT and EBITDA bases, DSCR, and fixed-charge cover that brings lease payments into the denominator. Fourth, the accounting overlay (s04): how Ind AS 116 moved every one of these ratios and why covenant definitions must specify frozen or current GAAP. Fifth, the consequence layer (s05): what happens when a covenant is breached — classification of borrowings under Ind AS 1 with its Indian carve-out — and how CRISIL, ICRA, CARE and India Ratings weave the same ratios into credit ratings. Finally, the statutory layer (s06): the Schedule III ratio disclosures and the discipline of explaining variances above 25%.

The key concepts formalise the arithmetic: the current and quick ratio formulas (c01), the cash ratio and the definition of liquid investments (c02), net debt/EBITDA and EBIT interest cover (c03), the lender-basis DSCR (c04), and the Ind AS 1 waiver carve-out (c05). Throughout, the discipline is the same as in the rest of the week: every ratio has a written numerator/denominator definition in a dictionary, applied identically across peers and years, with leases, cash, other income and one-offs treated consistently.

Related concepts to study

IAS 1 2020/2022 amendments versus the Ind AS 1 carve-out

IAS 1 2020/2022 amendments on covenants and liability classification vs Ind AS 1 carve-out

The IASB's January 2020 amendment ("Classification of Liabilities as Current or Non-current") replaced the "unconditional right" wording with a "right to defer settlement at the end of the reporting period", clarified that classification is unaffected by management's intentions, and addressed settlement in own equity. The October 2022 amendment ("Non-current Liabilities with Covenants") added that only covenants an entity must comply with on or before the reporting date affect classification, and required disclosures about covenant-linked non-current liabilities (carrying amount, covenant nature, facts indicating difficulty in compliance). Both apply under IFRS from 1 January 2024. Ind AS 1 retains its para 74 carve-out on post-year-end waivers; whether MCA has notified the IASB changes into Ind AS must be checked at each reporting date. Analysts comparing Indian and IFRS reporters must restate classification on a common basis.

Credit analysis, Altman Z and covenant tests in models

Credit analysis linkage → Week 7 Altman Z; debt schedule and covenant tests in models → Week 14

The liquidity and solvency ratios here are the inputs to Week 7's credit-scoring models. Altman's Z-score combines working capital/total assets (a liquidity measure), retained earnings/total assets, EBIT/total assets, market value of equity/total liabilities (a solvency measure) and sales/total assets; the emerging-markets variant drops sales/assets. Understanding what drives each ratio explains why a Z-score moves. In Week 14 the same ratios become formula-driven covenant tests in the 3-statement model: minimum DSCR, maximum net debt/EBITDA and minimum interest cover are computed per forecast year, compared with thresholds and surfaced in a flag row that feeds the model's summary dashboard and drives cash sweep or equity-cure logic.

Project finance DSCR, sculpting and DSRA

Project finance DSCR, sculpting and DSRA → Week 21

In project finance (roads, renewables, transmission), DSCR is computed on cash flow available for debt service (CFADS = revenue − opex − tax − working capital changes − maintenance capex), not on accounting profit. Lenders size the debt so that DSCR in every period meets a target (e.g., 1.20–1.30x for contracted renewables), and sculpt repayments so principal in each period equals CFADS ÷ target DSCR − interest, giving a flat DSCR profile. A debt service reserve account (DSRA), typically funded with one or two quarters of debt service, protects against short-term CFADS shortfalls. Week 21 builds this; the corporate DSCR here is its balance-sheet cousin.

Common pitfalls

Ranking net-cash companies on net debt/EBITDA

Treating negative net debt/EBITDA as "lower is better" without separating net-cash companies.

The mistake is to sort a peer table on net debt/EBITDA and treat −2.0x as "safer" than −0.5x, or to average negative and positive values into a sector mean. It happens because spreadsheets sort numbers mechanically and the ratio's sign is easy to ignore. Once net debt is negative the ratio no longer measures solvency — the company could repay all debt today — and the magnitude only describes how much surplus cash it holds relative to earnings, which is a capital-allocation question. Correct treatment: report net-cash companies as "net cash" with the cash amount and cash/EBITDA separately; compute peer medians only over levered companies; use gross debt/EBITDA or interest cover for the net-cash group if needed. In review, look for any negative values in a leverage column feeding an average or a rank formula.

Excluding lease liabilities with post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA

Excluding lease liabilities from debt but using post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA (understates leverage).

Analysts often pull "borrowings" straight from the balance sheet and "EBITDA" from the P&L. Post-2019, that EBITDA has rent added back (ROU depreciation and lease interest sit below it), but the corresponding lease liability sits outside "borrowings". The ratio therefore shows less leverage than either a consistent post-116 or pre-116 computation. For retailers, QSR and airlines the understatement can be 2–3 turns. Correct treatment: either include lease liabilities in debt with post-116 EBITDA, or exclude leases and deduct cash lease payments from EBITDA. Detect in review by checking whether the ratio dictionary lists lease liabilities and whether EBITDA margin jumped in FY2019-20 for the companies concerned.

Reading a current ratio above 1 as safe

Reading a current ratio above 1 as safe when inventory is slow-moving or receivables are disputed.

The current ratio counts every current asset at book value. Slow-moving or obsolete inventory not yet provided for, receivables under dispute or over a year old, and advances to related parties can all sit in current assets and keep the ratio above 1 while the company struggles to pay suppliers. People make the mistake because the ratio is quick to compute and textbook benchmarks are memorable. Correct treatment: adjust the numerator for non-realisable amounts using inventory ageing, ECL provisioning matrices and the receivables ageing note (Schedule III requires ageing of trade receivables, including disputed amounts), and supplement with quick and cash ratios, DSO/DIO trends and operating cash flow. In review, compare the trend in the current ratio with operating cash flow and the payables ageing: a stable ratio with rising DPO and falling OCF is a red flag.

Q2. Covenant breach, waiver and rating [L3]

A ₹800 crore term loan has covenants of net debt/EBITDA ≤ 3.0x and DSCR ≥ 1.25x, defined under current GAAP with lease liabilities included. At 31 March 2025: borrowings ₹2,400 crore, lease liabilities ₹600 crore, cash ₹200 crore, EBITDA (post-116) ₹880 crore; DSCR 1.18x. The bank waives the breach by letter dated 30 April 2025; the board approves the accounts on 25 May 2025. Current assets ₹1,900 crore; current liabilities before any reclassification ₹1,500 crore (including ₹150 crore CMLTD on this loan). (a) Test both covenants. (b) Classify the loan under Ind AS and IFRS and compute the current ratio in each case. (c) Explain what a rating agency would likely do and which liquidity descriptor it might assign. (d) What would change if the covenant were frozen GAAP and the lease liabilities arose from leases that were operating leases under Ind AS 17?

💡 Hint: Under frozen GAAP, remove lease liabilities from debt and deduct the corresponding rent from EBITDA.

Q3. Bank appraisal note for a working-capital renewal [L2]

A trading company seeks renewal of a ₹120 crore cash-credit limit. Compute TOL/TNW with and without quasi-equity treatment and the current ratio on reported and bank (ex-CMLTD) bases, and assess against norms of TOL/TNW ≤ 4.0x and current ratio ≥ 1.33x.
