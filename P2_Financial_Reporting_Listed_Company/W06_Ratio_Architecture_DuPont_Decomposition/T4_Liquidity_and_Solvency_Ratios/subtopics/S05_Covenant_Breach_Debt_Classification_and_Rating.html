Subtopic note

Covenant Breach, Debt Classification and Rating Agency Methods

Scope: Covenant breach and current classification of borrowings; rating agency methodology (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings)

Why it matters

A covenant breach is where ratio analysis becomes an accounting and audit event. If a borrower breaches a financial covenant on or before the reporting date, the lender typically gets a right to recall the loan — and under Ind AS 1 that can force the entire long-term borrowing into current liabilities, collapsing the current ratio, triggering cross-default clauses, raising going-concern questions under SA 570 and requiring disclosure. Rating agencies, meanwhile, use a defined set of leverage and coverage ratios to assign ratings that drive the borrower's cost of debt, investor eligibility (many mutual funds and insurers have rating floors) and, for listed debt, disclosure obligations under SEBI LODR. FP&A teams that forecast covenant headroom and understand agency ratio definitions can see a downgrade or breach coming two quarters early.

Core notes

Classification rule: Ind AS 1

Ind AS 1.69(d) classifies a liability as current when the entity does not have the right (as worded in Ind AS 1, an unconditional right) at the end of the reporting period to defer settlement for at least twelve months. When a covenant is breached:

  • Ind AS 1.74 (Indian carve-out): where a breach of a material provision of a long-term loan arrangement occurs on or before the end of the reporting period such that the liability becomes payable on demand, the entity does not classify the liability as current if the lender agreed, after the reporting period and before the approval of the financial statements for issue, not to demand payment as a consequence of the breach.
  • IAS 1.74 (IFRS): the liability is current unless the waiver was obtained by the reporting date. A post-year-end waiver is a non-adjusting event under IFRS.
  • Grace period (Ind AS 1.75): if by the end of the reporting period the lender has agreed to a grace period ending at least twelve months after the reporting date, within which the entity can rectify the breach and the lender cannot demand immediate repayment, the liability is non-current.

The carve-out reflects Indian practice where bank waivers are processed slowly through credit committees and often arrive after 31 March. It is explained as a carve-out in the "Comparison with IAS 1" appendix to Ind AS 1. The IASB's 2020 and 2022 amendments to IAS 1 (classification and non-current liabilities with covenants) change the IFRS position further — the Indian status of these amendments depends on MCA notification and must be checked at the reporting date (see topic related items).

Practical audit and reporting checklist on breach

  1. Identify every financial covenant in sanction letters and debenture trust deeds; recompute each on the covenant definition (s04 frozen/current GAAP).
  2. Determine whether the breach is "material" and whether it makes the loan payable on demand (some agreements only increase the interest rate — a penal rate — without an acceleration right).
  3. Obtain the waiver letter and its date relative to the reporting date and the board approval date.
  4. Check cross-default clauses: a breach on one facility can make others payable on demand.
  5. Assess going concern and disclosures: Ind AS 1.76 events (refinancing, rectification, waiver after year-end) are disclosed as non-adjusting events where relevant; Ind AS 107 liquidity risk disclosures; Schedule III additional regulatory information on wilful defaulter status and on borrowings secured against current assets where quarterly returns to banks differ from books.
  6. For listed debt: SEBI LODR requires disclosure of defaults on loans and debt securities; the Reg 52 financial results for debt-listed entities include ratio disclosures (debt-equity, DSCR, interest service coverage, net worth).

Numerical illustration

At 31 March 2025 a company has a ₹400 crore term loan (₹80 crore due in FY2025-26). Covenant: DSCR ≥ 1.30x; actual 1.12x. Breach makes the loan repayable on demand. Board approves financial statements on 20 May 2025; the bank's waiver letter is dated 5 May 2025. Current assets ₹600 crore, other current liabilities ₹350 crore.

Scenario Current borrowings Current ratio
No breach 80 600 ÷ 430 = 1.40x
Breach, no waiver before approval 400 600 ÷ 750 = 0.80x
Breach, waiver 5 May (Ind AS carve-out) 80 1.40x
Same facts under IFRS 400 0.80x

Rating agency methodology in India

SEBI-registered credit rating agencies — CRISIL (S&P Global), ICRA (Moody's), CARE Ratings and India Ratings & Research (Fitch) — plus Acuité, Brickwork (registration cancelled in 2022) and Infomerics, follow broadly similar frameworks:

  • Business risk: industry risk, market position, operating efficiency, diversification, management and governance.
  • Financial risk: capital structure, coverage, cash flows and financial flexibility.
  • Liquidity: assessed and disclosed as Superior/Strong, Adequate, Stretched or Poor, based on cash accruals vs debt maturities, cash and unutilised limits, and working-capital utilisation.
  • Parent/group support and notching: consolidated or "moderately consolidated" approach for group entities, uplift for strategic subsidiaries, notching down for structural subordination.

Key financial ratios commonly used (names vary by agency):

Ratio Typical definition Comment
Gearing Total debt ÷ Tangible net worth Agencies adjust for leases, guarantees, acceptances
TOL/TNW Total outside liabilities ÷ TNW Especially for mid-sized and trading borrowers
Total debt/OPBDITA Debt ÷ Operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax The agency term for debt/EBITDA
Interest coverage OPBDITA ÷ Interest and finance charges Usually EBITDA-based
DSCR (Net cash accruals + interest) ÷ (Interest + scheduled repayment) Forward-looking projections
NCA/Total debt Net cash accruals ÷ Total debt Proxy for years to repay; NCA ≈ PAT + D&A − dividends (variants)
RoCE PBIT ÷ Average capital employed Profitability input

Agencies adjust reported numbers: operating lease treatment, inclusion of acceptances (letters of credit for raw material imports — a large item for Indian manufacturers), supplier finance, corporate guarantees, perpetual bonds and promoter loans. Default recognition is strict: for rated listed debt, a delay of even one day in servicing is a default under SEBI's circulars on rating agencies. Agencies publish rating rationales on their websites — the best free source for an analyst's peer ratio benchmarks.

Rating scale: long-term AAA to D (investment grade BBB− and above); short-term A1+ to A4 and D; outlook Stable, Positive, Negative; Rating Watch with developing/positive/negative implications.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Waiver received after approval of financial statements: the carve-out no longer applies — the loan stays current even though the waiver arrives a day later.
  • Conditional waivers (waiver subject to equity infusion by a date) — assess whether the condition is within the entity's control.
  • Breach expected but not yet occurred at the reporting date: classification is unaffected, but disclose liquidity risk and consider going concern.
  • Penal interest only: if the agreement does not give an acceleration right, no reclassification is needed.
  • Quarterly testing: a breach at Q3 cured by Q4 — check whether the lender's acceleration right survived.
  • Agency vs reported ratios can diverge sharply because of acceptances and guarantees; never quote a rating threshold against your own unadjusted ratio.

Key takeaways

  • Under Ind AS 1.74, a lender waiver received after the reporting date but before approval of the financial statements avoids current classification — IFRS requires the waiver by the reporting date.
  • A grace period of at least twelve months agreed by the reporting date also preserves non-current classification.
  • Covenant breach analysis covers acceleration rights, cross-default, going concern and disclosure.
  • Indian CRAs assess business risk, financial risk and liquidity; key ratios are gearing, TOL/TNW, debt/OPBDITA, interest cover, DSCR and NCA/debt.
  • Agencies adjust for leases, acceptances, guarantees and promoter funding; read the rating rationale.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classification on breach — three dates [L2]

A company breaches its net debt/EBITDA covenant at 31 March 2025 on a ₹600 crore NCD (₹100 crore due within twelve months). Board approval of financial statements is on 15 May 2025. Determine the classification of the NCD under Ind AS and IFRS if the trustee's waiver is dated (a) 25 March 2025, (b) 2 May 2025, (c) 20 May 2025. Compute the current ratio in each case given current assets ₹900 crore and other current liabilities ₹500 crore.

Q2. Cross-default cascade [L2]

A group has three facilities: Bank A term loan ₹300 crore (covenant breached), Bank B working-capital loan ₹200 crore (cross-default clause referencing "any financial indebtedness above ₹50 crore"), and NCDs ₹250 crore (cross-acceleration clause only). Explain the classification of each at year-end if only Bank A provides a waiver before approval.

💡 Hint: Distinguish cross-default from cross-acceleration.

Q3. Building agency ratios from a rating rationale [L3]

You are given a CRISIL rating rationale for a mid-sized auto component maker showing gearing 0.9x, interest coverage 4.5x and NCA/total debt 0.32x, while your own computations from the annual report give 0.6x, 6.1x and 0.45x. List the adjustments the agency is likely to have made (with Indian examples) and describe how you would reconcile your figures.

Q4. Covenant headroom forecast [L3]

Design a quarterly covenant-headroom dashboard for an FP&A team, covering DSCR, debt/EBITDA and TOL/TNW for the next four quarters. Specify input data, the formula for EBITDA headroom before breach, and the escalation triggers you would set for the treasury team and audit committee.

Q5. Interview — why the Indian carve-out exists [L1]

Explain in under two minutes why Ind AS 1 departs from IAS 1 on post-reporting-date waivers and the analytical implication when comparing an Indian borrower with an IFRS reporter.
