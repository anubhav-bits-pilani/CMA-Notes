Subtopic note

Current, Quick and Cash Ratios and Current Maturities of Debt

Scope: Short-term liquidity: current, quick and cash ratios; current maturities of long-term debt

Why it matters

Short-term liquidity ratios are the first screen a banker, rating analyst or auditor runs on a balance sheet. In statutory audit they feed the going-concern assessment under SA 570; in bank appraisal they drive the working-capital limit (the Tandon/Chore norms you studied for MPBF still shape how Indian banks read the current ratio); and Schedule III (Division II) now requires the current ratio to be disclosed with an explanation for any change above 25%. In FP&A and modelling, liquidity ratios are output checks on the balance sheet forecast: a model that shows a current ratio collapsing below 1 in year 3 is either signalling a refinancing need or carrying a debt-schedule error. The single most common error in practice is mishandling current maturities of long-term debt — a reclassification that can swing the current ratio by 0.3–0.5x in a year with a bullet repayment.

Core notes

Current ratio

\[ \text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \]

Current assets and liabilities are as classified under Ind AS 1.66 and Ind AS 1.69: an item is current if it is expected to be realised/settled within the normal operating cycle, held primarily for trading, due within twelve months of the reporting date, or (for liabilities) the entity does not have a right at the reporting date to defer settlement for at least twelve months. Schedule III Division II presents current assets as inventories, current financial assets (investments, trade receivables, cash and cash equivalents, other bank balances, loans, others), current tax assets and other current assets; current liabilities as current financial liabilities (borrowings, lease liabilities, trade payables split MSME/others, other financial liabilities), other current liabilities, provisions and current tax liabilities.

Interpretation: the current ratio measures coverage of short-term obligations by short-term assets. A ratio of 1.33 is the classic Tandon Method II floor (25% of current assets financed by long-term funds). A high current ratio is not automatically good — it may reflect idle cash, slow inventory or stuck receivables. A low ratio is not automatically bad — FMCG and quick-service retail companies run negative working capital (customers pay before suppliers are paid) and carry current ratios below 1 by design.

Quick (acid-test) ratio

\[ \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventories} - \text{Prepaid expenses}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \]

Inventory is removed because it is the least liquid current asset — it must first be sold and then collected. Prepaid expenses (and, by extension, most "other current assets" such as advances to suppliers and GST input credit balances) cannot be converted to cash to pay creditors. A stricter variant also removes advances to suppliers and balances with government authorities. State your variant in the ratio dictionary and apply it consistently across peers.

Cash ratio

\[ \text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Cash equivalents} + \text{Liquid investments}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \]

Cash equivalents follow Ind AS 7.6–7.7: short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value — usually maturity of three months or less from acquisition. "Liquid investments" typically adds current investments in liquid/overnight debt mutual funds (measured at FVTPL) and bank deposits shown under "Other bank balances" with residual maturity under twelve months. Exclude earmarked balances: unpaid dividend accounts, margin money deposits against guarantees, and deposits under lien. The cash ratio is the stress-test ratio — it assumes nothing in receivables or inventory can be realised.

Current maturities of long-term debt (CMLTD)

Schedule III requires the portion of long-term borrowings repayable within twelve months of the reporting date to be shown under current borrowings (Division II after the 2021 amendment shows "current maturities of long-term borrowings" as a separate line within current borrowings). This has three consequences:

  1. Mechanical drop in the current ratio in the year before a large repayment, even though nothing about the business has changed.
  2. Bank appraisal practice differs: Indian banks computing MPBF and the current ratio for working-capital assessment often exclude CMLTD from current liabilities (treating term-loan instalments as met from cash accruals), or show the ratio both ways. Your ratio dictionary must state which convention you follow.
  3. Refinancing signal: a rising CMLTD with no matching rise in cash or committed facilities is a refinancing risk flag — check the maturity analysis under Ind AS 107.39 and the liquidity risk note.

Worked illustration

A paint distributor's balances at 31 March 2025 (₹ crore): inventories 180, trade receivables 140, cash 25, liquid mutual funds 35, prepaid and other current assets 20 — current assets 400. Current liabilities: trade payables 160, working-capital borrowings 60, CMLTD 50, other current liabilities 30 — total 300.

Ratio Computation Result
Current ratio (reported) 400 ÷ 300 1.33x
Current ratio (bank, ex-CMLTD) 400 ÷ 250 1.60x
Quick ratio (400 − 180 − 20) ÷ 300 0.67x
Cash ratio (25 + 35) ÷ 300 0.20x

The same balance sheet passes or fails a 1.33x covenant depending purely on the CMLTD convention — which is why covenant definitions must be read literally.

Excel implementation

Current_Ratio   = SUM(CA_Range) / SUM(CL_Range)
Quick_Ratio     = (SUM(CA_Range) - Inventories - Prepaids) / SUM(CL_Range)
Cash_Ratio      = (Cash + Cash_Equiv + Liquid_MF + OBB_Unencumbered) / SUM(CL_Range)
CR_ex_CMLTD     = SUM(CA_Range) / (SUM(CL_Range) - CMLTD)

Keep CMLTD on its own row in the balance-sheet mapping so both conventions can be computed without re-mapping.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Operating cycle longer than twelve months (real estate, shipbuilding, distilleries with ageing stock): Ind AS 1.68 allows inventory and receivables realised over the operating cycle to be current even beyond twelve months. A real-estate developer's current ratio of 2.5x can hide five years of unsold inventory — use the quick ratio and inventory ageing instead.
  • Customer advances and contract liabilities inflate current liabilities for jewellers, real-estate and capital-goods companies; these are settled by delivering goods, not cash, so a "low" current ratio may overstate stress. Show an adjusted ratio excluding contract liabilities alongside the reported one.
  • Lease liabilities: the current portion of lease liabilities (Ind AS 116) sits in current financial liabilities and depresses the current ratio for retailers; the ROU asset is non-current. Peer comparison with pre-2019 data or US-GAAP filers needs care.
  • Breach-driven reclassification: a covenant breach can push the entire long-term loan into current liabilities (see s05), collapsing the ratio overnight.
  • Window dressing: year-end paydown of working-capital lines funded by a short-term inter-corporate deposit received after year-end, or delayed supplier payments, can flatter a single reporting-date ratio. Compare with the half-yearly balance sheet under SEBI LODR Reg 33.
  • Disputed receivables and slow inventory: a ratio above 1 is not "safe" if a large part of current assets is non-realisable (see topic pitfalls); read ECL and inventory provisioning notes.

Key takeaways

  • Current, quick and cash ratios form a liquidity ladder from broad coverage to a pure cash stress test.
  • Quick ratio removes inventories and prepaids; define whether advances and GST balances are also excluded.
  • Cash ratio uses Ind AS 7 cash equivalents plus unencumbered liquid investments only.
  • CMLTD sits in current borrowings under Schedule III; banks often exclude it — state your convention.
  • Negative-working-capital business models legitimately run current ratios below 1.
  • Always pair liquidity ratios with ageing and maturity analysis before concluding on safety.

Practice Questions

Q1. Ratio ladder for a cement company [L1]

UltraTech-style cement company, 31 March 2025 (₹ crore): inventories 2,400; trade receivables 1,100; cash and cash equivalents 600; current investments in liquid funds 1,900; fixed deposits with residual maturity of eight months 300 (of which 120 under lien for bank guarantees); prepaid expenses 150; other current assets (GST input credit, supplier advances) 850. Current liabilities: trade payables 3,000; short-term borrowings 900; CMLTD 1,200; current lease liabilities 100; other current liabilities 1,800. Compute the current, quick and cash ratios, stating the treatment of each borderline item.

Q2. CMLTD and a working-capital covenant [L2]

A company's sanction letter requires a current ratio of at least 1.25x "computed as per the bank's assessment norms". At 31 March 2025, current assets are ₹520 crore and current liabilities ₹450 crore, including ₹110 crore of term-loan instalments due in FY2025-26. A ₹150 crore NCD matures in May 2026. Compute the reported and bank-basis current ratios and explain how the ratio will move at 31 March 2026 if nothing else changes.

💡 Hint: Consider when the May 2026 NCD becomes a current maturity.

Q3. Negative working capital business [L2]

A quick-service restaurant chain reports a current ratio of 0.62x and a quick ratio of 0.48x, against 1.8x and 1.1x for a paint manufacturer. Explain, with reference to the cash conversion cycle and the nature of current liabilities, why the restaurant chain may be the lower liquidity risk, and list three data points you would check to confirm.

Q4. Excel liquidity block [L3]

Design an Excel ratio block for five peers that computes the current ratio (reported and ex-CMLTD), quick ratio (two variants) and cash ratio, pulling from a mapped Schedule III balance sheet. Specify the mapping rows needed, the formulas, and two integrity checks that flag mapping errors.

Q5. Interview — "Is a current ratio of 2 good?" [L1]

An interviewer asks whether a current ratio of 2.0x for a real-estate developer is a sign of strong liquidity. Structure a two-minute answer covering operating cycle classification, inventory composition and customer advances.
