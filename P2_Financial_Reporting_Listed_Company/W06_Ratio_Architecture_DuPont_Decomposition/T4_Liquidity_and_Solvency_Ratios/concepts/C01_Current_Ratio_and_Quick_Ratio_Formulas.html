Key concept

Current Ratio and Quick Ratio Formulas

Concept: Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities; Quick = (CA − Inventories − Prepaids) ÷ CL

Statement

\[ \text{Current ratio} = \frac{CA}{CL} \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{CA - INV - PRE}{CL} \]

where \(CA\) = total current assets as classified under Ind AS 1.66, \(CL\) = total current liabilities under Ind AS 1.69 (including current maturities of long-term borrowings and current lease liabilities), \(INV\) = inventories (raw materials, WIP, finished goods, stock-in-trade, stores and spares, goods in transit), and \(PRE\) = prepaid expenses. All balances are taken at the reporting date (closing), because liquidity is a point-in-time question.

Explanation

The current ratio asks whether assets that will turn into cash within the operating cycle cover obligations falling due in the same window. The quick ratio strips out assets that need a further conversion step: inventory must be sold (and then collected), and prepaid expenses will never convert to cash — they are consumed as services. Conceptually the quick ratio is a "liquidation without selling stock" test.

Conditions and variants to document in a ratio dictionary:

  • Strict quick ratio: also deduct advances to suppliers, balances with GST/customs authorities, and other non-financial current assets — keeping only cash, current investments, trade receivables and other current financial assets.
  • Bank convention: Indian banks sometimes compute the current ratio excluding CMLTD from CL (term-loan instalments met from cash accruals); state this explicitly.
  • Operating cycle exception: Ind AS 1.68 lets inventory and receivables beyond twelve months remain current if within the normal operating cycle; long-cycle businesses (real estate, aged spirits) carry inflated current ratios.
  • Benchmarks such as 2:1 (current) and 1:1 (quick) are textbook rules of thumb; the Tandon Method II implies a minimum current ratio of 1.33. Negative working-capital models (FMCG, QSR, e-commerce marketplaces) legitimately sit below 1.

Neither ratio says anything about the quality of the assets: slow-moving inventory, disputed or old receivables and doubtful advances all inflate the numerator. Pair the ratios with inventory ageing, ECL provisioning and DSO.

Illustration

Kansai Nerolac-style paint company, 31 March 2025 (₹ crore): inventories 1,250 (including ₹90 crore slow-moving stock with no provision), trade receivables 1,050, cash and current investments 700, prepaid expenses 40, other current assets 160. Current liabilities 1,900.

  • Current ratio = (1,250 + 1,050 + 700 + 40 + 160) ÷ 1,900 = 3,200 ÷ 1,900 = 1.68x
  • Quick ratio = (3,200 − 1,250 − 40) ÷ 1,900 = 1,910 ÷ 1,900 = 1.01x
  • Strict quick ratio = (1,050 + 700) ÷ 1,900 = 0.92x

The gap between 1.68x and 0.92x shows how much of the comfort depends on inventory and non-financial assets; the unprovided slow-moving stock is a further quality flag.

Practice Questions

Q1. Strict versus standard quick ratio [L1]

A company reports (₹ crore): inventories 320, trade receivables 410, cash 60, current investments 90, prepaid expenses 25, GST input credit 70, advances to suppliers 45; current liabilities 700. Compute the current ratio, quick ratio and strict quick ratio.

Q2. Quality adjustment [L2]

In Q1, ₹55 crore of receivables are over 365 days old and disputed, and ₹40 crore of inventory has not moved in eighteen months. Recompute an adjusted quick ratio and current ratio, and explain what these adjustments say about the reported current ratio.

💡 Hint: Remove non-realisable amounts from the numerator only.

Q3. Interview — negative working capital [L1]

Explain why a current ratio of 0.7x for an FMCG major may signal strength rather than stress.
