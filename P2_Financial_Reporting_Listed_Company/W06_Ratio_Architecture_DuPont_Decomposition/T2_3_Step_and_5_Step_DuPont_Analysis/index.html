Roadmap topic: 3-Step & 5-Step DuPont Analysis: Decomposing ROE into Tax Burden, Interest Burden, Operating Margin, Asset Turnover, and Financial Leverage

Overview

DuPont analysis turns ROE from a single number into a driver tree. The 3-step version splits ROE into net margin, asset turnover and equity multiplier — profitability, efficiency and gearing. The 5-step version further splits net margin into tax burden, interest burden and EBIT margin, so that policy (tax), financing (interest) and operations (EBIT margin) are visible separately. The identity is exact: the product of the components equals ROE, provided every component uses the same PAT, revenue, average assets and average equity. That exactness is both the power of the method (nothing is left unexplained) and its main practical trap (mixing averaging conventions breaks it).

For the target roles, DuPont is the backbone of peer comparison and of the "why did ROE move?" conversation. Equity research initiations, IC memos and board packs present it; interviewers test whether you can explain how a company with lower margins earns a higher ROE (the Saturday case) and whether you know why an interest burden can exceed 1. In FP&A, the same tree structures the annual plan's return targets: a 2-percentage-point ROE goal can be translated into margin, working-capital and leverage targets owned by different functions.

Indian specifics make DuPont more delicate than textbook examples. Net-cash paint, FMCG and IT companies earn large other income, so the EBIT definition swings both EBIT margin and interest burden. Consolidated groups have NCI and equity-accounted JVs requiring explicit alignment. The s.115BAA regime, adopted by companies in different years and accompanied by deferred tax remeasurement and MAT credit write-offs, makes the tax burden jump for reasons unrelated to management performance. Ind AS 116 raised total assets and liabilities for lease-heavy sectors from FY2019-20.

The learning sequence: build the 3-step tree and its check (s01); extend to 5 steps and fix the EBIT convention (s02); handle other income for net-cash companies (s03); align NCI and associates in consolidated data (s04); attribute ROE changes across years with sequential substitution or logs (s05); and diagnose tax-burden movements (s06). The concept notes state each identity precisely.

Related concepts to study

Penman's operating vs financial leverage decomposition

Operating vs financial leverage decomposition (Penman): ROE = RNOA + FLEV × (RNOA − NBC)

Penman's reformulation separates the balance sheet into net operating assets (NOA = operating assets − operating liabilities) and net financial obligations (NFO = financial liabilities − financial assets), with common equity = NOA − NFO. Then ROE = RNOA + FLEV × (RNOA − NBC), where RNOA = operating income after tax ÷ NOA, FLEV = NFO ÷ equity, and NBC = net financial expense after tax ÷ NFO. Unlike the DuPont equity multiplier, operating liabilities (trade payables, customer advances) reduce NOA and raise RNOA rather than counting as leverage. For net-cash companies NFO is negative, FLEV is negative and ROE falls below RNOA when the after-tax return on cash is below RNOA — formalising the "cash drag" seen in s03.

Exercise context: the three paint companies

Exercise context: Asian Paints, Berger, Kansai Nerolac; margin pressure from Birla Opus and crude-linked inputs (TiO2, monomers)

The week's exercise compares Asian Paints, Berger Paints and Kansai Nerolac over three March year-ends. All three are consolidated groups with overseas or JV interests, hold treasury balances (Asian Paints most of all), and face two margin pressures: input costs linked to crude and global chemicals — titanium dioxide (TiO2), monomers such as vinyl acetate and acrylics, solvents — and intensified competition since Grasim's Birla Opus launched in 2024, raising dealer incentives and A&P. Kansai Nerolac's mix is weighted to industrial/automotive coatings, giving it lower decorative margins. Expect EBIT margin compression to dominate ROE changes in FY2024-25, with tax burden and leverage minor, and check annual reports for exceptional items (e.g., land sale gains) before attributing.

Ratio engine with checks and DAX

Ratio engine design with checks (product equals ROE) → Week 9; DAX measures → Week 12

In Week 9 you will build a ratio engine in Excel: a data tab of Schedule III line items per company and year, a dictionary tab defining each ratio's numerator, denominator, averaging rule and EBIT convention, and a calculation tab driven by named ranges. Common pitfalls

Interest burden above 1 left unexplained

Interest burden above 1 when EBIT excludes other income; define EBIT consistently and explain

The mistake: presenting an interest burden of 1.12 without comment, or "fixing" it by silently switching EBIT definitions for some companies. Why it happens: analysts expect burdens below 1 and treat a value above 1 as an error. Correct treatment: if EBIT excludes other income, PBT = EBIT − finance cost + other income, so a net-cash company's interest burden exceeds 1 — economically correct. Choose one EBIT convention for every peer and year, state it in the ratio dictionary, and add a footnote. Consider the treasury factor (s03) for cleaner comparisons. Detection: scan the interest burden row for values above 1 and check that other income exceeds finance cost for those company-years; check that the EBIT formula is identical across columns.

Mixed averaging breaks the product

Mixing average balances in some components and closing balances in others, so product does not equal ROE

The mistake: asset turnover on closing assets, equity multiplier on averages, ROE on closing equity — typical when components are pulled from different database fields. Why it happens: data vendors publish ratios on different bases; copying them is quicker than computing. Correct treatment: compute every component from raw line items using the same averaging rule (two-point or three-point averages) for assets and equity; the same average-asset name must appear in turnover and leverage. Detection: the check row (product − ROE) is non-zero; or the "DuPont ROE" differs from PAT ÷ average equity computed independently.

Crediting efficiency for a one-off tax gain

Attributing ROE gains to "efficiency" when driven by a one-off lower tax rate

The mistake: reporting "ROE up 350 bps on operational improvement" when the tax burden rose from 0.65 to 0.79 on s.115BAA migration, including a deferred tax credit. Why it happens: headline ROE and PAT growth are read without the tree; management commentary may encourage the interpretation. Correct treatment: separate tax burden in the 5-step tree; split it into recurring (statutory rate change) and one-off (DTL remeasurement, MAT credit write-off, prior-year reversals) using the Ind AS 12 rate reconciliation; attribute ROE change on a normalised tax burden. Detection: large tax burden change (above ~0.03) or ETR outside 22–28% for a s.115BAA company; deferred tax line with unusual sign.

Q3. Tax migration distorting the tree [L2]

(b) Attribute the change to recurring tax, one-off tax and other factors. (c) Write the paragraph you would place in the Saturday case answer to avoid the "efficiency" pitfall.
