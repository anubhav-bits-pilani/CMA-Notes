Subtopic note

Attributing ROE Change: Sequential Substitution and Log Method

Scope: Attributing ROE change across years: sequential substitution / log decomposition

Why it matters

"ROE fell from 28% to 23% — why?" A DuPont table shows that every component moved; the CFO wants to know how many of the 500 basis points came from margin, how many from turnover, how many from leverage and how many from tax. Because DuPont is multiplicative, the changes do not simply add up. Two methods are standard: sequential substitution (the chain-substitution technique familiar from CMA variance analysis) and logarithmic decomposition. A third, the Shapley (average-of-orders) method, removes order dependence. Knowing their properties and limitations is what separates a ratio table from a diagnostic narrative — and it is exactly what the Saturday case requires.

Core notes

The problem

For ROE \(= \prod_{i=1}^{k} x_i\), the change \(\Delta \text{ROE} = \text{ROE}_t - \text{ROE}_{t-1}\) cannot be written as a sum of independent component effects without an allocation rule for the interaction terms. With two factors, \(\Delta(ab) = b_0 \Delta a + a_0 \Delta b + \Delta a \Delta b\); the last term belongs to neither factor alone.

Method 1 — Sequential (chain) substitution

Replace one component at a time, in a stated order, from prior-year to current-year values, recording the change in ROE at each step:

\[ \text{Effect}_1 = (x_1^{t} - x_1^{t-1}) \, x_2^{t-1} x_3^{t-1} \cdots, \quad \text{Effect}_2 = x_1^{t} (x_2^{t} - x_2^{t-1}) \, x_3^{t-1} \cdots, \quad \ldots \]

Properties:

  • Effects add exactly to \(\Delta \text{ROE}\) (no residual).
  • Results depend on the order: interaction effects are assigned to whichever factor is substituted later. With large changes, the order can flip the ranking of drivers.
  • Convention (as in CMA variance analysis): substitute "quantity-like" or operating factors first and financing factors last, or follow the tree top-down (tax, interest, margin, turnover, leverage). State the order.

Method 2 — Log decomposition

Because the log of a product is the sum of logs:

\[ \ln\frac{\text{ROE}_t}{\text{ROE}_{t-1}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln\frac{x_i^{t}}{x_i^{t-1}} \]

Each component's share of the total (log) change is \(s_i = \ln(x_i^t / x_i^{t-1}) / \ln(\text{ROE}_t / \text{ROE}_{t-1})\). Allocate the arithmetic ΔROE in proportion:

\[ \text{Effect}_i = s_i \times \Delta \text{ROE} \]

Properties:

  • Order-independent and symmetric.
  • Effects add exactly to ΔROE.
  • Requires all components and both ROEs to be positive — fails with losses, negative equity or sign changes in interest burden.
  • When ROE is almost unchanged, the denominator \(\ln(\text{ROE}_t/\text{ROE}_{t-1})\) approaches zero and shares explode even though the component log-changes are meaningful; in that case present the log changes themselves (in % terms) rather than allocated basis points.

Method 3 — Shapley (average over orders)

Compute sequential substitution for every possible order (5! = 120 for 5 factors) and average each factor's effect. Order-independent, works with negative values, adds exactly. In Excel it is laborious; in Python or a DAX table with permutations it is straightforward. For two factors it reduces to splitting the interaction term equally.

Worked illustration (illustrative paint company)

Component FY2023-24 FY2024-25 ln ratio
Tax burden 0.745 0.748 0.00402
Interest burden (Convention B) 1.120 1.110 −0.00897
EBIT margin 20.0% 17.0% −0.16252
Asset turnover 1.30× 1.25× −0.03922
Financial leverage 1.45× 1.48× 0.02048
ROE 31.46% 26.11% −0.18621

ΔROE = −535 bps (26.11% − 31.46%, using unrounded products).

Log method allocation:

Component Share \(s_i\) Effect (bps)
Tax burden −0.0216 +12
Interest burden 0.0482 −26
EBIT margin 0.8728 −467
Asset turnover 0.2106 −113
Financial leverage −0.1100 +59
Total 1.0000 −535

Sequential substitution (order: tax → interest → margin → turnover → leverage) gives approximately: tax +13, interest −28, margin −470, turnover −102, leverage +53 (verify in Q1). The ranking is the same here, but the turnover and leverage effects differ by 6–11 bps because of order and interaction — material when the narrative hinges on them.

Narrative from the numbers

"ROE declined about 535 bps, of which about 465 bps reflects EBIT margin compression (input cost inflation and higher dealer incentives as competition intensified), and about 110 bps lower asset turnover (capacity added ahead of demand). Higher leverage, mainly from trade payables, offset about 60 bps. Tax and treasury effects were minor."

Excel implementation

Log share for one component, with components in rows and years in columns:

=LN(TaxBurden_FY25/TaxBurden_FY24)/LN(ROE_FY25/ROE_FY24)*(ROE_FY25-ROE_FY24)

Sequential substitution in a five-row "ladder": row 0 = prior-year product; row k = product with the first k components at current-year values; effect k = row k − row (k − 1). Add a check that the sum of effects equals ΔROE.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Negative or zero values (loss years, negative equity): use sequential or Shapley; never logs.
  • Large changes: the gap between methods widens; present the method and, ideally, the Shapley result.
  • Definition changes between years (e.g., Ind AS 116 adoption, a change in EBIT convention): restate the prior year first, otherwise attribution captures the definition change.
  • Multi-year attribution: chain year-on-year log effects (they add across years) for a three-year bridge; sequential effects do not chain cleanly.
  • Tax one-offs: isolate them before attribution (s06) or the tax burden effect will dominate in the migration year.
  • Rounding: compute on unrounded components; rounded tables will not sum.

Key takeaways

  • Multiplicative DuPont changes need an allocation rule for interaction terms.
  • Sequential substitution adds exactly but depends on order; state the order.
  • Log decomposition is order-independent and adds exactly, but needs positive values and breaks down when ΔROE is near zero.
  • Shapley averaging is the robust general solution.
  • Always restate definitions first and strip one-offs before attributing.

Practice Questions

Q1. Verify the sequential effects [L2]

Using the illustration's components, perform sequential substitution in the order tax → interest → EBIT margin → turnover → leverage, and then in the reverse order. Tabulate both and comment on which effects are most order-sensitive.

💡 Hint: Build a ladder of six products, starting from all prior-year values and switching one factor per row.

Q2. Log attribution for a 3-step tree [L1]

FY2023-24: net margin 9.5%, asset turnover 1.40×, equity multiplier 1.90×. FY2024-25: net margin 10.4%, asset turnover 1.32×, equity multiplier 1.75×. Compute ROE for both years and allocate the change using the log method.

Q3. Two-factor Shapley by hand [L2]

ROE = ROA × leverage. ROA moved from 12% to 15% and leverage from 2.0× to 1.6×. Compute the change in ROE and allocate it by sequential substitution in both orders and by Shapley averaging. Explain why the Shapley result is the fairest.

Q4. When logs fail [L3]

A chemicals company had ROE of 2% in FY2023-24 (interest burden 0.15) and 18% in FY2024-25 (interest burden 0.80) after a debt-funded plant ramped up. A colleague's log attribution shows interest burden "explaining" over 75% of the change. Explain why the result, while arithmetically correct, may mislead, and propose an alternative presentation for the board.
