Subtopic note

3-Step DuPont: Net Margin, Asset Turnover, Equity Multiplier

Scope: 3-step decomposition: net margin, asset turnover, equity multiplier

Why it matters

ROE is the headline return for shareholders, but a single number cannot tell you how it was earned. The 3-step DuPont identity — devised at DuPont in the 1910s–1920s and still the opening slide of most equity research initiations — splits ROE into profitability (net margin), efficiency (asset turnover) and financing (equity multiplier). Two companies with identical 20% ROE can be a high-margin, low-turnover branded business with no debt and a thin-margin, high-turnover distributor with significant leverage; their risk profiles, sensitivity to price competition and valuation multiples are completely different. For an FP&A analyst the 3-step tree is the simplest way to explain to a CFO why ROE moved, and for a credit analyst it shows immediately whether ROE depends on leverage.

Core notes

The identity

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Revenue}} \times \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\overline{\text{TA}}} \times \frac{\overline{\text{TA}}}{\overline{E}} \]

where \(\overline{\text{TA}}\) is average total assets and \(\overline{E}\) is average equity. Revenue and average total assets cancel, leaving PAT ÷ average equity. The product equals ROE exactly only if:

  1. the same PAT appears in net margin and in the ROE being reconciled;
  2. the same revenue appears in net margin and asset turnover;
  3. the same average total assets appear in asset turnover and in the equity multiplier;
  4. the same average equity appears in the multiplier and in ROE.

Violating any of these produces a "DuPont" whose product differs from ROE — a common error when databases mix closing and average balances.

The three components

Component Formula Drivers Typical Indian ranges
Net margin PAT ÷ Revenue from operations Pricing power, input costs, operating costs, depreciation, interest, tax, other income Paints 10–15%; FMCG 12–20%; retail 2–6%; cement 6–12%
Asset turnover Revenue ÷ Average total assets Capital intensity, working capital, cash holdings Retail/distribution 2–4×; paints 1.2–1.6×; cement 0.6–0.9×
Equity multiplier Average total assets ÷ Average equity Debt, lease liabilities, trade payables and other liabilities Net-cash FMCG 1.4–1.8×; leveraged infra 3–5×; banks 9–12×

Ranges are indicative only; derive the actual figures for the peers you analyse.

Equity multiplier is not just debt. Because \(\text{TA} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}\), the multiplier equals \(1 + \text{Total liabilities}/\text{Equity}\) and includes trade payables, provisions, contract liabilities (customer advances) and deferred tax. An FMCG company with no debt but large trade payables shows a multiplier of 1.6–2.0×. That "operating leverage" from suppliers is cheap or free financing — and is why the Penman-style decomposition (topic related item) separates operating from financial liabilities.

Worked illustration (illustrative, ₹ crore, FY2024-25)

Item Paint Co. Distributor Co.
Revenue from operations 10,000 30,000
PAT 1,340 600
Total assets: 31 Mar 2024 / 2025 7,400 / 8,600 9,000 / 11,000
Equity: 31 Mar 2024 / 2025 5,000 / 5,800 2,200 / 2,800
Component Paint Co. Distributor Co.
Net margin 13.4% 2.0%
Asset turnover (avg TA 8,000 / 10,000) 1.25× 3.00×
ROA = margin × turnover 16.75% 6.0%
Equity multiplier (avg E 5,400 / 2,500) 1.481× 4.000×
ROE (product) 24.8% 24.0%
Check: PAT ÷ avg equity 24.8% 24.0%

Similar ROE, opposite architecture: the paint company earns it through margin; the distributor through turnover and leverage. A 1 percentage point fall in net margin costs the distributor half its ROE but costs the paint company less than a tenth.

ROA as the bridge

Margin × turnover = PAT ÷ average total assets = ROA (PAT basis). So:

\[ \text{ROE} = \text{ROA} \times \text{Equity multiplier} \]

This shows that leverage multiplies ROA into ROE. But because PAT is already after interest, the 3-step equity multiplier does not tell you whether leverage helps: borrowing at 9% to fund assets earning 7% pre-tax lowers PAT and ROA while raising the multiplier. The 5-step version (s02) isolates the interest burden to answer that.

Building it in Excel

Lay out components in rows, years in columns, with a check row:

=NetMargin*AssetTurnover*EquityMultiplier-ROE_Reported

The check must return zero (or within a rounding tolerance such as 0.0001). Use the same named range AvgTotalAssets in both turnover and multiplier formulas so the cancellation is guaranteed by construction.

Interpreting changes

Read the tree top-down:

  1. Did ROE move? By how much?
  2. Which component moved most? (Use the attribution methods in s05 to quantify.)
  3. Is the movement operating (margin, turnover) or financial (multiplier)?
  4. Is it sustainable? Margin gains from a one-off tax benefit or turnover gains from year-end receivable factoring are not.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Cash-rich balance sheets depress asset turnover (cash is an asset with no revenue) and raise net margin (other income in PAT). The 3-step tree cannot separate these; use the 5-step tree and an operating-asset version.
  • Ind AS 116 raised total assets (ROU) and liabilities (lease liabilities) from FY2019-20, lowering turnover and raising the multiplier for retailers and QSRs; restate prior years or flag the break.
  • Negative equity makes the multiplier negative and the decomposition meaningless.
  • Revenue definition: use revenue from operations, net of GST; using total income breaks comparability with margin ratios elsewhere.
  • Consolidated accounts with NCI: use owners' PAT and owners' equity, with consolidated total assets; the NCI portion then sits within the multiplier (s04).
  • Quarterly analysis: annualise PAT and revenue consistently or turnover will be off by a factor of four.

Key takeaways

  • ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier; revenue and assets cancel.
  • The product equals ROE only if the same averages are used throughout — build a check row.
  • Equity multiplier = 1 + total liabilities ÷ equity, so it includes operating liabilities, not just debt.
  • Similar ROEs can come from very different architectures with very different risk.
  • The 3-step tree cannot show whether leverage is value-accretive; move to the 5-step tree for that.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute and reconcile [L1]

A company reports revenue from operations ₹4,800 crore and PAT ₹432 crore for FY2024-25. Total assets were ₹3,600 crore on 31 March 2024 and ₹4,400 crore on 31 March 2025; equity was ₹2,000 crore and ₹2,400 crore. Compute the three DuPont components and show that their product equals ROE.

Q2. Broken product [L2]

A database reports for the same company: net margin 9.0%, asset turnover 1.09× (revenue ÷ closing total assets) and equity multiplier 1.82× (average total assets ÷ average equity). Compute the product, compare it with the true ROE from Q1, and explain exactly which convention breaks the identity.

💡 Hint: Write out the product symbolically and see which terms fail to cancel.

Q3. Architecture comparison [L2]

Company X: net margin 18%, asset turnover 0.9×, equity multiplier 1.3×. Company Y: net margin 4%, asset turnover 2.8×, equity multiplier 2.0×. Compute both ROEs. Then compute each company's ROE if net margin falls by 2 percentage points with other components unchanged, and discuss which business model is more exposed to a price war.

Q4. Interview: zero-debt but multiplier of 1.9 [L1]

"An FMCG company has no borrowings, yet its equity multiplier is 1.9×. Is that a sign of hidden debt?" Prepare a structured answer naming the balance-sheet items that drive the multiplier.
