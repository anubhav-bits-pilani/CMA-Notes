Subtopic note

Other Income, EBIT and Interest Burden for Net-Cash Companies

Scope: Treatment of other income in EBIT and its effect on the interest burden ratio for net-cash companies

Why it matters

Many of the most valuable Indian listed companies — paint majors, FMCG leaders, IT services, some pharma and auto companies — are net-cash: their cash, bank deposits and liquid mutual funds exceed their borrowings and lease liabilities. Their "other income" (interest, dividends, FVTPL gains on mutual funds, profit on sale of investments) can be 5–20% of PBT. Where that income is placed in the DuPont tree decides whether the interest burden reads 0.96 or 1.15, whether EBIT margin is 16% or 18.5%, and therefore which story the analyst tells. In the week's exercise, Asian Paints (large treasury), Berger and Kansai Nerolac (smaller but material treasuries) will produce misleading comparisons unless the treatment is identical. The Saturday case asks you to explain a higher-ROE, lower-margin company — and the placement of other income is often the hidden answer.

Core notes

What is in "other income"

Schedule III Division II presents other income separately from revenue from operations, with a note break-up. Typical components:

Component Nature Operating?
Interest on bank deposits, bonds, ICDs Treasury No
Dividend income on investments Treasury / strategic No
Net gain on FVTPL mutual funds (fair value changes, Ind AS 109) Treasury No
Profit on sale of investments Treasury No
Profit on sale of PPE Non-recurring No
Interest on income-tax refunds Non-recurring No
Rental income from investment property Non-operating No (unless core)
Unwinding of discount on security deposits Accounting Neutral
Exchange gains on operating items (some companies) Operating Possibly yes
Scrap sales, insurance claims, liabilities written back Mixed Judgement

The first step is to split other income into treasury income, other non-operating income and operating items misclassified (e.g., export incentives, forex gains on trade receivables). Week 5 (w5_t2) covered this normalisation.

Three conventions for the 5-step tree

Convention A — EBIT includes other income ("reported EBIT").

\[ \text{EBIT}_A = \text{PBT} + \text{Finance cost}, \qquad \text{Interest burden}_A = \frac{\text{EBIT}_A - \text{Finance cost}}{\text{EBIT}_A} \le 1 \]

Interest burden is always at or below 1, and close to 1 for net-cash companies. EBIT margin is inflated by treasury income, so a cash-rich company looks operationally more profitable than it is. Asset turnover is depressed by the cash in total assets. Margin and turnover distortions partially offset, so ROE is right but the components mislead.

Convention B — EBIT excludes other income ("operating EBIT").

\[ \text{EBIT}_B = \text{PBT} + \text{Finance cost} - \text{Other income}, \qquad \text{Interest burden}_B = \frac{\text{EBIT}_B - \text{Finance cost} + \text{Other income}}{\text{EBIT}_B} \]

If other income exceeds finance cost, interest burden exceeds 1. This is not an error: it says the financing side adds to profit because the company is a net lender. EBIT margin is a clean operating margin. Asset turnover is still depressed by cash.

Convention C — operating DuPont with a treasury factor. Separate operating and treasury assets entirely:

\[ \text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{PBT}}}_{\text{TB}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{PBT}}{\text{EBIT}_B}}_{\text{IB}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBIT}_B}{\text{Rev}}}_{\text{Op. margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Rev}}{\overline{\text{OA}}}}_{\text{Op. asset turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\overline{\text{OA}}}{\overline{\text{TA}}}}_{\text{Operating asset share}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\overline{\text{TA}}}{\overline{E}}}_{\text{Leverage}} \]

where \(\overline{\text{OA}}\) = average total assets − average surplus cash and treasury investments. The "operating asset share" (< 1) captures the dilution from holding cash. The product still equals ROE. This six-factor tree is the cleanest for comparing a cash-rich incumbent with a leaner challenger.

Component Cash-rich (B) Lean (B)

Operating EBIT margin 16.0% 15.0%
Operating asset turnover (OA 5,500 / 5,800) 1.818× 1.724×

Leverage 1.481× 1.667×

The cash-rich company has the better operating engine (higher margin and higher operating turnover). The lean company's higher ROE comes from deploying almost all assets operationally and from more leverage. Under Convention A, the cash-rich company's EBIT margin would read 18.5% and turnover 1.25× — a "high margin, low turnover" story that is an artefact of cash.

Choosing and documenting

Goal Recommended convention
Replicate a broker's or company's published DuPont A (match their definition)
Peer comparison of operating quality B with an operating-asset adjustment, or C
Board-level explanation of why ROE is "low" despite great operations C (shows the cash drag explicitly)
Saturday case and exercise B or C, stated in the ratio dictionary

Whatever is chosen, write a definitional footnote: "EBIT excludes other income of ₹X crore; interest burden above 1 reflects net treasury income in excess of finance costs."

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mixed other income: if half of other income is a one-off profit on sale of land, place it in exceptional items for a normalised tree rather than in the interest burden.
  • FVTPL volatility: mutual fund mark-to-market gains swing with bond yields; in a year of falling rates, other income spikes and interest burden rises without any change in operations.
  • Lease interest: post-Ind AS 116 finance cost includes lease interest; a net-cash company can still have interest burden below 1 under Convention B if it is lease-heavy. Decide whether leases are debt.
  • Operating cash: do not classify all cash as surplus; retain an operating cash allowance (e.g., 1–2% of revenue) within operating assets.
  • Forex gains: operating forex on receivables arguably belongs in EBIT; financing forex on borrowings belongs with finance cost (Ind AS 23.6(e) treats some exchange differences as borrowing cost).
  • Sign of interest burden in loss years: if EBIT is negative while PBT is positive because of treasury income, the ratio becomes negative; describe it in words.

Key takeaways

  • Other income for net-cash companies is mostly treasury income and must be placed deliberately in the tree.
  • Including it in EBIT keeps interest burden below 1 but inflates EBIT margin; excluding it gives a clean margin and an interest burden that can exceed 1.
  • An interest burden above 1 is economically meaningful: the company is a net lender.
  • The cleanest comparison adds an operating-asset share factor to isolate the drag of idle cash on turnover.
  • Document the convention and apply it identically across peers and years.

Practice Questions

Q1. Three conventions [L2]

A company reports (₹ crore): revenue 22,000; PBT 4,150; finance cost 180; other income 650 (treasury 560, profit on sale of PPE 90); tax 1,040. Average total assets 18,500; average surplus cash and liquid investments 6,200; average equity 13,100. Build the DuPont tree under Conventions A, B and C (treat the PPE gain as non-operating and include it with treasury income for this purpose), and show that each reproduces ROE.

Q2. Explaining an interest burden of 1.14 [L1]

Draft a two-sentence footnote for an IC memo explaining why a paint company's interest burden is 1.14 and why it is not a calculation error.

Q3. Bond-rally distortion [L2]

In FY2024-25, a company's FVTPL gains on debt mutual funds rose from ₹80 crore to ₹210 crore as yields fell, with operating EBIT and finance cost unchanged at ₹1,200 crore and ₹40 crore. Show the effect on interest burden (Convention B) and on EBIT margin (Convention A) for revenue of ₹8,000 crore, and explain how you would present the year-on-year change to avoid crediting management for the bond rally.

💡 Hint: Compute both years under each convention and isolate the change in the treasury component.

Q4. Case: incumbent vs lean challenger [L3]

Using any two listed Indian peers in the same sector where one is materially net-cash (e.g., a paint major and a smaller paint company), describe the data you would extract from their annual reports to build Convention C, the judgement calls you expect (operating cash, investments in subsidiaries, deposits over 12 months), and the conclusion you would test.
