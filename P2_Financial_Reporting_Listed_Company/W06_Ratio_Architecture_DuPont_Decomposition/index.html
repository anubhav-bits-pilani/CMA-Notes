How this week fits

Week 6 sits in Phase 2 (Financial Reporting & Listed Company Analysis). Week 5 cleaned the raw material: horizontal and common-size analysis, normalised EBITDA, the reclassified balance sheet (operating, non-operating, financing buckets), invested capital, cash-flow forensics and segment data. This week turns those clean inputs into a ratio architecture — a defined, consistent set of margins, returns, DuPont components, working-capital days, liquidity, solvency, turnover and sector KPIs — and into diagnostic narratives: why ROE moved, why one company out-earns another, how much cash a change in working-capital days releases.

The six topics form one system. Profitability and return ratios (t1) provide the margins and ROE/ROCE/ROIC. DuPont (t2) decomposes ROE into tax, interest, operating margin, turnover and leverage. Working capital and CCC (t3) explain much of turnover and of the EBITDA-to-CFO gap. Liquidity and solvency (t4) test whether the leverage in DuPont is safe. Asset turnover and fixed-asset productivity (t5) explain the rest of turnover. Sector KPIs (t6) connect ratios to operating drivers such as volume, realisation per tonne, SSSG and utilisation.

Prerequisites: Week 5's normalised EBITDA, invested capital and net debt definitions; CMA Final ratio and working-capital knowledge; Ind AS 115 and 116 mechanics. What comes next: Week 7 uses these ratios in credit analysis (Altman Z, distress signals); Week 9 codifies them in an Excel ratio engine with checks; Week 12 rebuilds them as DAX measures; Week 14 uses days and turnover as drivers in the 3-statement model; Week 15 builds the WACC that ROIC is compared with.

Refresh before starting

CMA Final ratio analysis and working-capital management

CMA Final ratio analysis and working-capital management (operating cycle, MPBF)

  • Operating cycle = raw-material storage period + WIP period + finished-goods storage period + debtors collection period − creditors payment period (net operating cycle). Each stage is a balance divided by its own flow: RM by materials consumed, WIP by cost of production, FG by cost of sales, debtors by credit sales, creditors by credit purchases. This is the cost-sheet version of the CCC you will compute from published accounts.
  • Working-capital financing: Tandon Committee methods — Method I (25% of working-capital gap from long-term funds), Method II (25% of total current assets from long-term funds; minimum current ratio 1.33), Method III (core current assets fully long-term funded). Nayak Committee: 25% of projected turnover for smaller borrowers (5% margin, 20% bank finance). Drawing power from stock and book-debt statements.
  • Ratio families: liquidity, solvency, turnover, profitability; the classic DuPont chart from CMA Strategic Financial Management/Financial Analysis papers.
  • What changes now: published Ind AS accounts rarely give cost of production or credit purchases, so you must approximate with Schedule III lines and document the approximations.

Week 5 definitions

Week 5 normalized EBITDA, net debt and invested capital definitions

  • Normalised EBITDA: revenue from operations − operating costs, excluding other income, exceptional items, FVTPL gains, and one-off items; Ind AS 116 basis stated (post-116 or rent-adjusted).
  • Net debt: borrowings (current and non-current, including current maturities) + lease liabilities (if post-116 basis) + debt-like items (gold metal loans, unfunded gratuity, NCI puts, customer deposit schemes where applicable) − cash, cash equivalents, bank deposits and liquid investments (excluding earmarked balances).
  • Invested capital: operating NWC + net PPE + CWIP + ROU assets + operating intangibles (± goodwill), reconciled to equity + net debt − non-operating assets.
  • Use this week: EBIT for ROCE/ROIC and DuPont comes from normalised EBITDA − D&A; invested capital is the ROIC denominator; operating NWC is the NWC % numerator.

Averages and leverage arithmetic

Average-balance conventions and leverage arithmetic

  • Two-point average = (opening + closing) ÷ 2; three-point adds the 30 September Reg 33 balance sheet; time-weighted for QIPs, buybacks and acquisitions.
  • Rule: flow ÷ average stock for analysis; closing stock for forecast drivers; same convention in every component of a product identity.
  • Leverage identities: \(A = L + E\), so \(A/E = 1 + L/E\); debt/equity is a subset of \(L/E\); net debt/EBITDA uses EBITDA on the same lease basis as debt.
  • Growth bias: closing-based days and returns diverge from average-based by roughly half the growth rate — e.g., 20% balance growth biases closing-based days up by ~10%.
  • Percentage vs percentage-point: Schedule III's 25% variance test is relative (\(\Delta\text{ratio}/\text{ratio}_{t-1}\)), not in percentage points.

Ind AS 116 and Ind AS 115 contract balances

Ind AS 116 and Ind AS 115 contract balances impacts on ratios

  • Ind AS 116 (from FY2019-20): ROU assets and lease liabilities on balance sheet; rent replaced by ROU depreciation and lease interest. Effects: EBITDA and EBITDA margin up; EBIT up slightly; finance cost up; interest coverage down; total assets up (asset turnover down); liabilities up (equity multiplier and debt/equity up if leases counted); CFO up (principal in financing). Short-term and low-value leases stay in rent.
  • Ind AS 115 contract balances: receivable (unconditional right), contract asset (conditional right), contract liability (advance or billing in excess). Effects: DSO depends on whether unbilled and contract assets are included; contract liabilities reduce NWC and can make CCC negative; significant financing components move amounts between revenue and finance cost.
  • Practical rule: decide the lease basis and the contract-balance basis once, in the ratio dictionary, and apply to all peers and years.

Cross-cutting concepts

Ratio architecture and the dictionary

Ratio architecture: every ratio links to a defined numerator/denominator dictionary used consistently across peers

A ratio is only as good as its definition. Most ratio errors in Indian peer analysis — ROCE including cash for one company and excluding it for another, DPO on revenue, closing equity in a buyback year — are not arithmetic errors but definition drift. The remedy is a dictionary: a table where each ratio has an ID, name, formula in words, numerator line items (with Schedule III captions), denominator line items, averaging rule, Ind AS 116 basis, other-income and exceptional-item treatment, GST adjustment flag, unit (%, ×, days), "higher is better" flag, and source notes. Every calculation cell references the dictionary's named inputs rather than raw cells, so a definition change propagates everywhere and is visible.

The dictionary also documents architecture: which ratios are building blocks of others (EBIT margin × asset turnover = EBIT ÷ assets; DSO + DIO − DPO = CCC; tax burden × interest burden × EBIT margin = net margin). Product and sum identities become automated check rows. For Schedule III reporting, the same dictionary records the company's disclosed definitions and the 25% variance flags. In Week 9 this becomes the backbone of the Excel ratio engine; in Week 12 each dictionary entry becomes one DAX measure.

ROE driver tree as a diagnostic narrative

ROE drivers tree connects margin, turnover and leverage ratios into one diagnostic narrative

The ROE driver tree places the 5-step DuPont at the top and hangs every other ratio beneath its parent. Under EBIT margin sit gross (material) margin, employee cost %, other expense %, A&P %, freight % and D&A % — the margin stack. Under asset turnover sit fixed-asset turnover (with and without CWIP and ROU), working-capital turnover, DSO, DIO and DPO, and the cash drag (operating-asset share). Under interest burden sit interest coverage, other income yield and finance cost rate. Under financial leverage sit debt/equity, net debt/EBITDA and operating liabilities as % of assets. Under tax burden sit the statutory rate and the Ind AS 12 reconciliation items.

The value of the tree is narrative discipline: an explanation of ROE change must travel down the branches to a cause ("EBIT margin fell 300 bps because material margin fell 250 bps as TiO2 and monomer prices rose and dealer incentives increased against a new entrant; asset turnover fell 0.08× because a new plant's CWIP was capitalised and inventory days rose 6"). Log attribution quantifies each branch's contribution so the narrative is proportionate. The Saturday case is built around this tree.

Cash conversion and ROIC as joint tests of growth quality

Cash conversion and ROIC jointly test growth quality

Growth is high-quality when it earns returns above the cost of capital and converts into cash. ROIC tests the first: incremental ROIC on capital added (lagged, multi-year) must exceed WACC. Cash conversion tests the second: CFO ÷ EBITDA (or FCF ÷ NOPAT) and the CCC trend show whether profit is being turned into cash or absorbed by working capital. The two are linked — working capital is part of invested capital, so a lengthening CCC both lowers ROIC and lowers cash conversion.

Four combinations matter. High ROIC with high cash conversion is the compounding profile of paint and FMCG leaders. High ROIC with low cash conversion suggests aggressive revenue recognition or channel stuffing (rising DSO, rising unbilled). Low ROIC with high cash conversion is a mature, under-earning business returning cash. Low ROIC with low cash conversion — growth funded by receivables and inventory at below-WACC returns — is value-destroying growth. In the exercise, plot the three paint companies on ROIC vs CFO/EBITDA axes for each year, and annotate movements with CCC changes.

The exercise as an integrated build

Exercise: 5-step DuPont and CCC for Asian Paints, Berger, Kansai Nerolac across 3 FYs, all March year-end

The three companies are a natural laboratory. All are NSE-listed decorative and industrial paint companies with March year-ends, so balance-sheet dates and seasonal timing align — no calendarisation is needed. They differ in scale, mix and balance-sheet structure: Asian Paints is the cash-rich market leader with significant treasury income and international subsidiaries; Berger Paints is the second-largest decorative player with international operations; Kansai Nerolac has a larger industrial/automotive coatings mix and a Japanese parent. All face the same input-cost environment (TiO2, crude-linked monomers and solvents) and the same competitive shock (Birla Opus's entry in 2024 and other new entrants), so differences in ratios reflect strategy and execution rather than macro conditions.

The exercise integrates t1–t3: margins and returns, 5-step DuPont with a consistent EBIT convention and NCI alignment, log attribution of ROE change, and CCC with GST and MSME adjustments. It also forces judgement on other income (interest burden above 1), exceptional items (land sales, impairments) and three-point averaging using Reg 33 half-yearly balance sheets.

Indian-specific distortions

Indian-specific distortions: GST in receivables, other income, s.115BAA transition, MSME payables

Four Indian features distort ratios in predictable directions. GST: receivables and payables include GST while revenue and purchases exclude it, overstating DSO and DPO by roughly the effective GST rate (18% for paints); rate changes such as cement's move from 28% to 18% on 22 September 2025 create breaks. Other income: large treasury income at cash-rich companies inflates EBIT margin if included, or pushes interest burden above 1 if excluded; it also depresses asset turnover through idle cash. s.115BAA transition: companies opted for the 25.168% regime in different years, with one-off deferred tax remeasurements and MAT credit write-offs in the migration year, making tax burden jump for non-operating reasons. MSME payables: the MSMED Act 45-day limit, enforced through s.43B(h) from FY2023-24, shortened MSE payable days and lowered blended DPO for many buyers — a regulatory, not commercial, change.

Each distortion has a standard fix: GST-adjust days for comparison; choose and document the other-income convention and add a cash-drag factor; normalise tax burden using the Ind AS 12 reconciliation; decompose DPO into MSE and other suppliers.

Roadmap exercise: Build a 5-Step DuPont and Cash Conversion Cycle comparison across Asian Paints, Berger Paints, and Kansai Nerolac over 3 financial years.

  3.   8. WorkingCapital — DSO, DIO, DPO, CCC (reported); GST-adjusted; MSE/other DPO decomposition; factoring adjustments; NWC % revenue (trade and operating); cash released by component.

  10. Summary — peer table, charts (ROE tree waterfall, CCC stacked bars, ROIC vs CFO/EBITDA scatter), and key messages.

Required checks

  • Balance sheet total assets = total equity and liabilities, every date, every company.
  • PAT attributable to owners in Inputs ties to the reported figure and to EPS × weighted shares within rounding.
  • DuPont product − owners' ROE (owners' PAT ÷ average owners' equity) = 0 for every company-year, both conventions.
  • Tax burden × interest burden × EBIT margin = PAT ÷ revenue (with NCI and associate factors included).
  • Sum of attribution effects = ΔROE for every pair of years and for the two-year chain.
  • DSO + DIO − DPO = CCC; cash-released components sum to total.
  • Reclassified operating + treasury + financing buckets = total assets and total liabilities.
  • Flags: interest burden > 1 (requires footnote), tax burden outside 0.70–0.78 (requires explanation), any Schedule III ratio change > 25% (cross-check with company's explanation).
