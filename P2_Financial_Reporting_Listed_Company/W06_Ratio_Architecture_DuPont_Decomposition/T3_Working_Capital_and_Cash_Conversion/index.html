Roadmap topic: Working Capital & Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC): DSO (Receivable Days), DIO (Inventory Days), DPO (Payable Days), and Net Working Capital as % of Revenue

Overview

Working-capital analysis measures how much cash a business ties up in its operating cycle — buying inputs, holding inventory, selling on credit — and how much of that cycle its suppliers and customers finance. DSO, DIO and DPO translate each balance into days; the cash conversion cycle (CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO) combines them into the number of days the company funds itself; and NWC as a percentage of revenue converts the same information into the cash cost of growth. Together they explain a large part of the gap between EBITDA and operating cash flow, and a large part of invested capital in ROIC.

For the target roles, the topic is everywhere. In FP&A and treasury, days drive the cash forecast, revolver sizing and working-capital improvement programmes ("release ₹200 crore by cutting DSO five days"). In 3-statement modelling, the working-capital schedule is days- or %-of-revenue driven (Week 14). In credit and banking, the operating cycle underpins drawing power and MPBF. In equity research and forensic work, deteriorating days often precede earnings disappointments, and flattering devices (factoring, supplier finance, year-end window dressing) are classic red flags.

Indian specifics make naive computations wrong in predictable ways. Receivables and payables include GST while revenue and COGS exclude it. The nature-wise P&L gives only a material COGS. IT services carry large unbilled balances; real-estate developers and jewellers run on customer advances. The MSMED Act's 45-day limit and the s.43B(h) disallowance from FY2023-24 have reshaped payables. Channel finance, factoring, TReDS and supplier finance move days without changing terms. And seasonal businesses such as paints have very different balance sheets at 31 March and 30 September — which Reg 33 half-yearly statements of assets and liabilities let you see.

The learning sequence: define days on matched denominators (s01); correct for GST (s02); extend receivables and advances for IT, real estate and jewellery (s03); analyse MSME payables and s.43B(h) (s04); strip financing devices (s05); and aggregate into NWC % revenue while controlling for seasonality and window dressing (s06). The concept notes give the formulas and the conversion of days into cash.

Related concepts to study

Bank working-capital assessment

Bank working-capital assessment: drawing power, MPBF (Tandon Method II requires current ratio 1.33)

Indian banks size fund-based working-capital limits (cash credit, WCDL) from the operating cycle. The Tandon Committee (1975) defined the working-capital gap as current assets minus current liabilities other than bank borrowings. Under Method I, the borrower funds 25% of the gap from long-term sources; under Method II, 25% of total current assets — so MPBF = 75% of current assets − other current liabilities, implying a minimum current ratio of 1.33; Method III additionally excludes core current assets. The Nayak Committee turnover method (for smaller borrowers) sets working capital at 25% of projected turnover, of which 5% is margin and 20% bank finance. Drawing power is computed monthly from stock and receivable statements: eligible inventory and receivables (often receivables under 90 days) less margins less trade creditors. CCC changes therefore change bank limits directly.

Factoring and Ind AS 109 derecognition

Ind AS 109 derecognition for factoring with/without recourse

When receivables are factored, Ind AS 109 determines whether they leave the balance sheet. The entity first asks whether it has transferred the contractual rights to the cash flows (or entered a qualifying pass-through arrangement). If it has transferred substantially all risks and rewards — typically without-recourse factoring — it derecognises the receivables; DSO falls and the cash received is operating. If it retains substantially all risks and rewards — with-recourse factoring, or a first-loss guarantee covering expected losses — the receivables stay on the balance sheet and the proceeds are a financial liability (a borrowing), with cash classified as financing. In between, control decides, with continuing involvement accounting. Ind AS 107 requires disclosure of transferred assets. For analysis, add derecognised factored balances back to DSO and consider recourse exposure as debt.

Working-capital schedule and cash forecasting

Working-capital schedule drivers in the 3-statement model → Week 14; cash forecasting → Week 18

In Week 14 you will build the working-capital schedule of a 3-statement model: closing receivables = DSO × revenue ÷ 365, inventory = DIO × COGS ÷ 365, payables = DPO × purchases (or COGS) ÷ 365, plus other operating items as % of revenue; ΔNWC flows to CFO and the balance sheet balances through cash or revolver. Historical drivers must use the same balance basis (closing, GST-inclusive) as forecasts to avoid a first-year jump. In Week 18, cash forecasting moves to monthly or weekly granularity: collections by ageing bucket, MSE payment schedules within 45 days, GST cash flows, seasonal inventory build, and covenant headroom — the operational use of everything in this topic.

Common pitfalls

Wrong averaging for the situation

Using closing balances for growing companies, overstating days; or average balances for a single-year shock

The mistake: computing days on closing balances for a company growing 25% a year — the closing balance reflects year-end run-rate sales, so days are overstated against full-year revenue; or, conversely, using averages when a single event (a large customer default, a one-off inventory build in March) should be reflected fully. Why it happens: closing balances are easy; averages are the textbook default; neither is applied with thought. Correct treatment: use averages (ideally three-point with Reg 33 September data) for analysing behaviour across a year; use closing balances for forecast drivers and when analysing a specific year-end event — and say which. Detection: compare closing-based and average-based days; a gap above ~10% indicates growth or a year-end event worth explaining.

DPO on revenue

Computing DPO on revenue instead of COGS/purchases

The mistake: dividing trade payables by revenue. Why it happens: databases and some bank formats use sales for all ratios; it is convenient. Correct treatment: payables arise from purchases at cost; use purchases (COGS + Δinventory, plus credit-purchased services where material) or at least COGS. Revenue-based DPO understates days by the COGS/revenue ratio and makes CCC inconsistent across companies with different margins. Detection: check the formula; a CCC built from revenue-based DIO and DPO will differ from one built on COGS by an amount related to gross margin; compare with management-reported payable days.

Ignoring GST in receivables

Ignoring GST in receivables, overstating DSO by the GST rate

The mistake: computing DSO on GST-inclusive receivables over GST-exclusive revenue and comparing across sectors or periods. Why it happens: the balance sheet shows receivables as invoiced, and the mismatch is invisible. Correct treatment: for comparison, divide by \((1 + g)\) using the effective GST rate on domestic credit sales (lower for exporters; zero for exports); apply the same logic to payables with ITC. Keep unadjusted days only as model drivers on a consistent basis. Watch rate changes (cement from 28% to 18% on 22 September 2025). Detection: a paint company at 18% GST showing DSO about 18% above management's quoted collection period; sector comparisons where high-GST sectors appear to collect slower.

Q2. Cash impact for a CFO [L2]

A company with revenue ₹9,000 crore, COGS ₹5,600 crore and purchases ₹5,750 crore targets DSO −6 days, DIO −10 days and accepts DPO −4 days (MSE compliance). (a) Compute cash released by component and in total. (b) Compute the annual interest saving at 8.25% pre-tax. (c) Compute the effect on ROIC if NOPAT is ₹900 crore and average invested capital ₹6,000 crore. (d) Explain why the DPO change should not be reversed through supplier finance without disclosure.

💡 Hint: Price each component on its own denominator before summing; ROIC changes through the denominator only.

Q3. Seasonality vs window dressing [L3]

A listed paint company's trade NWC (₹ crore) at 31 March and 30 September: Mar-2022 1,300; Sep-2022 2,050; Mar-2023 1,350; Sep-2023 2,300; Mar-2024 1,250; Sep-2024 2,600; Mar-2025 1,200. Revenue grew 10% a year from ₹9,000 crore in FY2021-22. H1 CFO was negative and H2 CFO positive in each year; MSE payables fell 55% at Mar-2024. (a) Compute two-point and three-point NWC % revenue for FY2022-23 to FY2024-25. (b) Assess whether the widening March–September gap is seasonality, s.43B(h) behaviour, or window dressing. (c) Recommend the averaging convention for the week's exercise and justify it.
