Key concept

DSO: Average Trade Receivables to Revenue Days

Concept: DSO = Average trade receivables ÷ Revenue × 365

Statement

\[ \text{DSO} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\text{TR}_{t-1} + \text{TR}_t\right)}{R_t} \times 365 \]

where:

  • \(\text{TR}\) = trade receivables at the balance-sheet dates (state whether gross or net of expected credit loss allowance, and whether unbilled receivables are included).
  • \(R_t\) = revenue from operations for year \(t\) (GST-exclusive; credit sales if the cash-sale share is material and known).
  • 365 = days in the period (use the period's days for half-years and quarters).

Equivalent form: \(\text{DSO} = 365 \div \text{Receivables turnover}\), with receivables turnover = \(R / \overline{\text{TR}}\) — the Schedule III "trade receivables turnover ratio".

Explanation

DSO expresses receivables as days of sales outstanding — how long, on average, it takes to collect. It drives the receivables line in working-capital forecasts and is a key credit-quality indicator.

Adjustments that change the number materially in India:

  1. GST: receivables include GST; revenue excludes it. Divide by \((1 + g)\), with \(g\) the effective GST rate on domestic credit sales, for cross-sector or cross-period comparison (s02).
  2. Unbilled revenue (IT services): include unconditional unbilled receivables and, for a "total DSO", contract assets (s03).
  3. Factoring and channel finance: add back receivables sold without recourse and dealer finance where the company retains risk (s05).
  4. ECL allowance: gross DSO shows collection behaviour; net DSO ties to the balance sheet.

Average vs closing: averages match the year's revenue; closing balances are used for model drivers and when a single-year shock (e.g., a large customer default at year-end) should be captured (topic pitfall). For seasonal businesses, use a three-point average with the Reg 33 30 September balance sheet (s06).

Interpretation: compare with contractual credit terms (paint dealers typically 30–45 days; IT clients 45–90 days; government contracts much longer). Rising DSO with stable terms suggests weaker collections, disputes or channel stuffing; falling DSO may reflect factoring rather than better collection. Cross-check with the Schedule III ageing schedule (not due, < 6 months, 6 months–1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years, > 3 years; disputed and undisputed; considered good, significant increase in credit risk, credit impaired).

Illustration

Illustrative paint company (₹ crore): trade receivables ₹1,080 (31 March 2024) and ₹1,320 (31 March 2025); revenue FY2024-25 ₹10,950 crore; 95% domestic at 18% GST.

  • Reported DSO = 1,200 ÷ 10,950 × 365 = 40.0 days.
  • Effective GST = 18% × 0.95 = 17.1%; GST-adjusted DSO = 40.0 ÷ 1.171 = 34.2 days.
  • Closing-based DSO = 1,320 ÷ 10,950 × 365 = 44.0 days — 4 days higher, because receivables grew 22% while revenue grew less.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute DSO variants [L1]

Revenue ₹4,600 crore (80% domestic at 18% GST); gross trade receivables ₹700 crore opening and ₹860 crore closing; ECL allowance ₹40 crore and ₹70 crore. Compute DSO on gross and net average balances, and GST-adjusted gross DSO.

Q2. Turnover to days [L1]

A company's Schedule III note shows a trade receivables turnover ratio of 7.3 in FY2023-24 and 5.8 in FY2024-25, with an explanation citing "delays in collection from a large OEM customer". Convert to DSO, compute the change in days, and state what further disclosure you would read to assess credit risk.

💡 Hint: Days equal 365 divided by turnover; then look at the ageing schedule's buckets.

Q3. Growth distortion [L2]

A fast-growing company's revenue doubled from ₹1,000 crore to ₹2,000 crore; receivables went from ₹120 crore to ₹300 crore. Compute DSO on closing, average and opening balances and explain which is most representative of collection behaviour during the year.
