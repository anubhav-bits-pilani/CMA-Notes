Subtopic note

DSO, DIO and DPO: Definitions and the Right Denominators

Scope: DSO, DIO, DPO definitions and denominators (revenue vs COGS vs purchases)

Why it matters

Working-capital days convert balance-sheet stocks into a time dimension that operations, sales and procurement teams understand: "we collect in 48 days", "we hold 70 days of stock", "we pay suppliers in 60 days". They drive the working-capital schedule in every 3-statement model, the cash forecast in FP&A, the bank's drawing-power and MPBF assessment, and the cash conversion cycle (CCC) diagnostics in this week's exercise. The arithmetic is simple; the errors come from mismatched denominators — inventory divided by revenue, payables divided by sales, a "COGS" that omits half the costs — which make days non-comparable across companies and wrong as model drivers.

Core notes

Matching principle

Each balance should be divided by the flow that creates it, measured on the same price basis:

Metric Balance (numerator) Flow that creates it (denominator) Price basis
DSO (receivable days) Average trade receivables (billed + unbilled receivables) Revenue from operations (credit sales if known) Selling price
DIO (inventory days) Average inventories (RM, WIP, FG, stock-in-trade, stores) COGS Cost
DPO (payable days) Average trade payables (for goods and services) Purchases (preferred) or COGS Cost

\[ \text{DSO} = \frac{\overline{\text{TR}}}{R} \times 365, \quad \text{DIO} = \frac{\overline{\text{Inv}}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365, \quad \text{DPO} = \frac{\overline{\text{TP}}}{\text{Purchases}} \times 365 \]

Use 365 days for annual data (some banks and textbooks use 360; be consistent). For quarterly or half-yearly data, scale the flow: \(\text{DSO}_{\text{H1}} = \text{TR}_{\text{Sep}} / R_{\text{H1}} \times 183\).

Why inventory is on COGS, not revenue

Inventory is carried at cost (Ind AS 2: lower of cost and net realisable value). Dividing a cost-based balance by a price-based revenue mixes bases and understates days by the gross margin: a paint company with a 42% material margin would show DIO 42% lower on revenue than on COGS. Revenue-based "inventory days" appear in some databases and in older Indian bank formats — recompute.

Defining COGS under the Indian nature-wise P&L

Schedule III Division II gives:

\[ \text{COGS}_{\text{mat}} = \text{Materials consumed} + \text{Purchases of stock-in-trade} + \Delta\text{Inventories (opening} - \text{closing)} \]

This is a material COGS (subtopic t1/s02). Inventory, however, is valued at full absorption cost, including conversion costs and production overheads. So DIO on material COGS overstates days relative to a full-cost DIO. Two practical options:

  1. Material-COGS DIO — easy, comparable across Indian peers, but overstated; label it.
  2. Full-cost DIO — add manufacturing costs (power and fuel, stores, job-work, manufacturing wages, plant depreciation) to the denominator; closer to IFRS/US peers.

For trading businesses and retailers the issue is minor (purchases of stock-in-trade dominate). For manufacturers with significant conversion cost (cement, chemicals) it can change DIO by 20–40%.

Raw-material days are often more useful operationally: \(\text{RM}_{\text{avg}} / \text{Materials consumed} \times 365\). Schedule III requires inventory to be shown by class (raw materials, WIP, finished goods, stock-in-trade, stores and spares, loose tools, goods in transit), which allows component days.

DPO: purchases vs COGS

Trade payables arise from purchases of materials and services on credit, not from COGS. Purchases can be derived:

\[ \text{Purchases} = \text{COGS}_{\text{mat}} + \text{Closing inventory} - \text{Opening inventory} \]

(for material purchases; strictly, use RM inventory change for materials consumed plus purchases of stock-in-trade). Since trade payables also include services and expenses (freight, advertising, power, contract labour, job-work), a broad denominator is:

\[ \text{Purchases}_{\text{broad}} = \text{Material purchases} + \text{Other expenses (excluding non-cash and non-supplier items)} \]

Choosing COGS (material) as the denominator is common and acceptable for peer comparison if applied consistently, but it overstates DPO for companies with large service payables. Never use revenue: DPO on revenue understates payable days by the margin and makes CCC inconsistent (topic pitfall).

What not to include in trade payables: capex creditors (payables for capital goods — classified under other financial liabilities), employee dues, statutory dues (GST, TDS), interest accrued, and customer advances. Schedule III requires trade payables to be shown separately with MSME and ageing disclosures (from FY2021-22).

Receivables definition

Include trade receivables (net of expected credit loss allowance — or gross, but consistently) and, for service businesses, unbilled receivables that are unconditional rights to consideration (s03). Exclude loans, deposits, other receivables, and contract assets unless you are computing an "all-in" DSO. Remember that receivables are GST-inclusive while revenue is GST-exclusive (s02).

Illustration (illustrative paint company, ₹ crore, FY2024-25)

Item 31 Mar 2024 31 Mar 2025 Average
Trade receivables 1,150 1,350 1,250
Inventories 1,950 2,150 2,050
Trade payables (goods and services) 1,550 1,750 1,650

Revenue ₹11,500 crore; material COGS ₹6,600 crore; material purchases ₹6,800 crore (COGS + inventory increase of ₹200 crore).

  • DSO = 1,250 ÷ 11,500 × 365 = 39.7 days.
  • DIO = 2,050 ÷ 6,600 × 365 = 113.4 days (material-COGS basis).
  • DPO (purchases) = 1,650 ÷ 6,800 × 365 = 88.6 days; DPO (COGS) = 91.3 days.
  • CCC ≈ 39.7 + 113.4 − 88.6 = 64.5 days.

If DIO had been computed on revenue (65.1 days) and DPO on revenue (52.4 days), CCC would read 52.4 days — wrong, and wrong in a different direction for every company depending on its margin.

Model implementation

=AVERAGE(TradeRec_Open,TradeRec_Close)/RevenueOps*DaysInYear

In the 3-statement model (Week 14), forecast balances from days: \(\text{TR}_t = \text{DSO} \times R_t / 365\) — applied to closing balances. Keep historical days on the same basis (closing or average) as the forecast driver, or the first forecast year will show a spurious working-capital swing.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Cash sales: retailers and QSRs have near-zero DSO; dividing receivables by total revenue is fine but meaningless — focus on DIO and DPO.
  • Export receivables are GST zero-rated, so the GST gross-up (s02) applies only to the domestic portion.
  • ECL allowance: compute DSO on gross receivables to see underlying collection behaviour, and on net to tie to the balance sheet.
  • Leap years: 366 days in FY2023-24 (includes 29 February 2024); usually ignored but be consistent.
  • Inventory held at NRV after write-downs lowers DIO — a "better" number signalling a problem.
  • Consignment and goods in transit: check whether inventory includes goods in transit (it should, if title has passed).

Key takeaways

  • Match balance to flow: receivables to revenue, inventory to COGS, payables to purchases (or COGS).
  • Indian material COGS omits conversion costs, so DIO on it is overstated; label the basis or build full-cost COGS.
  • Purchases = COGS + Δinventory; payables also include services, so a broad denominator may be needed.
  • Exclude capex creditors, statutory dues and employee dues from trade payables.
  • Keep historical days on the same basis as forecast drivers.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute days on correct denominators [L1]

A cement company reports (₹ crore): revenue 21,000; materials consumed 3,400; purchases of stock-in-trade 300; changes in inventories (100) (an increase); power and fuel 5,200; freight 4,700. Opening/closing trade receivables 1,050/1,250; inventories 1,900/2,100; trade payables 2,300/2,700. Compute DSO, DIO on material COGS and on COGS including power and fuel, and DPO on COGS and on purchases.

Q2. Broad purchases denominator [L2]

For the cement company in Q1, trade payables include creditors for power, fuel and freight. Construct a broad purchases denominator and recompute DPO. Explain why the material-COGS DPO is misleading for this sector.

💡 Hint: Ask which costs actually generate trade payables and add only those to the denominator.

Q3. Spot the errors [L2]

A database shows for a paint company: inventory days 62 (inventory ÷ revenue × 365) and payable days 49 (payables ÷ revenue × 365). Its material margin is 41%. Recompute both on COGS, compute the effect on CCC, and write a two-line warning for a peer table.

Q4. Interview: why not revenue for DPO [L1]

"Why can't I just divide everything by revenue to keep it simple?" Answer in 100 words, using a numerical illustration of your own.
