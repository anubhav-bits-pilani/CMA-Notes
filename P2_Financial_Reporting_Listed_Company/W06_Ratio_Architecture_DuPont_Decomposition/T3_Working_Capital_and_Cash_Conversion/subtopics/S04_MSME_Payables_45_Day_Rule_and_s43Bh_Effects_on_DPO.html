Subtopic note

MSME Payables: 45-Day Rule and s.43B(h) Effects on DPO

Scope: MSME payables: MSMED Act 45-day limit and s.43B(h) disallowance changing DPO behaviour from FY2023-24

Why it matters

For decades, large Indian buyers financed part of their working capital by stretching payments to small suppliers. The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006 (MSMED Act) capped payment terms at 45 days and imposed penal interest, but enforcement was weak. The Finance Act, 2023 changed incentives decisively by inserting clause (h) in s.43B of the Income-tax Act, 1961: from FY2023-24 (AY 2024-25), any sum payable to a micro or small enterprise beyond the MSMED Act time limit is deductible only in the year of actual payment. Companies rushed to clear MSME dues before 31 March 2024, re-papered contracts to 45 days, and in some cases shifted purchases to medium or large suppliers or to traders. The result is a visible break in DPO for many manufacturers and FMCG companies between FY2022-23 and FY2024-25 — a structural change an analyst must recognise before reading "falling DPO" as weakening bargaining power.

Core notes

MSMED Act provisions

Section Provision
s.2(n) and Udyam classification Micro, small and medium enterprises classified by investment in plant and machinery and turnover (revised thresholds notified from 1 April 2025: micro up to ₹2.5 crore investment / ₹10 crore turnover; small up to ₹25 crore / ₹100 crore; medium up to ₹125 crore / ₹500 crore)
s.15 Buyer must pay on or before the date agreed in writing, which cannot exceed 45 days from the day of acceptance or deemed acceptance of goods/services; where there is no written agreement, before the "appointed day" — 15 days from acceptance
s.16 Delayed payment attracts compound interest with monthly rests at three times the bank rate notified by RBI
s.17 Buyer liable to pay amount with interest
s.18 Disputes referable to the MSE Facilitation Council (conciliation, arbitration)
s.22 Buyer's audited annual accounts must disclose unpaid principal and interest to MSEs, interest paid, and interest accrued and remaining unpaid
s.23 Interest payable under s.16 is not deductible for income-tax

Deemed acceptance: where no objection is raised within 15 days of delivery, the day of delivery is the day of acceptance.

Coverage nuance: delayed-payment protection (Chapter V of the MSMED Act) is for micro and small enterprises; medium enterprises are not covered. Retail and wholesale traders registered on Udyam were brought within the MSME fold in 2021 for priority-sector lending, but the government's notification limited their benefit to priority-sector lending, so delayed-payment provisions — and hence s.43B(h) — are generally understood not to apply to purchases from traders. Verify supplier status (Udyam certificate, category, activity) — this is a significant compliance exercise.

Income-tax s.43B(h)

  • Inserted by the Finance Act, 2023, applicable from AY 2024-25 (FY2023-24).
  • Any sum payable by the assessee to a micro or small enterprise beyond the time limit specified in s.15 of the MSMED Act is allowed as a deduction only in the year of actual payment, irrespective of the accounting method.
  • Effect: an unpaid ₹10 crore MSE payable outstanding beyond 45 days at 31 March is added back to taxable income for that year; the deduction comes in the year of payment — a timing difference creating a deferred tax asset under Ind AS 12 (and a current-tax cash cost).
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaces the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026; the provision is carried into the new Act under a different section number — confirm the mapping for FY2026-27 onwards.

Schedule III disclosure

From FY2021-22, Schedule III requires trade payables to be split into total outstanding dues of micro and small enterprises and others, with an ageing schedule (not due, < 1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years, > 3 years; disputed and undisputed) for each. The s.22 MSMED disclosures appear in the trade payables note. Together these let an analyst compute an MSE DPO and track the shift.

Behavioural change and DPO

Behaviour Effect on reported DPO
Paying MSE dues within 45 days MSE portion of DPO falls to ≤ 45 days
Year-end clearance of MSE dues before 31 March Closing payables fall sharply; average-based DPO falls less
Re-papering contracts with medium/large suppliers to longer terms Non-MSE DPO rises
Moving purchases from MSEs to traders/medium enterprises MSE share of payables falls
Using TReDS to let MSE suppliers discount invoices (s05) Supplier paid early by financier; buyer pays financier at due date (≤ 45 days for MSE invoices)

Blended DPO decomposition:

\[ \text{DPO} = w_{\text{MSE}} \times \text{DPO}_{\text{MSE}} + (1 - w_{\text{MSE}}) \times \text{DPO}_{\text{other}} \]

where \(w_{\text{MSE}}\) is the MSE share of credit purchases. If MSE purchases are 30% of the total and their DPO falls from 75 to 40 days, blended DPO falls by 10.5 days — even with no change in bargaining power over other suppliers.

Illustration (illustrative FMCG company, ₹ crore)

Item FY2022-23 FY2023-24
Purchases (COGS basis) 9,000 9,600
MSE share of purchases 28% 25%
Closing trade payables — MSE 610 290
Closing trade payables — others 1,750 1,920
Closing total payables 2,360 2,210
  • Closing DPO FY2022-23 = 2,360 ÷ 9,000 × 365 = 95.7 days; FY2023-24 = 2,210 ÷ 9,600 × 365 = 84.0 days.
  • MSE closing DPO: 610 ÷ 2,520 × 365 = 88.4 days → 290 ÷ 2,400 × 365 = 44.1 days.
  • Other-supplier DPO: 1,750 ÷ 6,480 × 365 = 98.6 days → 1,920 ÷ 7,200 × 365 = 97.3 days.

The 11.7-day fall in DPO is almost entirely the MSE effect; terms with other suppliers are broadly unchanged. Cash impact: ≈ ₹320 crore of extra cash paid to MSEs — a one-time working-capital outflow, partly reversing tax-wise as no disallowance arises.

Analytical checklist

  1. Pull MSE and other payables from the trade payables note for at least FY2021-22 to FY2024-25.
  2. Read the s.22 disclosure: principal and interest overdue; interest paid under s.16. Non-zero overdue amounts indicate a s.43B(h) disallowance and possible penal interest liability.
  3. Check the deferred tax note for a DTA on s.43B items.
  4. Decompose DPO into MSE and other; restate the trend.
  5. Look for offsetting moves — supplier finance (s05), TReDS, longer terms with large suppliers.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Supplier classification is self-reported via Udyam; buyers depend on suppliers' declarations. Misclassification risk sits with the buyer for tax.
  • Agreed terms vs actual payment: s.43B(h) bites only when payment is beyond the s.15 limit; a written 45-day term paid on day 44 is compliant.
  • Disputed invoices: acceptance date may be deferred by a valid objection; disputed MSE dues appear in the ageing schedule as disputed.
  • Capex creditors to MSEs are also covered by s.43B(h) if the sum is otherwise deductible (e.g., revenue expenditure); capital items are depreciated, so the application is debated — note the uncertainty.
  • Medium-enterprise reclassification: as suppliers grow past the small threshold, they exit the protection — DPO on them can lengthen.
  • Quarterly data: Reg 33 half-yearly balance sheets show whether MSE clearance is a year-end event (window dressing) or sustained.

Key takeaways

  • MSMED Act s.15 caps MSE payment terms at 45 days (15 days without written agreement); s.16 imposes compound interest at three times the RBI bank rate; s.23 makes that interest non-deductible.
  • s.43B(h), from FY2023-24, disallows unpaid MSE dues beyond the s.15 limit until paid — creating a strong incentive to pay on time.
  • Schedule III MSE payables and ageing disclosures allow an MSE-specific DPO.
  • Falling DPO from FY2023-24 is often an MSE compliance effect, not lost bargaining power; decompose before concluding.
  • Watch for offsetting levers: TReDS, supplier finance, and terms with larger suppliers.

Practice Questions

Q1. Disallowance and deferred tax [L1]

At 31 March 2025 a company has MSE payables of ₹140 crore, of which ₹38 crore is outstanding beyond the s.15 limit (all revenue expenditure). It pays ₹30 crore of the overdue amount in May 2025 and the rest in FY2026-27. Tax rate 25.168%. Compute the s.43B(h) disallowance for FY2024-25, the deferred tax asset at 31 March 2025, and the s.16 interest exposure if the RBI bank rate is 6.75% and the overdue amount was 60 days late on average.

💡 Hint: Interest compounds monthly at three times the bank rate; the disallowance is the overdue principal.

Q2. Decompose DPO [L2]

FY2023-24 and FY2024-25 data (₹ crore): purchases 12,000 and 13,200; MSE share of purchases 35% and 31%; average MSE payables 1,250 and 640; average other payables 2,050 and 2,400. Compute blended, MSE and other DPO for both years, and the cash impact of the change in MSE DPO.

Q3. Commentary review [L2]

A company's management commentary says "DPO reduced from 92 to 78 days as we strengthened supplier relationships." Its trade payables note shows MSE payables down from ₹820 crore to ₹310 crore, and its other financial liabilities show a new ₹400 crore line "Acceptances under supplier finance arrangements". Draft three questions for the earnings call and explain how you would restate DPO.

Q4. Policy design [L3]

As FP&A lead of a mid-cap auto component manufacturer with 40% of purchases from MSEs, design a payables policy that complies with s.15/s.43B(h), minimises the working-capital impact, and is defensible to auditors. Cover contract terms, supplier master data, TReDS, month-end controls and KPI reporting.
