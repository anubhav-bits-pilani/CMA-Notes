Subtopic note

GST Gross-Up in Receivable and Payable Days

Scope: GST gross-up: receivables and payables include GST while revenue and COGS exclude it

Why it matters

Since 1 July 2017, Indian revenue from operations is reported net of GST because GST is collected on behalf of the government (Ind AS 115.47: transaction price excludes amounts collected on behalf of third parties). But the invoice the customer owes — and therefore the trade receivable — includes GST. The same asymmetry applies to trade payables: the supplier's invoice includes GST, while purchases and COGS are recorded net of input tax credit (ITC) where credit is available. Dividing a GST-inclusive balance by a GST-exclusive flow overstates DSO and DPO by the GST rate — 18% for paints, which turns a true 40-day DSO into a reported 47 days. Errors of this size swamp the year-on-year changes you are trying to explain, and they distort comparisons across sectors with different GST rates and across periods when rates changed.

Core notes

The mechanics

For a domestic sale of ₹100 at 18% GST, revenue is ₹100, the customer owes ₹118, and ₹18 is a GST output liability (netted against ITC and paid to the government). If the customer pays in 45 days:

\[ \text{Reported DSO} = \frac{118 \times 45/365}{100} \times 365 = 53.1 \text{ days}, \qquad \text{True DSO} = 45 \text{ days} \]

Overstatement factor = \(1 + g\), where \(g\) is the effective GST rate on credit sales.

Two equivalent corrections

Deflate the balance:

\[ \text{DSO}_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\overline{\text{TR}} / (1 + g_s)}{R} \times 365 \]

Gross up the flow:

\[ \text{DSO}_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\overline{\text{TR}}}{R \times (1 + g_s)} \times 365 \]

Both give the same answer. Similarly for payables, with \(g_p\) the effective GST rate on credit purchases on which ITC is claimed:

\[ \text{DPO}_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\overline{\text{TP}}}{\text{Purchases} \times (1 + g_p)} \times 365 \]

Inventory is carried at cost excluding recoverable GST (ITC is a receivable from the government, not part of inventory cost under Ind AS 2.11), so DIO needs no adjustment — unless ITC is blocked (e.g., certain inputs under s.17(5) CGST Act), in which case GST is part of cost on both sides and also needs no adjustment.

Estimating the effective GST rate

The effective rate is rarely a single statutory rate:

Factor Effect on effective \(g\)
Exports (zero-rated supplies) Reduce \(g_s\) — receivables from export customers carry no GST
Product mix across slabs (5%, 18%, 40% after the September 2025 rationalisation; earlier 12% and 28% slabs) Blend by revenue share
Exempt or nil-rated supplies Reduce \(g_s\)
SEZ supplies Zero-rated
Purchases from unregistered suppliers, imports (IGST paid at customs, not in payables) Reduce \(g_p\)
Services in payables (freight under RCM, advertising, power — electricity outside GST) Mixed; electricity and some fuels outside GST

A practical estimate: \(g_s = \text{statutory rate} \times \text{domestic share of revenue}\). Disclosure sources: segment/geography revenue (Ind AS 108), the revenue note (domestic vs export), and GST rate notifications for the product.

Sector rates and comparability

  • Paints: 18% (unchanged in the 2025 rationalisation). All three exercise companies (Asian Paints, Berger, Kansai Nerolac) are predominantly domestic, so \(g_s\) ≈ 16–18% on standalone figures; consolidated figures include overseas subsidiaries' receivables with VAT/GST at local rates.
  • Cement: 28% until 21 September 2025; 18% from 22 September 2025 under the GST rate rationalisation. Receivable gross-up drops mid-year — a structural break in reported DSO for FY2025-26 that is not a collection improvement.
  • IT services: largely exports of services (zero-rated), so \(g_s\) is small for Indian IT majors.
  • FMCG: mixed slabs; many items moved to 5% in 2025.
  • Jewellery: 3% on gold jewellery — small gross-up.

Always check the rate history for the periods in your dataset; the GST rate changes (2017 transition, 2018–19 cuts, and the 2025 rationalisation) change the gross-up factor.

Illustration (illustrative, ₹ crore)

A paint company with revenue ₹12,000 crore, 94% domestic (18% GST), 6% exports; average trade receivables ₹1,600 crore; purchases ₹7,000 crore, of which 80% domestic with 18% GST ITC and 20% imports (IGST paid at customs, not in trade payables); average trade payables ₹2,100 crore.

  • Reported DSO = 1,600 ÷ 12,000 × 365 = 48.7 days.
  • \(g_s\) = 18% × 0.94 = 16.92%; adjusted DSO = 48.7 ÷ 1.1692 = 41.6 days.
  • Reported DPO = 2,100 ÷ 7,000 × 365 = 109.5 days.
  • \(g_p\) = 18% × 0.80 = 14.4%; adjusted DPO = 109.5 ÷ 1.144 = 95.7 days.
  • Net effect on CCC: DSO falls 7.1 days, DPO falls 13.8 days, so CCC rises by 6.7 days after adjustment — the GST effect is not one-directional.

When the adjustment matters and when it doesn't

  • Trend analysis within one company at a constant GST rate and mix: the bias is constant; trends are still valid, though levels are overstated.
  • Cross-sector or cross-rate comparisons: adjust, otherwise a cement company (28% before September 2025) looks to collect much slower than a jeweller (3%).
  • Model drivers: in the 3-statement model, if you forecast receivables as DSO × revenue ÷ 365 using reported (unadjusted) DSO, the forecast balance is GST-inclusive — consistent with the balance sheet. That is fine as long as historical and forecast days are on the same basis. Adjusted days are for analysis; unadjusted days can be used as drivers.
  • Cash forecasting (Week 18): model GST output and ITC cash flows separately; the GST payable/receivable balances are distinct working-capital items.

Excel implementation

=AvgTradeRec/(RevenueOps*(1+GSTRate*DomesticShare))*DaysInYear

Keep GSTRate and DomesticShare as named input cells by company and year, with a source note.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • TCS/TDS on receivables: TDS deducted by customers (s.194Q, s.194C/J) reduces cash received; the receivable is settled partly by a tax credit. High TDS receivable balances belong in non-current/current tax assets, not trade receivables.
  • Pre-GST periods: excise duty was part of revenue (gross) in early Ind AS years (FY2016-17 and Q1 FY2017-18), while VAT/CST was excluded; the gross-up logic differs.
  • Credit notes and discounts: post-sale discounts with GST credit notes reduce both receivable and output tax.
  • Reverse charge (RCM): payables to suppliers under RCM exclude GST (paid directly by the recipient).
  • Consolidated data with foreign subsidiaries: use local VAT rates or adjust only the Indian component.
  • Ineligible ITC (s.17(5)): GST is a cost and included in purchases; no gross-up needed for those payables.

Key takeaways

  • Receivables and payables include GST; revenue and COGS/purchases exclude it — reported DSO and DPO are overstated by roughly the effective GST rate.
  • Correct by deflating balances or grossing up flows by \((1 + g)\), using an effective rate that reflects exports, mix and exemptions.
  • Inventory needs no adjustment because recoverable GST is not part of cost.
  • Adjust for cross-sector, cross-rate and cross-period comparisons; unadjusted days are acceptable as model drivers if used consistently.
  • GST rate changes (e.g., cement 28% to 18% from 22 September 2025) create breaks in reported days.

Practice Questions

Q1. Adjust DSO and DPO [L1]

A consumer durables company reports revenue ₹8,000 crore (90% domestic at 18% GST), average trade receivables ₹1,300 crore, purchases ₹5,500 crore (70% domestic with 18% ITC; rest imported) and average trade payables ₹1,400 crore. Compute reported and GST-adjusted DSO and DPO and the net effect on CCC.

Q2. Cement rate change break [L2]

A cement company's revenue is ₹20,000 crore a year, evenly spread, entirely domestic, and its customers pay in 30 days throughout. GST was 28% until 21 September 2025 and 18% from 22 September 2025. Estimate reported DSO on 31 March 2025 and 31 March 2026 balances, and explain how you would present the change in an investor note.

💡 Hint: Compute the receivable balance from 30 days of GST-inclusive sales at each date.

Q3. Cross-sector table [L2]

Prepare a peer table comparing reported and adjusted DSO for a paint company (18%, 95% domestic, reported DSO 44 days), a jeweller (3%, 100% domestic, reported DSO 12 days) and an IT services company (18% on domestic services, 8% domestic, reported DSO 72 days). Comment on which comparisons change materially.

Q4. Driver basis in the model [L3]

Your colleague uses GST-adjusted DSO as the forecast driver in a 3-statement model and multiplies it by GST-exclusive revenue. Explain the effect on forecast receivables, operating cash flow and the balance sheet, and recommend the correct practice.
