Subtopic note

Channel Finance, Factoring, TReDS and Supplier Finance in Days

Scope: Channel finance, factoring, TReDS and supplier finance flattering DSO/DPO

Why it matters

A company can shorten DSO and lengthen DPO without changing its commercial terms at all — by bringing a bank or NBFC into the chain. Dealers borrow from a bank under a channel finance programme and pay the company early; receivables are sold to a factor; MSE suppliers discount invoices on TReDS; large suppliers are paid early by a bank under a reverse-factoring (supplier finance) programme while the buyer pays the bank later. Each arrangement makes working-capital days look better and operating cash flow look stronger, while the economic cost (interest, discount, guarantees) and risk (recourse, first-loss) sit elsewhere — in finance costs, in discounts deducted from revenue, in contingent liabilities, or in an obscure line of "other financial liabilities". For peer comparison, credit analysis and forensic work, an adjusted CCC that reverses these effects is essential.

Core notes

The four arrangements

Arrangement Who finances Effect on company's balance sheet Reported effect Economic reality
Channel / dealer finance Bank lends to dealers against company's invoices; company may give first-loss default guarantee (FLDG), interest subvention or a buy-back of stock Receivable collected early (or never recorded as long-dated) DSO falls Company bears subvention cost (often in discounts or finance cost) and credit risk via guarantees
Factoring / bill discounting Factor or bank buys receivables Receivable derecognised if without recourse (Ind AS 109 tests); stays on BS with a borrowing if with recourse DSO falls if derecognised; CFO up Financing cost; with recourse, it is debt
TReDS Financiers discount MSE suppliers' invoices accepted by the buyer, without recourse to the MSE Buyer's payable remains until due date; payable to financier rather than supplier DPO unchanged at ≤ 45 days for MSE invoices; supplier gets cash early Helps MSE compliance; buyer's obligation is unchanged
Supplier finance / reverse factoring Bank pays supplier early at buyer's request; buyer pays bank at an extended date Payable may stay as trade payable or be reclassified as borrowing/other financial liability depending on terms DPO rises if extended terms are in trade payables Bank-funded extension of payment terms — debt-like

Factoring and Ind AS 109 derecognition

Ind AS 109 Chapter 3.2 sets a sequence: has the entity transferred the contractual rights to cash flows (or a qualifying pass-through)? If so, has it transferred substantially all risks and rewards? If yes — derecognise. If it has retained substantially all — continue to recognise and record a financial liability for the proceeds (collateralised borrowing). If neither — derecognise only if control of the asset has been given up; otherwise continue to recognise to the extent of continuing involvement.

  • Without recourse (factor bears credit loss): usually derecognition.
  • With recourse (company reimburses defaults): risks retained — no derecognition; proceeds are a borrowing, and cash received is financing, not operating, cash flow under Ind AS 7.
  • Partial recourse/first-loss guarantees: judgement on whether substantially all risks are retained.

Supplier finance: classification and disclosure

The IASB issued amendments to IAS 7 and IFRS 7 in May 2023 requiring disclosure of supplier finance arrangements — terms, carrying amounts of liabilities in the arrangement, the portion already paid by finance providers, ranges of payment due dates compared with non-arrangement payables, and non-cash changes. Check whether the corresponding Ind AS 7 / Ind AS 107 amendments have been notified by MCA for the period you analyse; disclosure practice in India before notification is uneven. Classification judgement (trade payable vs borrowing) turns on whether the terms are substantially modified — extended well beyond normal supplier terms, bank-imposed security, or interest borne by the buyer suggests the liability is in substance a borrowing.

TReDS

  • RBI-regulated Trade Receivables Discounting System platforms (e.g., RXIL, M1xchange, Invoicemart, and newer licensees) under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act framework.
  • The buyer uploads/accepts MSE invoices; financiers bid; the MSE gets paid at a discount without recourse; the buyer pays the financier on the due date.
  • The government required companies above a turnover threshold and CPSEs to onboard TReDS; a November 2024 notification lowered the threshold to companies with turnover above ₹250 crore — verify the current threshold and the related Form MSME filing requirements.
  • For the buyer, DPO on MSE invoices is still capped by s.15/s.43B(h) (s04); TReDS helps compliance, not DPO extension.

Building an adjusted CCC

Adjusted DSO:

\[ \text{DSO}_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\overline{\text{TR}} + \overline{\text{Receivables factored and derecognised}} + \overline{\text{Channel finance outstanding (if company bears risk)}}}{R \times (1 + g)} \times 365 \]

Adjusted DPO:

\[ \text{DPO}_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\overline{\text{TP}} - \overline{\text{Payables under supplier finance beyond normal terms}}}{\text{Purchases} \times (1 + g_p)} \times 365 \]

and add the removed supplier-finance amount to debt. For credit analysis, add recourse factoring and supplier finance to borrowings; for channel finance with FLDG, consider a contingent debt adjustment.

Where to find the data

  • Notes on trade receivables (derecognised receivables, "bills discounted"), financial risk management (Ind AS 107 transfers of financial assets disclosures), contingent liabilities (guarantees to banks for dealer finance), other financial liabilities ("acceptances", "supplier finance"), borrowings note (bill discounting), related-party notes, and earnings call transcripts.
  • Bank appraisal notes and credit rating rationales (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings) often quantify channel finance and acceptances.

Illustration (illustrative auto-component company, ₹ crore)

Item Average balance
Revenue (GST-exclusive) 8,000
Purchases 5,200
Reported trade receivables 900
Receivables factored without recourse (outstanding) 600
Reported trade payables 1,700
of which: supplier finance (terms extended from 60 to 150 days) 500
GST rate (all domestic) 18%
  • Reported DSO = 900 ÷ 8,000 × 365 = 41.1 days; GST-adjusted = 34.8 days.
  • Adjusted DSO incl. factored = 1,500 ÷ (8,000 × 1.18) × 365 = 58.0 days.
  • Reported DPO = 1,700 ÷ 5,200 × 365 = 119.3 days; GST-adjusted = 101.1 days.
  • Adjusted DPO ex-supplier finance = 1,200 ÷ (5,200 × 1.18) × 365 = 71.4 days.
  • Reported CCC (ignoring inventory, which is unaffected) moves from 41.1 − 119.3 = −78.2 days to an adjusted 58.0 − 71.4 = −13.4 days. Debt rises by ₹500 crore (and by ₹600 crore if the factoring is judged to be with recourse in substance).

Cash-flow effects

Factoring accelerates CFO; supplier finance delays operating outflows — both flatter CFO and FCF. When programmes are scaled back (bank limits cut, interest rates rise), CFO falls abruptly. This "working-capital cliff" is a known forensic pattern (Week 5 w5_t4 covered the CFO side; Ind AS 7.44A financing-liabilities reconciliation can reveal supplier finance reclassified as debt).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Channel finance via related NBFCs: if the company's own NBFC or a group entity finances dealers, consolidation may bring the receivable back.
  • Interest subvention booked as discount: reduces revenue (Ind AS 115 variable consideration) rather than finance cost — margin effect, not interest.
  • Year-end-only programmes: factoring spikes in March and reverses in April — compare Reg 33 half-yearly data (s06).
  • Supplier finance that keeps normal terms: if the buyer pays the bank on the original due date, DPO is not flattered — no adjustment needed.
  • TReDS acceptances may be presented as "acceptances" in other financial liabilities; they are trade-related and within 45 days.
  • Bills of exchange accepted (LC-backed buyer's credit) are debt-like.

Key takeaways

  • Channel finance and factoring shorten DSO; supplier finance lengthens DPO — none changes commercial terms.
  • Ind AS 109 derecognition depends on transfer of risks and rewards and control; recourse factoring is a borrowing.
  • TReDS is without recourse to the MSE and helps s.15 compliance; it does not extend buyer DPO.
  • Build an adjusted CCC that adds back factored receivables, strips extended supplier finance from payables, and moves those amounts to debt.
  • Watch for working-capital cliffs when programmes shrink.

Practice Questions

Q1. Adjusted days [L1]

A company reports average trade receivables ₹1,200 crore, revenue ₹9,500 crore, average trade payables ₹2,000 crore, purchases ₹6,300 crore. Notes disclose average receivables factored without recourse ₹450 crore and supplier finance payables (terms extended from 45 to 120 days) ₹700 crore. GST 18%, all domestic. Compute reported and adjusted DSO, DPO and the receivables/payables part of CCC, and the debt adjustment.

Q2. Derecognition judgement [L2]

A company sells ₹300 crore of dealer receivables to a bank. The bank bears credit losses beyond a first-loss amount of 8% of the pool, which the company absorbs; historical losses are 1.5%. The company continues to collect from dealers on the bank's behalf. Discuss whether the receivables are derecognised under Ind AS 109, the effect on DSO and CFO, and the disclosure required.

💡 Hint: Compare the retained first-loss exposure with the expected variability of losses before deciding on risks and rewards.

Q3. Paints channel finance [L2]

A paint company encourages dealers to use a bank channel finance programme with 30-day credit at a subvented interest rate; the company bears 2 percentage points of the dealers' interest cost, accounted for as a discount. Dealer finance outstanding averages ₹700 crore; the company's revenue is ₹9,000 crore and reported DSO is 22 days. Explain how you would compare this company's DSO with a peer offering 45-day direct credit, and quantify the subvention's margin cost.

Q4. Working-capital cliff [L3]

A mid-cap company's CFO rose from ₹400 crore to ₹1,100 crore over two years while EBITDA grew 15%; reported DPO rose from 70 to 125 days and "acceptances" in other financial liabilities rose from nil to ₹650 crore. Prepare a forensic note: likely explanation, adjustments to CFO, net debt and CCC, and the risk if the bank cuts its supplier-finance limit by half.
