Roadmap topic: Profitability & Return Ratios: Gross Margin, EBITDA Margin, EBIT Margin, PAT Margin, ROA, ROE, ROCE, and Return on Invested Capital (ROIC vs WACC spread)

Overview

Profitability ratios answer two different questions that are often confused. Margins ask how much of each rupee of revenue survives each layer of cost — materials, operating overheads, depreciation, interest and tax. Returns ask how much profit is generated per rupee of capital tied up in the business, from the perspective of a particular capital provider: shareholders (ROE), all capital providers pre-tax (ROCE), all capital providers post-tax on operating capital only (ROIC), or the asset base as a whole (ROA). A high-margin business can earn a poor return if it needs heavy capital; a thin-margin business (a retailer, a distributor) can earn an excellent return if capital turns quickly. Topic t2 (DuPont) formalises that link; this topic builds the components.

For the target roles the topic matters in three ways. In financial reporting, Schedule III (as amended in 2021) requires every company to disclose ROE, net profit ratio, ROCE and return on investment with stated numerators and denominators and explanations of changes above 25% — a year-end deliverable that auditors test. In equity research and valuation, ROIC relative to WACC determines whether growth creates value, and EVA translates the spread into rupees; Indian markets pay very high multiples to the few companies (paint majors, consumer staples, IT services) that sustain wide spreads. In FP&A, margin bridges explain monthly performance and incremental ROIC disciplines capex and working-capital decisions.

Indian specifics run through every subtopic: the nature-wise Schedule III P&L means "gross margin" is really a material margin; s.115BAA (25.168% all-in rate) versus the old regime (up to 34.944%) changes NOPAT and the debt tax shield; Ind AS 116 changes EBITDA, EBIT and capital employed for lease-heavy sectors; and cash-rich balance sheets make the treatment of surplus cash the single biggest swing factor in ROCE.

The learning sequence is: (1) build the margin stack on revenue from operations (s01); (2) understand why Indian gross margin is a material margin and how to build a pseudo-COGS (s02); (3) define the four return ratios with matched numerators and denominators (s03); (4) design denominators — averages, cash, goodwill, leases (s04); (5) connect returns to value through the ROIC–WACC spread, EVA and incremental ROIC (s05); and (6) produce and interpret the Schedule III return ratios and their 25% variance explanations (s06).

Related concepts to study

Cost-of-sales vs nature-method gross margin

IFRS/US GAAP cost-of-sales presentation inflates "gross" cost vs Indian nature-method COGS

IFRS permits a function-of-expense (cost of sales) presentation and US GAAP filers almost always use it: cost of sales absorbs factory wages, power, plant depreciation and production overheads, so "gross cost" is much larger than the Indian Schedule III material cost, and gross margin is correspondingly lower. Comparing an Indian paint company's 40%+ material margin with a global coatings company's cost-of-sales gross margin is therefore misleading. To compare, rebuild a pseudo-COGS from the notes to other expenses (power and fuel, stores, job-work, repairs), an assumed manufacturing share of employee cost and plant depreciation (s02). Infosys and Wipro, which file function-wise IFRS statements on Form 20-F alongside nature-wise Ind AS statements, provide a live reconciliation of the two formats. IFRS 18 will further change IFRS presentation from 2027.

WACC build for the value spread

WACC build (Ind G-sec, ERP, beta, cost of debt post 115BAA tax) → Week 15

The ROIC–WACC spread is only as reliable as the WACC. In Week 15 you will build it from the Indian 10-year G-sec yield as the risk-free rate, an India equity risk premium (practitioner range 6–8%, sourced from surveys or implied from Nifty), a beta (regression against Nifty 50 or bottom-up from relevered peer betas), and a pre-tax cost of debt from ratings spreads or actual borrowing cost, tax-effected at 25.168% for a s.115BAA company (34.944% for an old-regime company with income above ₹10 crore). For this week, take WACC as given, but ensure it is post-tax and nominal, and compare it only with post-tax ROIC on the same operating capital base. Sensitivity of EVA to ±100 bps of WACC is a standard part of the analysis.

Common pitfalls

Pre-tax ROCE against post-tax WACC

Mixing pre-tax ROCE with post-tax WACC when computing the value spread

The mistake: computing "ROCE − WACC" — e.g., 24% ROCE minus 12% WACC = 12% spread — and calling it value creation. Why it happens: ROCE is the ratio quoted in broker reports and Schedule III, and it is readily available. Correct treatment: WACC is post-tax (the debt tax shield is inside \(k_D(1 - t)\)), so the comparator must be post-tax ROIC: \(24\% \times (1 - 0.25168) = 18.0\%\), giving a 6% spread, half the naive figure. For a company at the margin (ROCE 15%, WACC 12%), the naive spread is positive but the true spread is negative (\(15\% \times 0.748 = 11.2\%\)). Detection in review: check that the numerator labelled "ROIC" uses NOPAT; check the tax line; recompute one company's spread manually.

Excess cash in capital employed

Including excess cash in capital employed for cash-rich companies (Asian Paints, IT majors) and understating operating returns

The mistake: computing ROCE as EBIT (excluding other income) over equity + debt, with ₹ thousands of crore of cash and liquid mutual funds sitting in equity. Why it happens: the financing-side definition (equity + debt) is quick, and cash is "part of capital". Correct treatment: either remove surplus cash (cash, bank deposits, liquid/debt MFs, net of an operating cash allowance) from the denominator and exclude treasury income from the numerator, or keep both in; report operating ROCE/ROIC for business quality and inclusive ROCE for capital allocation. For IT majors with cash at 30–50% of equity, the difference is 10–20 percentage points. Detection: compare cash and current investments to capital employed; if above ~15%, check that numerator and denominator treatment are symmetric.

Closing equity in a buyback or QIP year

Using closing equity in a year of large buyback or QIP

The mistake: computing ROE on closing equity (or a simple average) when a large equity event occurred during the year. Why it happens: databases often show ROE on closing equity, and a simple average looks reasonable. Correct treatment: a QIP late in the year inflates closing equity and understates ROE; a buyback early in the year depresses closing equity and overstates ROE (IT majors running ₹10,000+ crore buybacks are the classic case). Use a time-weighted average equity, weighting each issue or buyback by the months it was outstanding, and treat profit and dividends as accruing at their actual timing. Detection: scan the statement of changes in equity for issues, buybacks and special dividends greater than ~10% of opening equity; if present, check the averaging method.

Q2. Schedule III disclosure and analyst reconciliation [L2]

A company's standalone Schedule III note shows ROE 19.8% (prior 26.4%), ROCE 25.1% (prior 30.2%) and net profit ratio 10.9% (prior 12.6%), with ROCE defined as EBIT ÷ (tangible net worth + total debt + DTL) on closing balances. During the year it completed a ₹2,000 crore QIP on 15 March and capitalised a ₹1,400 crore plant on 1 February. Opening tangible net worth was ₹8,000 crore, closing ₹11,200 crore; debt ₹1,500 crore at both dates; DTL ₹300 crore at both dates; EBIT ₹3,200 crore. (a) Identify which ratios need 25% explanations. (b) Recompute ROCE on average capital employed and on time-weighted capital employed (assume profit accrues evenly). (c) Draft the variance explanations. (d) Explain why your recomputed ROCE is a better analytical number than the disclosed one.

💡 Hint: Separate the QIP timing effect from the plant ramp-up effect before drafting explanations.

Q3. Incremental ROIC and the capex decision [L3]

A cement company's NOPAT and invested capital (₹ crore, 31 March): 2021 IC 12,000; 2022 IC 12,800, NOPAT FY22 1,500; 2023 IC 14,500, NOPAT FY23 1,350; 2024 IC 17,000, NOPAT FY24 1,600; 2025 IC 19,500, NOPAT FY25 1,700. WACC is 11%. The board is considering a further ₹4,000 crore expansion that management projects will add ₹520 crore of NOPAT at maturity. (a) Compute average ROIC and lagged three-year incremental ROIC for FY2024-25. (b) Compute EVA for FY2022-23 to FY2024-25 on opening IC. (c) Evaluate whether management's projection is consistent with its track record. (d) List three questions you would ask before recommending approval.

Q4. Lease-heavy retailer: denominator consistency [L2]

EBITDA (post-Ind AS 116) 2,100; ROU depreciation 680; other D&A 260; lease interest 390; other finance cost 45; lease payments (principal + interest) 1,020; tax rate 25.168%. (a) Compute ROCE and ROIC on a lease-as-debt basis. (b) Compute rent-adjusted ROCE and ROIC excluding leases. (c) Show the inconsistent ROCE a junior would get by using post-Ind AS 116 EBIT with lease liabilities excluded from capital employed. (d) Recommend the basis for comparing this retailer with a franchise-led peer and justify it.
