Subtopic note

Indian Gross Margin Is a Material Margin, Not a Full-Cost Margin

Scope: Indian gross margin limitation: nature-wise P&L excludes conversion costs, so gross margin is closer to material margin

Why it matters

Analysts trained on US or IFRS filers expect "gross margin" to mean revenue less cost of goods sold, where COGS absorbs factory labour, power, plant depreciation and production overheads. Schedule III Division II forces Indian companies to present expenses by nature, so the only line you can subtract cleanly from revenue is material cost. The result is a "gross margin" that is 15–30 percentage points higher than a cost-of-sales gross margin for the same business. Comparing Asian Paints' "gross margin" with Sherwin-Williams' or PPG's, or an Indian cement company with a European peer, without adjusting, produces nonsense. Inside India the problem is subtler: two domestic peers with different degrees of outsourcing (contract manufacturing versus own plants) also show non-comparable material margins.

Core notes

Function-wise vs nature-wise presentation

Ind AS 1.99 requires an analysis of expenses using a classification based on either their nature or their function within the entity, whichever provides information that is reliable and more relevant. IFRS filers choose freely. Schedule III Division II, however, prescribes a nature-wise format for the Statement of Profit and Loss: cost of materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade, changes in inventories, employee benefits expense, finance costs, depreciation and amortisation, other expenses. Indian companies cannot present a "cost of sales" line in the face of the P&L. (IFRS 18, issued by the IASB in 2024 and effective internationally from 2027, will change IFRS presentation by requiring defined operating subtotals; whether and when an Ind AS equivalent is notified is date-sensitive — check MCA notifications.)

What the Indian "COGS" contains

\[ \text{COGS}_{\text{Ind}} = \text{Materials consumed} + \text{Purchases of stock-in-trade} + \Delta\text{Inventories (opening} - \text{closing)} \]

Missing relative to a full-absorption COGS:

Cost element Where it sits in the Indian P&L In IFRS cost of sales?
Factory wages, supervisors Employee benefits expense Yes
Power and fuel Other expenses Yes
Stores and spares, packing (if not in materials) Other expenses or materials Yes
Plant depreciation Depreciation and amortisation Yes
Factory rent, repairs to plant Other expenses Yes
Job-work / contract manufacturing charges Other expenses (or materials, varies) Yes
Inbound freight Often materials; sometimes other expenses Yes
Outbound freight, A&P, selling staff Other expenses / employee No (distribution)

The "changes in inventories" line is valued at full absorption cost under Ind AS 2.10–12 (inventory cost includes conversion costs and allocated fixed production overheads based on normal capacity). So the ΔInventory line contains conversion cost embedded in finished goods, while the conversion cost actually incurred in the year sits in employee, power and depreciation lines. In a year of inventory build-up, this mismatch makes material margin look better than true material cost would suggest.

Constructing a pseudo-COGS

To compare with function-format peers, or to get a true gross margin for pricing decisions, build:

\[ \text{Pseudo-COGS} = \text{COGS}_{\text{Ind}} + \text{Manufacturing employee cost} + \text{Power and fuel} + \text{Stores and spares} + \text{Plant D\&A} + \text{Other manufacturing expenses} \]

Sources for the split:

  • Notes to "Other expenses": power and fuel, stores and spares, repairs to plant, job-work charges and freight are usually separately disclosed.
  • Segment and plant data in investor presentations (cost per tonne for cement and steel).
  • Cost audit records: companies covered by the Companies (Cost Records and Audit) Rules, 2014 maintain cost records (Form CRA-1); the cost audit report itself is not public, but the CMA background makes the cost-sheet logic natural.
  • Employee cost split is rarely disclosed; use a documented assumption (e.g., 40% manufacturing for a paint company with own plants) and sensitivity-test it.

Line Company X (own plants) Company Y (60% contract-manufactured)

Y buys finished product from contract manufacturers, so the manufacturer's labour, power and margin all land in "purchases of stock-in-trade". X's reported gross margin advantage of 1,000 bps shrinks to 400 bps on a full-cost basis.

Why paints and FMCG show 40%+ "gross margins"

Indian paint companies typically report material margins in the high-30s to mid-40s. A large part of that headroom is consumed by freight, A&P, dealer incentives and employee cost below the line. When input costs (TiO2, crude-linked monomers, solvents) spike, the material margin is the first and most sensitive indicator — which is useful — but it overstates the profit cushion against price competition.

Model implementation

In the ratio engine, keep three gross-margin definitions side by side and label them:

  1. Material margin (Schedule III arithmetic, used for Indian peer comparison).
  2. Material + direct manufacturing margin (adds power and fuel, stores, job-work).
  3. Full-cost gross margin (adds allocated wages and plant D&A — assumption-driven).

Use named ranges so that the formula reads like the definition and survives row insertions.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Traded goods share. A company that shifts from manufacturing to trading (purchases of stock-in-trade) sees material margin fall while EBITDA margin may hold — mix effect, not pricing weakness.
  • Inventory valuation effects. Large inventory build-ups at absorption cost credit the ΔInventory line with conversion costs; read material margin alongside the inventory change.
  • Freight classification. Some companies include inbound freight in materials, others in other expenses; normalise for cement and paints where freight is 6–25% of revenue.
  • Captive power. Companies with captive power plants show coal in materials or in power and fuel — inconsistent across peers.
  • 20-F filers. Infosys and Wipro present function-wise under IFRS in their SEC filings and nature-wise in Ind AS statements — a ready-made reconciliation of the two formats.
  • Normal capacity. Under Ind AS 2, unallocated fixed overheads in low-utilisation years are expensed, not inventoried — another reason year-on-year material margin and full-cost margin diverge.

Key takeaways

  • Schedule III's nature-wise P&L means Indian "gross margin" is effectively a material margin.
  • Conversion costs (wages, power, plant depreciation) sit in other lines, so Indian gross margins look structurally higher than IFRS/US cost-of-sales margins.
  • Outsourcing shifts conversion cost into purchases, distorting peer comparison within India too.
  • Build a documented pseudo-COGS from notes to other expenses when comparing across formats or business models.
  • Material margin is the best early indicator of input inflation; it is a poor indicator of pricing headroom.

Practice Questions

Q1. Pseudo-COGS from notes [L2]

A paint company reports (₹ crore): revenue from operations 8,200; cost of materials consumed 4,100; purchases of stock-in-trade 520; changes in inventories 60 (a decrease); employee benefits 640, of which management indicates 35% relates to plants; other expenses 1,750 including power and fuel 95, stores and spares 40, job-work charges 110, repairs to plant 30 and freight outward 410; depreciation 260 of which 70% relates to plant and machinery and factory buildings. Compute material margin and full-cost gross margin.

Q2. Cross-border comparison [L2]

A global coatings company filing under US GAAP reports gross margin of 46% on a cost-of-sales basis. An Indian peer reports material margin of 43%. Explain why the Indian peer's comparable full-cost gross margin is likely much lower, list the data you would extract to quantify it, and state the main assumption that would drive your estimate.

💡 Hint: Think about which Indian P&L lines contain the conversion costs that the US GAAP filer has already deducted.

Q3. Mix shift diagnosis [L3]

Over three years a consumer durables company's purchases of stock-in-trade rose from 15% to 45% of total material cost as it closed two plants and moved to contract manufacturing. Material margin fell 600 bps while EBITDA margin rose 80 bps. Draft a short memo explaining whether the business has lost pricing power, and propose two KPIs that would separate mix from pricing effects.

Q4. Inventory build and absorption [L2]

In FY2024-25 a manufacturer increased finished goods inventory by ₹300 crore (at absorption cost, of which ₹90 crore is conversion cost). Explain the direction of the effect on reported material margin and on EBITDA margin compared with a year of no inventory change, and how you would adjust when comparing the two years.
