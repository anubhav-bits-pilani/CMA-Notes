Subtopic note

Denominator Design: Averages, Cash, Goodwill and Lease Liabilities

Scope: Average vs closing balances; treatment of cash, goodwill and lease liabilities in denominators

Why it matters

The numerator of a return ratio is a flow earned over twelve months; the denominator is a stock measured at a point in time. How you convert the stock into a period-representative base — and what you put into it — often moves ROCE or ROIC by more than a year of operating improvement. Four choices dominate: average versus closing balances, inclusion of surplus cash, inclusion of goodwill and acquired intangibles, and treatment of Ind AS 116 lease liabilities and ROU assets. Each must be made once, documented in the ratio dictionary and applied to every peer and every year.

Core notes

Average versus closing balances

\[ \overline{X}_t = \frac{X_{t-1} + X_t}{2} \]

Use averages by default. Profit for FY2024-25 was earned on capital that existed throughout the year, not only on 31 March 2025. For a company growing capital at 20% a year, closing balances understate returns by roughly 10%.

Refinements:

  • Three-point or five-point averages. Listed companies publish half-yearly balance sheets under SEBI LODR Reg 33(3)(f) (statement of assets and liabilities as at 30 September). A three-point average (31 March prior year, 30 September, 31 March current year) captures intra-year capital deployment and seasonality better than two points.
  • Opening balances are preferred by some practitioners for incremental-return analysis ("what did last year's capital earn?") and for EVA capital charges.
  • Closing balances are defensible only when (a) the first year of data has no opening balance, (b) the analysis is forward-looking (return on today's capital at run-rate profit), or (c) a massive event late in the year (acquisition on 25 March) would make an average meaningless — in which case pro-forma the profit instead.

Large equity events. A ₹5,000 crore QIP in month 11 or a buyback in month 2 distorts a simple two-point average. Use a time-weighted average:

\[ \overline{E} = E_{\text{open}} + \sum_i \text{Issue}_i \times \frac{m_i}{12} - \sum_j \text{Buyback}_j \times \frac{n_j}{12} + \text{Retained profit} \times \tfrac{1}{2} \]

where \(m_i\) and \(n_j\) are the months remaining in the year after each event.

Treatment of cash

Cash-rich Indian companies (paint majors, IT services, FMCG, some pharma) carry cash, bank deposits and liquid/debt mutual funds amounting to 20–50% of capital employed. The rule is symmetry:

Choice Numerator Denominator Measures
Inclusive ROCE EBIT + other income (treasury income) Equity + debt (incl. cash) Return on all capital the board chooses to retain
Operating ROCE / ROIC EBIT excluding treasury income Capital employed − surplus cash and liquid investments Return of the operating business

Distinguish operating cash (working balances needed to run the business — commonly assumed at 1–2% of revenue, or the minimum month-end balance) from surplus cash. Earmarked balances (unpaid dividend accounts, margin money, escrow) are neither surplus nor free: Week 5 excluded them from free cash; in ROIC they stay with the operating or restricted bucket. Bank deposits over 12 months shown in "other financial assets" and treasury mutual funds in "current investments" are surplus cash despite their Schedule III captions.

Treatment of goodwill and acquired intangibles

Goodwill arises only in business combinations (Ind AS 103) and is not amortised but tested for impairment annually (Ind AS 36). Two views:

  • ROIC including goodwill — return on the price paid for acquisitions; the right measure for judging capital allocation and for EVA.
  • ROIC excluding goodwill — return on tangible operating capital; the right measure for comparing an acquisitive company with an organic peer, and for judging the economics of the underlying operations.

Report both. Also consider whether acquired customer relationships and brands (amortised) should be treated like goodwill: their amortisation depresses EBIT while internally generated brands of organic peers are expensed through A&P and never capitalised (Ind AS 38.63). Many analysts add back acquired-intangible amortisation (net of tax) to NOPAT and keep the gross intangible in capital.

Note that Ind AS 101 allowed companies to carry forward Indian GAAP goodwill, and some Indian GAAP amalgamations under the pooling method created no goodwill at all — so goodwill balances are not comparable across groups.

Lease liabilities and ROU assets (Ind AS 116)

Post-Ind AS 116, the balance sheet carries ROU assets and lease liabilities, and the P&L shows depreciation on ROU plus interest on the lease liability instead of rent. Consistency rules:

If you… Then numerator should be… And denominator should include…
Treat leases as debt (post-Ind AS 116 basis) EBIT after ROU depreciation, before lease interest ROU assets in invested capital; lease liabilities in capital employed
Treat leases as operating costs (pre-Ind AS 116, rent-adjusted) EBIT after full rent (EBITDAR − rent − D&A on owned assets) No ROU, no lease liabilities

Never mix: excluding lease liabilities from capital employed while using post-Ind AS 116 EBIT (which excludes the lease interest component of rent) overstates ROCE. For retailers, quick service restaurants and multiplexes, where lease liabilities can exceed financial debt several times, the choice moves ROCE by 5–15 percentage points.

Illustration (illustrative retailer, ₹ crore)

Item Opening Closing Average
Equity 2,000 2,600 2,300
Borrowings 400 400 400
Lease liabilities 3,000 3,600 3,300
Surplus cash 300 500 400

EBIT (post-Ind AS 116, lease interest excluded) = 900; lease interest = 300; rent paid = 750; ROU depreciation = 480.

  • ROCE excluding leases from capital, using post-116 EBIT: 900 ÷ (2,300 + 400 − 400) = 39.1% — inconsistent and overstated.
  • ROCE including leases: 900 ÷ (2,300 + 400 + 3,300 − 400) = 16.1%.
  • Rent-adjusted: EBIT pre-116 = 900 − (750 − 480) = 630; ROCE = 630 ÷ 2,300 = 27.4%.

The two consistent answers (16.1% and 27.4%) differ because they answer different questions (lease-as-debt vs lease-as-cost); the inconsistent 39.1% answers none.

Model implementation

Define each denominator as a separate named block in the ratio dictionary tab, with switches:

=IF(IncludeCash=1,AvgCapitalEmployed,AvgCapitalEmployed-AvgSurplusCash)

In DAX, compute averages with a measure that takes the current and previous fiscal year-end balances:

Avg Equity :=
VAR CurrYE = CALCULATE ( [Equity Closing], LASTDATE ( 'Date'[Date] ) )
VAR PrevYE = CALCULATE ( [Equity Closing], DATEADD ( LASTDATE ( 'Date'[Date] ), -1, YEAR ) )
RETURN DIVIDE ( CurrYE + PrevYE, 2 )

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • First year in the dataset: no opening balance — either pull one more year or flag the year as closing-based.
  • Acquisitions mid-year: average balance sheet includes half of the acquiree's capital but the P&L includes only post-acquisition months; pro-forma the numerator or time-weight the denominator.
  • Cash trapped abroad or in regulated subsidiaries: not freely surplus; consider keeping it in capital.
  • Negative operating invested capital (FMCG with strong negative working capital): ROIC becomes very large or undefined; analyse returns on fixed capital instead.
  • Short-term leases and low-value leases (Ind AS 116.5) stay in rent expense even post-Ind AS 116; adjust only if material.
  • Impaired goodwill: past impairments lower the base and flatter future ROIC; add back cumulative impairments for a "gross" capital view.

Key takeaways

  • Use average balances by default; time-weight for large QIPs, buybacks and acquisitions; use Reg 33 half-yearly balance sheets for three-point averages.
  • Treat cash symmetrically: treasury income in the numerator only if surplus cash is in the denominator.
  • Report ROIC with and without goodwill; consider adding back acquired-intangible amortisation.
  • Lease liabilities in the denominator if and only if EBIT is post-Ind AS 116 (rent replaced by depreciation and interest).
  • Document every choice in the ratio dictionary and apply it across peers and years.

Practice Questions

Q1. Time-weighted equity [L2]

A company's owners' equity on 1 April 2024 is ₹6,000 crore. It completes a QIP of ₹2,400 crore on 1 February 2025 and a buyback of ₹600 crore on 1 June 2024. PAT for the year is ₹900 crore and dividends of ₹300 crore are paid on 1 August 2024. Closing equity is ₹8,400 crore. Compute ROE on closing equity, simple average equity and time-weighted average equity, and comment on which is defensible.

💡 Hint: Weight each equity movement by the fraction of the year it was in place; treat profit as accruing evenly.

Q2. Cash symmetry [L2]

An IT services company reports EBIT (excl. other income) ₹9,800 crore, other income ₹1,450 crore, average equity ₹38,000 crore, no debt, average lease liabilities ₹1,800 crore, average cash, deposits and liquid investments ₹21,000 crore. Assuming operating cash of 2% of revenue of ₹60,000 crore, compute inclusive ROCE and operating ROCE (with leases as debt). Explain what each number tells a shareholder.

Q3. Goodwill-heavy acquirer [L3]

Company A has grown through acquisitions: average goodwill ₹4,000 crore and acquired intangibles ₹1,500 crore (amortisation ₹200 crore a year), other invested capital ₹6,000 crore, EBIT ₹1,800 crore, tax rate 25.168%. Company B is organic with invested capital ₹6,500 crore and EBIT ₹1,650 crore. Construct a comparison table of ROIC with and without goodwill and with amortisation added back, and write a conclusion on which management team has allocated capital better.

Q4. Lease consistency audit [L2]

You are reviewing a junior's peer table for multiplex operators. One company's ROCE is computed with lease liabilities in capital employed; another's excludes them; both use reported post-Ind AS 116 EBIT. Describe the review checks you would perform, the corrections required, and how you would present the corrected comparison.
