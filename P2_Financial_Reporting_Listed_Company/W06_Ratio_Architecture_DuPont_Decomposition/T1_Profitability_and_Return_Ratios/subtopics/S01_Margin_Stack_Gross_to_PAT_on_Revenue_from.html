Margin Stack: Gross to PAT on Revenue from Operations

Scope: Margin stack: gross, EBITDA, EBIT, PBT, PAT margins on revenue from operations

Why it matters

The margin stack is the first page of every equity research note, credit appraisal memo and FP&A monthly pack. It tells you where in the P&L a rupee of revenue is consumed: by materials, by people and overheads, by the asset base (depreciation), by lenders (finance cost) and by the tax authority. An analyst who can read the stack can tell, in one glance, whether a fall in PAT margin came from input inflation (gross margin), operating cost creep (EBITDA margin), a capex cycle (EBIT margin), a debt-funded expansion (PBT margin) or a tax change (PAT margin). In DuPont analysis (topic t2) the same stack becomes the tax burden, interest burden and EBIT margin components, so a consistently built stack is a precondition for everything else this week.

Core notes

The denominator: revenue from operations, not total income

Schedule III Division II presents "Revenue from operations" and "Other income" separately, then sums them into "Total income". Every margin in the stack should use revenue from operations as the denominator because:

  • Other income (interest on deposits, dividend, FVTPL gains on liquid mutual funds, profit on sale of PPE) is not generated by the operating asset base and is not repeatable in the same way.
  • Total income varies with treasury size, so two companies with identical operations show different "margins" merely because one holds more cash.
  • Revenue from operations already includes "other operating revenue" (scrap sales, export incentives, service income) — decide, as in Week 5, whether these are core and document the choice.

Revenue from operations under Ind AS 115 is net of GST (GST is collected on behalf of the government, Ind AS 115.47), net of trade discounts, volume rebates and other variable consideration. Pre-July 2017 revenue was excise-inclusive under Indian GAAP and early Ind AS years — a structural break when you index margins over long periods.

The five layers

Layer Numerator (Schedule III Div II nature-wise P&L) What it measures
Gross margin Revenue from operations − (Cost of materials consumed + Purchases of stock-in-trade + Changes in inventories of FG, WIP and stock-in-trade) Pricing power over raw material; product mix
EBITDA margin Gross profit − Employee benefits expense − Other expenses Operating cost structure before capital intensity
EBIT margin EBITDA − Depreciation and amortisation Return on sales after consuming fixed assets
PBT margin EBIT − Finance costs (+ other income, if excluded above) ± exceptional items Effect of financing choices and non-operating income
PAT margin PBT − Tax expense (current + deferred) Bottom-line conversion of revenue for owners

Two definitional choices must be made explicitly and applied to every peer:

  1. Where other income sits. The "operating" convention computes EBITDA and EBIT excluding other income and adds it back between EBIT and PBT. The "reported" convention used by many Indian companies in investor presentations computes EBITDA including other income. For a cash-rich paint company the difference can be 150–300 basis points of margin. Pick one, label it, and reconcile to the company's own reported EBITDA.
  2. Exceptional items. Schedule III Div II shows exceptional items as a separate line before tax. For a normalised stack, exclude them from EBITDA/EBIT and show reported PBT and "PBT before exceptional items" separately.

Formulas

\[ \text{Gross margin} = \frac{\text{Revenue from operations} - \text{COGS}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \]

\[ \text{EBITDA margin} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Revenue}}, \quad \text{EBIT margin} = \frac{\text{EBITDA} - \text{D\&A}}{\text{Revenue}} \]

\[ \text{PBT margin} = \frac{\text{PBT}}{\text{Revenue}}, \quad \text{PAT margin} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Revenue}} \]

In consolidated statements, use PAT attributable to owners of the parent for the owners' PAT margin and show total PAT margin separately if NCI is material.

Worked illustration (illustrative paint company, ₹ crore, FY2024-25)

Note that PBT margin (17.9%) exceeds EBIT margin (16.0%) because other income exceeds finance cost — the same effect that pushes the DuPont interest burden above 1 in topic t2.

Reading the stack: the "margin waterfall"

The FP&A use of the stack is the year-on-year waterfall: start with last year's PAT margin and walk each layer's basis-point change. A 300 bps fall in gross margin partly offset by 100 bps of lower A&P spend tells a different story from a uniform 200 bps fall everywhere. In Excel, a margin table with each line divided by revenue and a "bps change" column (=(C5-B5)*10000) is the standard build; in Power BI, a DAX measure such as

EBITDA Margin % = DIVIDE ( [EBITDA], [Revenue from Operations] )

with [EBITDA] itself defined as a measure (not a calculated column) keeps definitions identical across companies and periods.

Ind AS 116 and the stack

Since FY2019-20, lease rent for most leases is replaced by depreciation on the right-of-use asset and interest on the lease liability. EBITDA margin rises (rent no longer in other expenses), EBIT margin rises slightly (depreciation on ROU is usually below the rent it replaced in early years), PBT margin is broadly neutral to slightly lower in early lease years (front-loaded interest). For retailers this can add 600–1,000 bps to EBITDA margin. Compare peers on the same basis — either all post-Ind AS 116 or all rent-adjusted ("pre-Ind AS 116") EBITDA, as built in Week 5.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Other operating revenue that is really other income. Some companies classify export incentives or scrap within revenue from operations, others in other income. Reclassify to a common basis before comparing.
  • Discounts shown as expenses. Cash discounts and dealer schemes that are variable consideration under Ind AS 115 must reduce revenue; if a peer shows "scheme expenses" inside other expenses, gross margin is overstated and revenue inflated. Look at the revenue note and accounting policy.
  • Excise and GST breaks. Margins computed on excise-inclusive revenue (pre-July 2017) are mechanically lower; restate or flag.
  • Capitalised costs. Companies in a capex cycle capitalise employee and borrowing costs (Ind AS 16, Ind AS 23), lifting EBITDA margin temporarily.
  • Negative PBT with positive tax. When deferred tax remeasurement or MAT credit write-off produces tax on a loss, PAT margin is not interpretable as "tax at rate"; present it with commentary.
  • Share of associates/JVs. In consolidated P&L this sits near PBT; exclude it from EBIT margin (no revenue behind it) but keep it in PAT.

Key takeaways

  • Always use revenue from operations (net of GST and variable consideration) as the margin denominator.
  • Decide and document where other income and exceptional items sit; reconcile to the company's own reported EBITDA.
  • Each layer isolates one cost family: materials, operating costs, capital intensity, financing, tax.
  • PBT margin can exceed EBIT margin for net-cash companies — a feature, not an error.
  • Ind AS 116 lifts EBITDA margin; compare peers on a common lease basis.
  • Build margins as measures, not hard-coded numbers, so the definition is identical across peers and years.

Practice Questions

Q1. Build the stack [L1]

A cement company reports (₹ crore, FY2024-25): revenue from operations 18,400; other income 220; cost of materials consumed 2,950; purchases of stock-in-trade 410; changes in inventories (40) (i.e., an increase in inventory of 40); employee benefits 1,050; power and fuel 4,600; freight and forwarding 4,250; other expenses 1,900; depreciation 1,150; finance costs 480; exceptional item: impairment of a grinding unit 300; tax expense 820. Compute gross, EBITDA, EBIT, PBT (before and after exceptional item) and PAT margins on the operating convention (other income below EBIT).

💡 Hint: Remember the sign of the inventory change line and that power and fuel and freight sit in the EBITDA layer, not gross margin.

Q2. Reconcile to management's EBITDA [L2]

The same company's investor presentation reports EBITDA of ₹4,230 crore. Identify the most likely definitional differences between your EBITDA from Q1 and management's figure, and set out a reconciliation table that would make the two agree.

Q3. Margin waterfall [L2]

A FMCG company's margins moved as follows from FY2023-24 to FY2024-25: gross margin 51.2% to 48.9%; employee cost 8.1% to 7.6%; A&P 11.4% to 9.8%; other expenses 12.0% to 12.3%; D&A 2.1% to 2.3%; finance cost 0.3% to 0.3%; other income 1.6% to 1.9%; effective tax rate on PBT 25.5% in both years. Prepare a basis-point waterfall from FY2023-24 PAT margin to FY2024-25 PAT margin and write a three-sentence commentary for the CFO.

Q4. Lease basis comparability [L3]

Retailer A (post-Ind AS 116) reports EBITDA margin of 14.5%; its lease note shows principal plus interest paid on leases of 6.8% of revenue. Retailer B franchises 70% of its stores and reports EBITDA margin of 9.2% with lease payments of 1.4% of revenue. Explain which retailer has better store-level economics and design the comparison table you would put in an IC memo.

💡 Hint: Convert both to a rent-adjusted EBITDA before judging, then think about what franchise income does to the margin denominator.

Q5. Interview: which margin matters [L1]

An interviewer asks: "For a paint company facing a new entrant offering 20% higher dealer margins, which line of the margin stack would you watch first, and why might gross margin mislead you?" Prepare a two-minute answer.
