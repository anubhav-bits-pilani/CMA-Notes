Key concept

Gross, EBITDA and PAT Margin Formulas

Concept: Gross margin = (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue; EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue; PAT margin = PAT ÷ Revenue

Statement

\[ \text{Gross margin} = \frac{R - \text{COGS}}{R}, \qquad \text{EBITDA margin} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{R}, \qquad \text{PAT margin} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{R} \]

where:

  • \(R\) = revenue from operations (Schedule III Div II), net of GST and variable consideration under Ind AS 115; excludes other income.
  • \(\text{COGS}\) = cost of materials consumed + purchases of stock-in-trade + changes in inventories of finished goods, work-in-progress and stock-in-trade (Indian nature-wise definition).
  • \(\text{EBITDA}\) = profit before exceptional items and tax + finance costs + depreciation and amortisation − other income (operating convention), i.e., \(R - \text{COGS} - \text{employee benefits} - \text{other expenses}\).
  • \(\text{PAT}\) = profit for the year (consolidated: attributable to owners of the parent when computing owners' PAT margin).

Explanation

The three margins sample the P&L at three depths. Gross margin isolates the spread between selling price and material cost; EBITDA margin adds the operating cost structure (people, freight, advertising, power, royalty); PAT margin captures everything, including capital intensity, financing and tax.

Conditions for comparability:

  1. Same revenue denominator (revenue from operations, not total income).
  2. Same COGS definition — in India this is a material margin because conversion costs sit in other lines (subtopic s02). An IFRS or US GAAP cost-of-sales gross margin is not comparable.
  3. Same EBITDA convention on other income and exceptional items. Ind AS does not define EBITDA; SEBI's ICDR regulations require KPIs used in offer documents to be defined, and many listed companies show EBITDA including other income in presentations. Reconcile.
  4. Same lease basis — post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA excludes most rent; compare with rent-adjusted EBITDA where peers differ in lease intensity.

Exceptions and interpretation notes:

  • A company with large other income can show PAT margin close to or above EBITDA margin minus D&A — a sign that treasury income is material.
  • Negative changes in inventories (i.e., inventory build-up) reduce COGS; inventory is valued at absorption cost (Ind AS 2), so this line carries conversion cost into the material margin.
  • In consolidated accounts, total PAT margin includes NCI share; owners' PAT margin is the one consistent with ROE.

Illustration

Illustrative FY2024-25 data for a mid-sized paint company (₹ crore):

Line Amount
Revenue from operations 11,200
Cost of materials consumed 6,050
Purchases of stock-in-trade 640
Changes in inventories (90)
Employee benefits 820
Other expenses 1,960
Depreciation 380
Finance costs 70
Other income 210
Tax expense 535

COGS = 6,050 + 640 − 90 = 6,600; gross margin = (11,200 − 6,600) ÷ 11,200 = 41.1%. EBITDA = 4,600 − 820 − 1,960 = 1,820; EBITDA margin = 16.3%. PBT = 1,820 − 380 − 70 + 210 = 1,580; PAT = 1,045; PAT margin = 9.3%.

If the company reports EBITDA including other income (2,030), its "EBITDA margin" becomes 18.1% — 190 bps higher purely by definition.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute three margins [L1]

A QSR chain reports (₹ crore): revenue from operations 2,450; cost of materials consumed 760; employee benefits 410; other expenses 650 (of which short-term lease rent 40); depreciation (incl. ROU) 330; finance costs (incl. lease interest 150) 170; other income 35; tax 30. Compute gross, EBITDA and PAT margins on the operating convention.

Q2. Definitions drive the margin [L2]

Two FMCG peers report EBITDA margins of 22.0% and 20.5%. Peer 1 includes other income of 2.1% of revenue in EBITDA and classifies trade-scheme payouts as other expenses (3.0% of gross revenue); Peer 2 excludes other income and nets schemes off revenue. Restate both on a common basis and comment on which has the stronger operating margin.

💡 Hint: Restate revenue first for the schemes, then restate EBITDA; both numerator and denominator change for Peer 1.

Q3. Interview: why not total income [L1]

Explain in three sentences why margins should be computed on revenue from operations rather than total income, using a cash-rich IT services company as the example.
