Subtopic note

New Plant Ramp-Up in Capacity-Driven Businesses

Scope: Capacity-driven businesses: new plant ramp-up dragging turnover (paints, cement)

Why it matters

Paints and cement in India are textbook capacity-driven industries: growth comes in lumps when a new plant or a grinding unit is commissioned, while demand grows smoothly. Between commissioning and full utilisation, the asset base is fully on the balance sheet, depreciation and interest start immediately (capitalisation ends the Ind AS 23 borrowing-cost window), but revenue arrives gradually. Fixed asset turnover, asset turnover, ROCE and EBIT margin all dip — sometimes for two to three years. The analyst's job is to distinguish a temporary ramp-up drag from a structural fall in productivity; the FP&A analyst's job is to forecast the ramp curve credibly and explain the dip to the board. The Week 6 exercise peers — Asian Paints, Berger, Kansai Nerolac — together with the entry of Birla Opus (Grasim) since 2024, illustrate how industry-wide capacity additions also compress utilisation for incumbents.

Core notes

Anatomy of a ramp-up

  1. Construction (CWIP): capital committed, no revenue. CWIP-inclusive turnover falls (s01).
  2. Commissioning: transfer from CWIP to PPE; depreciation and interest expensing start; fixed costs (staff, power contracts, maintenance) begin.
  3. Ramp-up: utilisation climbs from 30–50% in year 1 to 70–85% by years 2–3 (cement) or with distribution build-out (paints). Fixed cost absorption is low, so EBITDA margin of the new plant is below the mature fleet.
  4. Steady state: turnover and returns converge toward the mature-plant level, or below it if capital cost per unit was higher (inflation, greenfield land costs).

Measuring the drag

Use capacity and volume data from investor presentations alongside the ratios:

\[ \text{Capacity utilisation} = \frac{\text{Production (or sales) volume}}{\text{Installed capacity}} \]

\[ \text{Revenue per unit of capacity} = \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average installed capacity}} \]

Decompose fixed asset turnover into utilisation and price/capital-intensity effects:

\[ \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Net PPE}} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Volume}}{\text{Capacity}}}_{\text{utilisation}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Volume}}}_{\text{realisation}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Capacity}}{\text{Net PPE}}}_{\text{capacity per rupee of assets}} \]

A falling FAT driven by the utilisation term is a ramp-up issue; a fall in capacity per rupee of assets reflects higher capital cost per tonne/KL (e.g., greenfield vs brownfield, or newer, more automated plants); a fall in realisation reflects pricing.

Time-weighting capacity

Capacity commissioned in February contributes one or two months of output. Use effective capacity = opening capacity + new capacity × months operational ÷ 12. Utilisation on nameplate year-end capacity understates the true operating rate.

Cement specifics

  • Capacity in MTPA (million tonnes per annum); clinker and grinding capacity separate — grinding units near markets ramp faster than integrated plants.
  • Capital cost per tonne: indicatively US$60–80 per tonne (roughly ₹500–650 crore per MTPA) for brownfield expansion and US$100–120 per tonne (roughly ₹850–1,000 crore per MTPA) for greenfield integrated capacity (varies with location, vintage and captive power; verify against current company disclosures). Newer capacity has lower FAT than legacy plants built at far lower cost.
  • EBITDA per tonne and cost per tonne (power, fuel, freight) are tracked alongside (t6 s04).

Paints specifics

  • Capacity in KL per annum (decorative) and tonnes (industrial); plants are less of a constraint than distribution — tinting machines and dealer network drive volume (t6 s05).
  • Asian Paints' Khandala, Mysuru and Visakhapatnam plants and Berger's Sandila plant (Uttar Pradesh) are examples of large capacity additions that temporarily lowered turnover.
  • New entrant capacity (Birla Opus's six plants targeting about 1,332 million litres per annum, per Grasim's disclosures) raises industry capacity faster than demand, pressuring incumbents' utilisation and pricing — a sector-level drag on turnover and margins.

Worked illustration

Paint company, ₹ crore:

Item FY2023-24 FY2024-25
Revenue 10,000 10,600
Average net PPE 3,000 4,400
Installed capacity (lakh KL), average effective 10.0 13.0
Volume (lakh KL) 7.5 8.1

FAT falls from 3.33x to 2.41x. Decomposition: utilisation 75% → 62.3%; realisation ₹1,333 → ₹1,309 crore per lakh KL; capacity per ₹ crore of net PPE 0.00333 → 0.00295 lakh KL. Most of the decline is utilisation (new plant) with a smaller contribution from higher capital cost per KL and slight price softness.

Forecasting the ramp in a model

  • Model capacity, utilisation curve (e.g., 40%/65%/80% years 1–3) and realisation; revenue = capacity × utilisation × realisation.
  • Fixed costs step up at commissioning; variable costs scale with volume.
  • Depreciation from the commissioning date per Schedule II useful life (s06).
  • Check that implied FAT converges to a defensible mature level; flag years where it falls below historical troughs.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Trial run revenue: the 2020 amendment to Ind AS 16 (notified in India for periods from 1 April 2022) prohibits deducting sale proceeds of items produced before the asset is ready for use from cost — they are recognised in P&L. Ramp-up revenue may therefore appear before formal commissioning.
  • Delayed commissioning: CWIP ageing over one to two years signals cost overruns; Schedule III requires a completion schedule for overdue projects.
  • Industry vs company utilisation: a company running at 85% while the industry runs at 65% has pricing power; the reverse implies price competition.
  • Demand shocks (monsoon, real-estate slowdown) overlapping a ramp-up confuse attribution — use peer volume growth as a control.
  • Acquired capacity (cement M&A) arrives with existing utilisation; separate inorganic from greenfield effects.

Key takeaways

  • Ramp-up years depress FAT, ROCE and EBIT margin because assets and fixed costs arrive before volume.
  • Decompose FAT into utilisation × realisation × capacity per rupee of assets to diagnose the cause.
  • Use effective (time-weighted) capacity for utilisation when plants commission mid-year.
  • Newer capacity costs more per unit; mature FAT for new plants may never match legacy plants.
  • New entrants' capacity (e.g., Birla Opus in paints) can depress incumbents' utilisation industry-wide.

Practice Questions

Q1. Effective capacity and utilisation [L1]

A cement company had 30 MTPA capacity on 1 April 2024 and commissioned 6 MTPA on 1 December 2024. FY2024-25 sales volume was 26.4 MT. Compute utilisation on nameplate year-end capacity and on effective capacity.

Q2. Decomposing a turnover decline [L2]

Data: FY2023-24 revenue ₹18,000 crore, volume 28 MT, effective capacity 35 MTPA, average net PPE ₹16,000 crore; FY2024-25 revenue ₹19,500 crore, volume 31 MT, effective capacity 42 MTPA, average net PPE ₹21,500 crore. Decompose the change in FAT into utilisation, realisation and capacity-per-asset effects and comment.

💡 Hint: Compute each of the three factors for both years and compare their ratios.

Q3. Ramp-up forecast [L3]

Build a five-year forecast specification for a ₹1,000 crore paint plant of 2 lakh KL commissioned on 1 October 2025: utilisation 35%, 60%, 75%, 85%, 85%; realisation ₹180 per litre with 3% annual growth; fixed costs ₹60 crore a year from commissioning; variable cost 58% of revenue; straight-line depreciation over 25 years. Specify the schedule layout and the FAT and EBIT margin checks you would include.

Q4. Competitive capacity and incumbent turnover [L2]

Industry decorative paint capacity rises 30% over two years due to a new entrant, while demand grows 8% a year. Explain the likely effects on an incumbent's utilisation, realisation and FAT, and list the investor-presentation data you would track quarterly.
