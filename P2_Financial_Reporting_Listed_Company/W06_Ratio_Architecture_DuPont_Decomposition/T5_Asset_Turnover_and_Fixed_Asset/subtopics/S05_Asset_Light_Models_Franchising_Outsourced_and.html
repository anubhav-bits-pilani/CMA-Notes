Subtopic note

Asset-Light Models: Franchising, Outsourced and Contract Manufacturing

Scope: Asset-light models: franchise stores (FOFO), outsourced manufacturing, contract manufacturing

Why it matters

Indian consumer companies increasingly grow by moving assets and working capital onto partners' balance sheets: franchise-owned stores in jewellery, eyewear and footwear; outsourced manufacturing for D2C and beauty brands; contract manufacturing for FMCG and electronics. Asset-light models raise fixed asset turnover and ROCE, often earn a valuation premium, and can reduce working capital — but they give away margin, shift (not eliminate) risk, and can hide commitments that act like debt. An analyst who compares an asset-light brand with an integrated manufacturer on turnover alone will reach the wrong conclusion; an FP&A team evaluating a COCO-to-FOFO shift needs to model the trade-off between margin and capital.

Core notes

Store ownership models

Model Store capex Store opex and rent Inventory Brand's revenue Typical examples
COCO (company-owned, company-operated) Brand Brand Brand Retail sales to consumer Large-format flagships; many apparel retailers
FOFO (franchise-owned, franchise-operated) Franchisee Franchisee Franchisee (or brand on consignment) Wholesale sale to franchisee + royalty/fees Tanishq franchise stores, Lenskart and footwear franchise networks
FOCO (franchise-owned, company-operated) Franchisee Brand operates; franchisee bears rent/capex Brand Retail sales; franchisee earns a revenue share Common in eyewear and QSR
COFO Brand Franchisee Varies Varies Less common

Effect of FOFO on ratios: store capex and ROU assets disappear from the brand's balance sheet → higher FAT and total asset turnover. Revenue per store is recognised at wholesale (lower) prices → lower revenue and gross margin per unit, but also no store opex → EBITDA margin may be similar or lower. Inventory may move to the franchisee (lower DIO) or remain with the brand on consignment/sale-or-return terms (inventory stays on the brand's books under Ind AS 115 — control has not passed). Receivables from franchisees appear (higher DSO).

Accounting points

  • Revenue (Ind AS 115): sale of goods to franchisees is recognised when control transfers. Sale-or-return arrangements require a refund liability and a right-to-recover asset; consignment means no sale until the franchisee sells to the end customer. Franchise fees and royalties: initial fees are assessed as part of the licence performance obligation; sales-based royalties on intellectual property licences are recognised as the underlying sales occur (Ind AS 115 sales-based royalty exception).
  • Leases (Ind AS 116): none for FOFO stores (franchisee is the lessee). In FOCO, check whether the brand controls the use of the store (possible embedded lease).
  • Gold on loan in jewellery franchising: gold supplied to franchisees may be on loan, retained on the brand's balance sheet (t6 s02).

Outsourced manufacturing

Brand designs, markets and distributes; third-party manufacturers produce (D2C personal care, apparel, consumer electronics brands). Effects: near-zero manufacturing PPE → very high FAT; COGS includes the manufacturer's margin → lower gross margin; flexibility to scale; quality and supply concentration risk.

Contract manufacturing

The mirror image: a company manufactures for brand owners (EMS players, pharma CDMOs, FMCG contract packers). Its own ratios show high asset turnover in assembly-type EMS (thin margins, high volume) or lower turnover in capital-intensive CDMO. Two accounting traps:

  • Embedded leases: a contract that gives the brand owner the right to substantially all output of a dedicated line, and to direct its use, may contain a lease of that line under Ind AS 116 — on the brand owner's books a ROU asset and lease liability, on the manufacturer's a lease receivable or operating lease.
  • Principal vs agent: if the brand supplies key materials and the manufacturer does not control them, the manufacturer may recognise only the conversion fee (job-work) as revenue — sharply raising its reported margins and lowering revenue relative to peers that buy materials.

Worked illustration: COCO vs FOFO

Jewellery retailer, 100 stores, per-store annual figures (₹ crore):

Item COCO FOFO
Revenue 40.0 (retail) 35.2 (wholesale at 12% discount)
Cost of goods (same product) 30.4 30.4
Gross margin 9.6 (24.0%) 4.8 (13.6%)
Store opex incl. rent 4.0 0
Royalty income 0 0.4
Store EBITDA contribution 5.6 5.2
Capital employed (fit-out, ROU, inventory) 30.0 6.0 (receivables, working inventory)
Pre-tax return on capital 18.7% 86.7%

FOFO lowers EBITDA per store by about 7% but raises return on capital more than four-fold — the economics that justify a valuation premium, provided franchisee viability and receivables quality hold.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Hidden commitments: minimum purchase obligations, take-or-pay clauses with contract manufacturers, guarantees given to franchisees' lenders, inventory buy-back undertakings. Read contingent liabilities and commitments notes; rating agencies may add guarantees to debt.
  • Franchisee financing support: brands that fund franchisee fit-outs via deposits or loans are less asset-light than they appear — check loans and advances notes.
  • Mixed networks: most brands run COCO and FOFO; segment-level store counts in investor presentations are needed to normalise turnover.
  • Channel stuffing risk is higher where franchisees are customers — watch receivables growth versus revenue.
  • Related-party franchisees (promoter-owned franchises) require Ind AS 24 disclosure and arm's-length checks.
  • Valuation: asset-light FAT should be compared with ROCE and FCF conversion, not used to claim superior operations by itself.

Key takeaways

  • FOFO, outsourcing and contract manufacturing move PPE, ROU and sometimes inventory off the brand's balance sheet, raising turnover and ROCE.
  • The trade-off is lower gross margin (partner's margin) and new risks (franchisee credit, supplier concentration).
  • Ind AS 115 (control transfer, consignment, royalties) and Ind AS 116 (embedded leases) determine where assets and revenue really sit.
  • Read commitments, guarantees and loans to franchisees before calling a model asset-light.
  • Compare asset-light and integrated peers on ROCE/ROIC and FCF, not on FAT alone.

Practice Questions

Q1. COCO-to-FOFO conversion [L2]

An eyewear retailer plans to convert 200 COCO stores to FOFO. Per store (₹ lakh): COCO revenue 120, gross margin 62%, store opex 45, capital employed 70. Under FOFO: wholesale price 70% of retail, brand gross margin 38% on wholesale revenue, royalty 3% of franchisee retail sales, capital employed 15. Compute per-store EBITDA and return on capital for both models and the aggregate change in FAT if store fit-outs (₹40 lakh per store) leave the balance sheet.

Q2. Consignment versus outright sale [L1]

A jewellery brand ships ₹50 crore of gold jewellery to franchisees on 28 March 2025 on sale-or-return terms (returns permitted for 90 days; historical returns 8%). Explain the revenue, refund liability and inventory accounting under Ind AS 115, and the effect on DIO and DSO at 31 March 2025.

💡 Hint: Consider when control transfers and how expected returns are measured.

Q3. Embedded lease in contract manufacturing [L3]

A D2C brand contracts a manufacturer to produce on a dedicated line for five years; the brand takes all output, decides production schedules and pays a fixed ₹6 crore a year plus a per-unit fee. Assess whether the contract contains a lease, and if so, describe the effect on the brand's FAT (including ROU), net debt/EBITDA and EBITDA margin, using a 10% incremental borrowing rate.

Q4. Interview — is asset-light always better? [L2]

An interviewer asks why investors pay a premium for asset-light consumer brands and whether the premium is always justified. Structure your answer around ROCE, growth, margin, and hidden risk transfer.
