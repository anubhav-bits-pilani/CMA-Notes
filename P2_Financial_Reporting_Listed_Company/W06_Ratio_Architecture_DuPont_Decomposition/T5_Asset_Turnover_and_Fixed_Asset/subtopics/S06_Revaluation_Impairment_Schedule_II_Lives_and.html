Subtopic note

Revaluation, Impairment, Schedule II Lives and Component Accounting

Scope: Revaluation and impairment effects on asset base; Schedule II useful lives and component accounting

Why it matters

Every fixed-asset productivity ratio depends on the carrying amount of PPE, and the carrying amount depends on accounting policy choices: cost or revaluation model, impairment write-downs, useful lives and component splits. Two companies with identical plants can report net PPE differing by 30–50% purely because one revalued land and buildings at Ind AS transition, the other impaired a loss-making unit, or one uses Schedule II lives while the other uses longer technically assessed lives. Analysts must normalise these differences before ranking turnover or ROCE; auditors test the judgements; and FP&A teams need to know that a change in useful life shifts depreciation, EBIT and ROCE prospectively without a single rupee of cash moving.

Core notes

Revaluation model (Ind AS 16)

  • Ind AS 16.29–31: an entity chooses the cost model or the revaluation model as its policy for an entire class of PPE (Ind AS 16.36); under revaluation, assets are carried at fair value at the revaluation date less subsequent depreciation and impairment, with revaluations made with sufficient regularity.
  • Ind AS 16.39–40: an increase goes to OCI and accumulates in revaluation surplus (unless reversing a previous P&L decrease); a decrease goes to P&L unless it reverses an existing surplus.
  • Ind AS 16.41: surplus may be transferred directly to retained earnings as the asset is used or derecognised — not recycled through P&L.
  • Depreciation is charged on the revalued amount, so EBIT falls.
  • Fair value measurement follows Ind AS 113; Indian practice typically uses registered valuers.

Ratio effects of an upward revaluation: higher net PPE and total assets → lower FAT, total asset turnover and ROCE; higher equity → lower ROE and D/E; higher depreciation → lower EBIT margin and PAT. Indian banks exclude revaluation reserve from tangible net worth (t4 s02).

Deemed cost at transition: Ind AS 101 allowed fair value as deemed cost at the transition date (1 April 2015/2016 for most companies). Many companies used it for land, producing a one-time step-up without an ongoing revaluation policy. The step-up sits in retained earnings, not revaluation surplus — easily missed.

US GAAP comparison: revaluation of PPE is prohibited; IFRS (IAS 16) and Ind AS permit it. Comparisons with US GAAP peers should be on a cost basis.

Impairment (Ind AS 36)

  • Test when indicators exist (and annually for goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles). Recoverable amount = higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use; assets grouped into cash-generating units (CGUs).
  • Impairment loss reduces the carrying amount and is charged to P&L (or against revaluation surplus).
  • Reversal is permitted for assets other than goodwill when indicators reverse, up to the carrying amount that would have existed without impairment.

Ratio effects: impairment lowers the asset base, mechanically lifting future FAT and ROCE and reducing future depreciation — a "big bath" can manufacture an apparent productivity improvement. Always look back three to five years for impairments when a company's turnover jumps without volume growth.

Schedule II useful lives (Companies Act 2013)

  • Part A: useful life is the period over which an asset is expected to be available for use; residual value generally not more than 5% of original cost. Companies may adopt a different useful life or residual value provided the justification, supported by technical advice, is disclosed.
  • Part C: indicative useful lives — illustratively, factory buildings 30 years, buildings other than factory 60 years, general plant and machinery 15 years, continuous process plant 25 years, furniture 10 years, motor cars 8 years, computers (end-user devices) 3 years, servers and networks 6 years.
  • Extra shift depreciation: for assets used in double shift, depreciation increases by 50% for that period; triple shift, by 100% — except for assets marked NESD (no extra shift depreciation).
  • Ind AS companies apply Ind AS 16 principles; Schedule II lives are the starting point, and Ind AS 16.51 requires annual review of useful life, residual value and method, with changes accounted prospectively as a change in accounting estimate (Ind AS 8).

Component accounting

Ind AS 16.43 requires each part of an item of PPE with a cost significant in relation to the total cost to be depreciated separately. Schedule II (in the notes to Part C) aligns: where the cost of a part is significant and its useful life differs from the remaining asset, the useful life of that part is determined separately. Examples: a cement kiln's refractory lining (2–3 years) vs the kiln shell (25 years); aircraft engines and airframes; a paint plant's building, process equipment and control systems. Major inspections and overhauls are capitalised as a component and depreciated to the next overhaul (Ind AS 16.14).

Worked illustration

Two identical chemical plants, revenue ₹2,000 crore each, FY2024-25 (₹ crore):

Item Company A (cost, Schedule II lives) Company B (land revalued, longer lives, prior impairment)
Land 100 500 (revalued)
Buildings and plant, net 900 750 (after ₹200 impairment in FY2021-22)
Net PPE 1,000 1,250
Depreciation 70 (15-year P&M) 45 (25-year technical life)
FAT 2.00x 1.60x
FAT ex-land 2.22x 2.67x

On total PPE A looks better; excluding land, B looks better because of its impairment and longer lives — neither reflects operations. Normalise: restate B at cost for land and add back the impairment (net of the depreciation it avoided) before comparing, or compare on gross block before impairment.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Change in useful life mid-series (e.g., extending plant lives from 15 to 25 years) cuts depreciation and lifts EBIT and ROCE prospectively; disclose and adjust for comparability.
  • Revaluation frequency: stale revaluations under-state current fair value; frequent ones create volatility.
  • Impairment reversals inflate the base and EBIT in the reversal year.
  • Component misallocation: lumping short-lived components into long-lived assets understates depreciation and overstates net PPE.
  • Land and investment property: revalued land used for operations is PPE; land held for capital appreciation is Ind AS 40 investment property (cost model only under Ind AS, with fair value disclosed) — exclude from operating turnover.
  • Tax: Income-tax Act depreciation (block of assets, WDV rates) differs; deferred tax arises, but ratios use book values.

Key takeaways

  • Revaluation raises the asset base and depreciation, lowering FAT, ROCE and ROE; banks exclude revaluation reserve from TNW.
  • Ind AS 101 deemed cost at fair value created hidden step-ups in retained earnings for many Indian companies.
  • Impairments lower the base and flatter future turnover; look back for big baths.
  • Schedule II gives indicative lives; different lives need technical justification and disclosure.
  • Component accounting separates significant parts with different lives; useful-life changes are prospective under Ind AS 8.

Practice Questions

Q1. Revaluation impact on ratios [L2]

On 1 April 2024 a company revalues its factory buildings from a carrying amount of ₹300 crore to ₹480 crore (remaining life 20 years). Before revaluation: revenue ₹2,400 crore, net PPE ₹1,200 crore (opening and closing), EBIT ₹360 crore, equity ₹1,500 crore. Compute FAT, EBIT margin, and ROE (PAT ₹250 crore before the extra depreciation, tax rate 25.17%) before and after the revaluation for FY2024-25.

Q2. Change in useful life [L2]

A cement company extends the useful life of plant and machinery (net block ₹6,000 crore, remaining life 10 years) to 18 years on technical advice from 1 April 2024. Compute the change in FY2024-25 depreciation, state the accounting treatment and disclosure, and explain the effect on ROCE comparability with a peer that did not change lives.

💡 Hint: The change is prospective; spread the remaining net block over the new remaining life.

Q3. Component accounting [L1]

A ₹500 crore kiln comprises the shell (₹420 crore, 25 years), refractory lining (₹50 crore, 3 years) and control systems (₹30 crore, 8 years). Compute annual depreciation with and without componentisation and the difference in net block after three years.

Q4. Normalising peers [L3]

You are comparing three cement companies: one revalued land at Ind AS transition (deemed cost), one recorded a ₹900 crore impairment on a loss-making plant in FY2022-23, and one uses Schedule II lives throughout. Design the normalisation steps and Excel adjustments needed to compare FAT and ROCE on a like-for-like basis, specifying which notes in each annual report you would use.
