Roadmap topic: Asset Turnover & Fixed Asset Productivity: Net Fixed Asset Turnover, Working Capital Turnover, and Capex-to-Depreciation ratio

Overview

Asset turnover ratios measure how hard a company makes its capital work. They form the middle leg of DuPont ROE (t2) and the capital-turnover leg of ROCE and ROIC (t1): two companies with identical margins can earn very different returns purely because one generates ₹3 of revenue per rupee of fixed assets and the other ₹1.5. In Indian capacity-driven sectors such as paints, cement, chemicals and auto components, turnover separates operational productivity from pricing; in retail and QSR, it is shaped by Ind AS 116 and franchise models; and in capex cycles, it is distorted by CWIP and ramp-up. For FP&A and modelling roles, the same ratios become forecast drivers — capex as a function of revenue and target turnover, working capital as days or a percentage of revenue — and output checks that keep a 3-statement model's balance sheet realistic.

The topic proceeds from measurement to diagnosis. Start with the core ratio definitions: net fixed asset turnover with and without CWIP and ROU assets, plus gross block turnover (s01); then working capital and total asset turnover and the statutory Schedule III net capital turnover ratio (s02). Next, look forward with capex-to- depreciation and asset age to judge whether the asset base is expanding, steady or ageing toward a replacement wave (s03). Then diagnose the two big structural drivers of turnover: capacity ramp-up after new plants in paints and cement (s04), and asset-light business models — FOFO franchising, outsourced and contract manufacturing — that move assets off the balance sheet (s05). Finish with the accounting choices that change the asset base without changing the assets: revaluation, impairment, Schedule II useful lives and component accounting (s06).

The key concepts formalise the arithmetic and the governing rule: NFAT with a mandatory scope statement (c01), working capital and total asset turnover (c02), capex/D&A (c03), the asset-age proxy (c04), and the Ind AS 16.51 annual review of useful life and residual value with prospective application under Ind AS 8 (c05). The unifying discipline is to label every turnover ratio with exactly what is in its denominator, use average balances consistently with the rest of the ratio engine, and never compare turnover across peers without adjusting for asset age, capex timing, leases and accounting policy.

Related concepts to study

Fixed asset and capex schedule in the 3-statement model

Fixed asset and capex schedule in 3-statement model → Week 14

In Week 14 you will build a PPE roll-forward: opening gross block + capex (including CWIP capitalisation) − disposals = closing gross block; opening accumulated depreciation + depreciation − disposals = closing accumulated depreciation. Capex is driven either top-down (capex as % of revenue, or a target NFAT that implies the required closing net PPE) or bottom-up (announced projects with commissioning dates). Depreciation is computed by vintage — existing assets over remaining life, new capex over Schedule II or policy lives from commissioning. This topic's ratios are the model's sanity checks: NFAT should converge to a defensible level, capex/D&A should be modestly above 1 in steady state, and asset age should not drift unrealistically.

Revaluation under Ind AS versus US GAAP

US GAAP prohibits PPE revaluation; Ind AS 16 permits revaluation model

Ind AS 16 (like IAS 16) allows an entity to choose, class by class, between the cost model and the revaluation model; revaluation increases go to OCI and revaluation surplus, decreases to P&L unless they reverse a surplus, and depreciation is based on the revalued amount. US GAAP (ASC 360) requires historical cost less depreciation and impairment and prohibits upward revaluation; impairment losses cannot be reversed for held-and-used assets. Comparing an Indian company that revalued land and buildings with a US-listed peer therefore requires restating the Indian company to cost — removing the revaluation surplus from PPE and equity and the incremental depreciation from P&L — before comparing NFAT, ROCE or ROE.

Capacity utilisation and capex appraisal links

Capacity utilization link → w6_t6; capex appraisal → Week 21

Fixed asset turnover can be decomposed into utilisation × realisation × capacity per rupee of assets (s04). The utilisation term is a sector KPI covered in t6 (capacity utilisation, volume growth, EBITDA per tonne) and sourced from investor presentations. Week 21 turns the question around: before capex is incurred, the project is appraised on NPV, IRR, payback and DSCR, using the same utilisation ramp and realisation assumptions. A good analyst reconciles the two — the turnover a new plant achieves in years two to four should be consistent with what was promised in its appraisal.

Common pitfalls

Comparing fresh capacity with fully depreciated plants

Comparing turnover between a company with fresh capacity and one with fully depreciated plants.

Net PPE shrinks as accumulated depreciation grows, so a company with old, largely depreciated plants shows high net fixed asset turnover even if its physical productivity is worse than a peer with new capacity. Analysts fall into this because net PPE is the default balance-sheet figure and the age of assets is not visible at a glance. Correct treatment: compute gross block turnover alongside net turnover, compute average asset age and life consumed (c04), and use revenue per unit of capacity where volume data exist. Detect it in review by checking whether the company with the "best" NFAT also has the highest accumulated depreciation to gross block ratio and the lowest capex/D&A.

Large CWIP in the denominator without flagging timing

Including large CWIP in denominator during a capex cycle without flagging timing.

During an expansion, CWIP can equal 30–60% of net PPE. Including it in the denominator causes turnover to fall sharply even though the existing plants are performing normally — the new capital simply has not started earning. The mistake arises from mechanically using "total fixed assets" or a ROCE-consistent denominator without commentary. Correct treatment: present NFAT both with and without CWIP, show the CWIP-to-net-PPE ratio, and use the Schedule III CWIP ageing and project completion schedule to estimate when capacity will commission. In a review, any year-on-year fall in turnover greater than 15–20% should prompt a check of CWIP movement and commissioning dates.

Ignoring ROU assets for retailers

Ignoring ROU assets for retailers, overstating fixed asset productivity.

Retailers, QSR chains and multiplexes lease most of their premises. Their owned PPE consists mainly of fit-outs, so NFAT on owned PPE alone looks spectacular (8–10x) compared with owner-operated peers (2–3x). People make the mistake because "fixed assets" in older templates means owned PPE, and ROU assets sit on a separate balance-sheet line. Correct treatment: include ROU assets for lease-heavy businesses (and lease liabilities in debt for consistency), or for pre-FY2019-20 series exclude ROU and capitalise rent consistently. Detect by checking whether the ratio dictionary names ROU assets, and by comparing NFAT with revenue per square foot (t6) — an unexpectedly high NFAT with ordinary revenue per square foot points to excluded leased assets.

Practice Questions

Q1. Three-company turnover diagnosis [L3]

Paint industry data for FY2024-25 (₹ crore, averages unless stated):

Item Company A Company B Company C
Revenue 34,000 11,200 7,800
Gross block (ex-land) 9,000 4,200 2,600
Accumulated depreciation 3,000 1,100 1,500
Net PPE 6,000 3,100 1,100
CWIP 800 900 60
ROU assets 1,500 400 150
Operating NWC 4,100 2,000 1,400
Total assets 30,000 9,500 7,600
Capex (year) 1,400 1,100 180
Depreciation 800 300 160

(a) Compute NFAT (PPE only, PPE + CWIP, PPE + CWIP + ROU), gross block turnover, WCT, TAT, capex/D&A and asset age. (b) Rank the companies on each and explain why rankings differ. (c) Write a four-sentence conclusion on which company is genuinely most productive, addressing asset age and capex timing.

Q2. Ramp-up and revaluation combined [L3]

A cement company commissions a 5 MTPA plant (cost ₹4,500 crore) on 1 January 2025, taking capacity from 25 to 30 MTPA. FY2024-25 volume is 21 MT, realisation ₹5,200 per tonne. On 1 April 2024 it also revalued land upward by ₹1,200 crore. Opening net PPE (including land) was ₹14,000 crore and depreciation for the year ₹900 crore (ignore other capex). (a) Compute effective capacity and utilisation. (b) Compute NFAT on average net PPE with and without the revaluation and with the new plant time-weighted. (c) Explain which figure you would present to an investment committee and why.

💡 Hint: The revaluation affects the asset base from the start of the year; the new plant only for three months.

Q3. FOFO conversion and turnover [L2]

A footwear brand with 600 COCO stores (average fit-out ₹0.8 crore and ROU ₹2.0 crore per store) converts 200 stores to FOFO over the year. Group revenue falls from ₹3,000 crore to ₹2,850 crore because of wholesale pricing; average owned PPE excluding stores is ₹500 crore. Compute NFAT including ROU before and after conversion, and discuss what this implies for ROCE and for the risk profile of the business.

Q4. Useful life change in a peer table [L2]

Company X extended plant lives from 15 to 25 years from 1 April 2024; its FY2024-25 depreciation fell from ₹420 crore to ₹270 crore. Revenue is ₹6,000 crore; average net PPE ₹3,500 crore; EBIT ₹780 crore; average capital employed ₹4,800 crore. Explain the effect on EBIT margin, ROCE, asset age and capex/D&A, and show how you would restate X for comparison with a peer still on 15-year lives.
