Key concept

s.186(7): Inter-Corporate Loan Interest Floor at G-sec Yield

Concept: s.186(7): no inter-corporate loan at interest below prevailing G-sec yield closest to the loan tenor

Statement

Under Section 186(7) of the Companies Act 2013, no loan shall be given under section 186 at a rate of interest lower than the prevailing yield of one-year, three-year, five-year or ten-year Government Security closest to the tenor of the loan:

\[ r_{\text{loan}} \ge y_{\text{G-sec}}(\tau^{*}), \qquad \tau^{*} = \arg\min_{\tau \in \{1,3,5,10\}} \lvert \tau - \text{tenor} \rvert \]

where \( r_{\text{loan}} \) is the contractual interest rate, \( y_{\text{G-sec}}(\tau) \) is the prevailing yield of the Government Security of tenor \( \tau \) years, and \( \tau^{*} \) is the benchmark tenor closest to the loan tenor.

Explanation

The rule prevents companies from transferring value through interest-free or cheap inter-corporate loans. It is a legal floor, not a measure of arm's length: a loan at the G-sec yield to a risky promoter entity is still far below a market rate that includes a credit spread.

Conditions and exceptions:

  • Applies to loans given by a company under s.186 to any person or other body corporate, including ICDs to group and promoter companies.
  • Exempt lenders under s.186(11): banking companies, insurance companies, housing finance companies and NBFCs in the ordinary course of business, and companies engaged in providing infrastructure facilities (Schedule VI) for loans, guarantees and security.
  • Government companies have specified exemptions by notification.
  • "Prevailing yield" is typically taken from RBI/FBIL/CCIL published yields at the date of the loan; the board minutes should document it.
  • Repayable-on-demand loans: practice benchmarks to the one-year G-sec.

Consequences of breach: penalties under s.186(13) on the company and officers in default; CARO 3(iv) requires the auditor to report non-compliance with s.185 and s.186; CARO 3(iii)(b) asks whether terms are prejudicial.

Analytical use: the G-sec floor tells you whether the loan is legal; the borrower's market rate (G-sec plus credit spread) tells you whether it is fair. The gap between the contract rate and the market rate drives the Ind AS 109 day-one fair value difference (s05).

Illustration

A listed company gives four ICDs on 1 April 2025, with prevailing yields assumed at 1-year 6.4%, 3-year 6.6%, 5-year 6.7%, 10-year 6.8%:

Borrower Tenor Benchmark Floor Contract rate Compliant? Borrower's market rate Fair?
Promoter NBFC 2 years 1-year or 3-year (equidistant — use the higher for prudence) 6.6% 7.0% Yes 12.5% No
Fellow subsidiary 4 years 3-year or 5-year (equidistant) 6.7% 6.0% No 10.0% No
Unrelated corporate 1 year 1-year 6.4% 9.5% Yes 9.5% Yes
Associate 7 years 5-year 6.7% 0% No 11.0% No

Where a tenor is equidistant between two benchmarks, the law does not specify; a prudent reading applies the higher yield, and the board should document its choice.

Practice Questions

Q1. Identify the benchmark [L1]

For loans of tenors 6 months, 2.5 years, 4 years, 8 years and 12 years, state the G-sec tenor that s.186(7) requires as the benchmark and any ambiguity.

Q2. Compliance and fair value [L2]

A company lends ₹150 crore for 5 years at 6.5% to a promoter-group company when the 5-year G-sec yield is 6.9% and the borrower's market rate is 11.5%. State the s.186(7) position and compute the Ind AS 109 day-one difference assuming annual interest and bullet repayment.

💡 Hint: Discount the contractual cash flows at the borrower's market rate.

Q3. Interview: legal vs fair [L1]

Explain in under 120 words why a loan that complies with s.186(7) can still be a related-party value transfer.
