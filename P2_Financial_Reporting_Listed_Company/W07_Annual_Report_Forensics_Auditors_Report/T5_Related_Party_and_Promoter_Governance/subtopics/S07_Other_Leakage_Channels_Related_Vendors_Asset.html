Subtopic note

Other Leakage Channels: Related Vendors, Asset Purchases and CSR

Scope: Other channels: related-party vendors/distributors, asset purchases from promoters, CSR routed to promoter trusts

Why it matters

Once royalties, ICDs and guarantees are under scrutiny, value transfer moves to channels that look like ordinary business: a promoter-linked vendor supplying at a premium, a related distributor that absorbs channel-stuffed stock and pays late, a one-off purchase of land or a business from the promoter at a high valuation, or CSR money routed to a foundation run by the promoter family. Each is individually small, often within omnibus approvals or the s.188 ordinary-course exemption, and spread across notes that analysts read separately. The forensic skill is aggregation: pulling these flows into a single related-party outflow table and testing pricing, terms and substance.

Core notes

1. Related-party vendors and distributors

Vendors (raw materials, packaging, logistics, EPC contractors, IT services):

  • Concentration: related-party purchases as % of total purchases; a single promoter vendor supplying 30% of inputs is a pricing and continuity risk.
  • Pricing: compare unit prices with independent suppliers (Reg 23 explanatory statements and the SEBI industry standards on minimum information require comparables); compare gross margins with peers who source independently.
  • Payment terms: days payable to related vendors vs others. Paying related vendors faster (or advancing money) is a working-capital transfer.
  • Capital advances to related EPC contractors with little CWIP progress — see Week 7 topic 3 s04 and t4 s02.

Distributors and customers:

  • Sales to related distributors at quarter-ends with high receivable days — a channel-stuffing route (Week 7 topic 3 s05).
  • Discounts and schemes to related distributors above those given to third parties.
  • Credit terms extended beyond normal; under s.185 a book debt can be a "loan".
  • Returns after period-end from related parties reversing prior sales.

2. Asset purchases from promoters

Acquisitions of land, buildings, brands, businesses or shares from the promoter or promoter entities transfer value if the price exceeds fair value. Regulatory gates:

  • s.192 Companies Act: an arrangement by which a director of the company (or its holding, subsidiary or associate) or a connected person acquires assets from the company for consideration other than cash, or the company acquires such assets from them, needs prior approval by a resolution in general meeting, with a valuation by a registered valuer in the notice.
  • s.188(1)(b): buying property of any kind from a related party; shareholder approval above 10% of net worth.
  • Reg 23 materiality and the industry-standard requirement to place valuation reports before the audit committee.
  • Ind AS 103 common control (Appendix C) for acquisitions of promoter-owned businesses: pooling of interests at carrying amounts, with the excess consideration debited to equity (capital reserve) — so a high price does not create goodwill; it reduces equity directly. This is the clearest accounting signal of overpayment.
  • Ind AS 16/40 for property: cost includes the purchase price; overpayment shows up later as impairment or low returns on the asset.

Illustration: a listed hospital chain acquires 100% of a promoter-owned clinic company for ₹420 crore cash. The clinic's net assets at carrying amounts are ₹150 crore; an independent valuation range was ₹250–300 crore.

Entry (common control, pooling) Dr (₹ cr) Cr (₹ cr)
Net assets of the clinic (carrying amounts) 150

The ₹270 crore equity debit, and specifically the ₹120–170 crore above the valuation range, is value transferred to the promoter. Net worth falls; no goodwill impairment test will ever surface it.

3. CSR routed to promoter trusts

Companies meeting the s.135 thresholds (net worth ₹500 crore or more, turnover ₹1,000 crore or more, or net profit ₹5 crore or more) must spend at least 2% of average net profit of the preceding three years on Schedule VII activities. Under the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014 as amended in 2021:

  • CSR may be implemented directly or through an implementing agency — a s.8 company, registered trust or society established by the company (alone or with others), or one with a track record — registered with the MCA via Form CSR-1.
  • Activities benefiting only employees and their families are excluded; contributions to political parties are excluded.
  • Unspent amounts relating to ongoing projects go to an Unspent CSR Account within 30 days of year-end; other unspent amounts go to a Schedule VII fund within six months.
  • Schedule III (2021 amendment) requires disclosure of CSR obligation, amount spent, shortfall, reasons, nature of activities, and related party transactions in relation to CSR, e.g. contributions to a trust controlled by the company.
  • Companies with average CSR obligation of ₹10 crore or more must undertake impact assessment for large projects.

Routing CSR through a foundation chaired by the promoter is legal and common. The forensic questions are about control and benefit: Does the trust own assets (schools, hospitals, land) in which the promoter family has beneficial or managerial interest? Does it pay salaries to family members? Does it buy services from promoter entities? Is the CSR spend persistently channelled to one entity with no project-level reporting? CSR is a cash outflow without return to shareholders by design; routing it through a promoter-controlled entity can turn it into an asset-building vehicle for the promoter family.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Undisclosed related vendors: entities outside the Ind AS 24 list but connected through relatives, ex-employees or common addresses. Use MCA director data, GSTIN legal-name searches and vendor master analysis where you have access (internal audit/due diligence).
  • Round-tripping: purchases from a vendor that funds the promoter entity — an outflow to an apparently unrelated party whose effect benefits a related party is an RPT under LODR Reg 2(1)(zc).
  • Distribution JV with promoter: margins earned by the JV are partly promoter income.
  • Asset sales to promoters at low prices are the mirror image; check profit on sale of assets and s.192 approvals.
  • CSR to promoter-linked university that also receives sponsored research or land from the company — aggregate both flows.

Key takeaways

  • Aggregate all channels — vendors, distributors, asset deals, CSR — into one related-party outflow table.
  • Test vendor pricing, concentration and payment terms; test distributor credit and quarter-end sales.
  • Common-control acquisitions from promoters debit excess consideration to equity; that debit measures overpayment.
  • s.192 needs prior general meeting approval and valuation for non-cash asset deals with directors.
  • CSR through promoter foundations is permitted; scrutinise control, beneficiaries and Schedule III related-party CSR disclosure.

Practice Questions

Q1. Vendor pricing and terms [L2]

A listed packaged-foods company buys ₹520 crore of packaging, of which ₹310 crore from a promoter-group company. Average price paid to the related vendor is ₹118 per unit versus ₹106 from independent vendors. Payables days are 25 for the related vendor and 75 for others. Compute the annual pricing premium, the working-capital transfer from faster payment, and draft two questions for the audit committee.

💡 Hint: Use the purchase volume implied by the related-party spend and the unit prices; then compute the payables difference on the related purchases.

Q2. Common control acquisition [L2]

A listed company acquires a promoter-owned logistics company for ₹600 crore in shares and cash. Carrying net assets are ₹220 crore; fair value of net assets is ₹310 crore; an independent DCF valued the business at ₹400 crore. Pass the consolidation entry under Ind AS 103 Appendix C, and quantify the value transfer under alternative benchmarks.

Q3. CSR review [L1]

A company's CSR obligation is ₹24 crore; it spent ₹24 crore, all contributed to a foundation whose trustees are the promoter, his wife and his son. List the disclosures you would expect under s.135, the CSR Rules and Schedule III, and five questions you would ask.

Q4. Undisclosed related vendor [L3]

During an internal audit, you find that a logistics vendor receiving ₹85 crore a year has a registered address that matches a promoter-group company and a director who was the listed company's former CFO. The vendor is not in the Ind AS 24 list. Analyse whether it is a related party under Ind AS 24, Companies Act s.2(76) and LODR Reg 2(1)(zb)/(zc), and describe the reporting steps and evidence you would gather.
