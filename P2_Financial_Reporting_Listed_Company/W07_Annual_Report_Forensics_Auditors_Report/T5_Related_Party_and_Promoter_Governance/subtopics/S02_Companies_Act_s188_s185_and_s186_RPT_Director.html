Subtopic note

Companies Act s.188, s.185 and s.186: RPT, Director Loans and ICD Limits

Scope: Companies Act s.188 (ordinary course and arm's length exemption), s.185 loans to directors' interested entities, s.186 limits on loans/ICDs

Why it matters

The Companies Act 2013 is the first legal filter on promoter transactions. Section 188 governs commercial related-party contracts, section 185 restricts loans and guarantees to directors and entities in which they are interested, and section 186 caps inter-corporate loans, guarantees, security and investments and prescribes a minimum interest rate. Forensic reviews in Indian cases — Coffee Day, Gensol, several NBFC-linked groups — turned on whether loans and ICDs to promoter-linked entities complied with these sections. Non-compliance is reported by the auditor under CARO 3(iii) and 3(iv), so the statutory framework, the auditor's report and the notes can be triangulated.

Core notes

Section 188 — related party transactions

Transactions covered (s.188(1)(a)–(g)): sale, purchase or supply of goods or materials; selling or otherwise disposing of, or buying, property of any kind; leasing of property; availing or rendering of services; appointment of an agent for purchase or sale; a related party's appointment to an office or place of profit in the company, subsidiary or associate; and underwriting of securities. Loans, guarantees and investments are not covered — they fall under s.185/186.

Approval layers:

  1. Audit committee approval of all RPTs (s.177(4)(iv)), with omnibus approval permitted under Rule 6A of the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014.
  2. Board consent at a meeting (s.188(1)); interested directors do not participate (s.184(2)).
  3. Prior ordinary resolution of shareholders where the transaction exceeds thresholds in Rule 15(3) — broadly, sale/purchase/supply of goods or services, or leasing, of 10% or more of turnover; buying/selling property of 10% or more of net worth; office of profit with monthly remuneration above ₹2.5 lakh; underwriting above 1% of net worth. Related parties cannot vote on the resolution (second proviso to s.188(1)), subject to the exemption for companies where 90% or more of members are relatives of promoters or related parties.

The ordinary course and arm's length exemption. The proviso to s.188(1) states that nothing in the sub-section applies to transactions entered into in the ordinary course of business other than transactions not on an arm's length basis. Both conditions must be met:

  • Ordinary course of business: the transaction is in line with the company's normal business (objects, frequency, volume, historical practice). A cement company buying clinker is ordinary course; buying a promoter's land parcel is not.
  • Arm's length: conducted as if the parties were unrelated, with no conflict of interest (Explanation (b) to s.188(1)). Evidence includes comparable uncontrolled prices, quotations, transfer-pricing benchmarking (often borrowed from s.92 Income-tax Act documentation) and independent valuation.

If exempt, board and shareholder approval under s.188 are not needed, but the audit committee approval under s.177 still applies and the transaction is still disclosed under Ind AS 24 and in Form AOC-2 (for contracts not at arm's length or material contracts at arm's length) under s.134(3)(h). For listed entities, LODR Reg 23 applies independently and has no ordinary-course exemption for audit committee approval (s03).

Consequences: a contract without required approval is voidable at the option of the board or shareholders (s.188(3)); penalties apply to directors and employees authorising it (s.188(5)).

Section 185 — loans to directors and interested entities

  • Prohibited (s.185(1)): loans (including book debts), guarantees or security for loans to any director of the company or of its holding company, any partner or relative of such director, or any firm in which such director or relative is a partner.
  • Permitted with special resolution (s.185(2)): loans, guarantees or security to any person in whom any of the directors is interested — including a private company of which a director is a director or member, a body corporate in which 25% or more voting power is controlled by directors, and a body corporate whose board acts on directions of the company's directors — provided the special resolution is passed with full disclosure in the explanatory statement and the loans are used by the borrower for its principal business activities.
  • Exemptions (s.185(3)): loans to MD/WTD as part of service conditions or under a member-approved scheme; lending companies in the ordinary course charging at least the RBI bank rate; loans to wholly-owned subsidiaries and guarantees/security for bank loans to subsidiaries, where used for principal business activities.

Contravention carries penalties on the company, the officer and the recipient (s.185(4)).

Section 186 — loans, guarantees, security and investments

Sub-section Rule
s.186(1) Investments through not more than two layers of investment companies (subject to exceptions)
s.186(2) Loans, guarantees, security and acquisitions of securities not to exceed the higher of 60% of paid-up share capital + free reserves + securities premium, or 100% of free reserves + securities premium
s.186(3) Beyond the limit, prior special resolution required
s.186(4) Disclose full particulars of loans, guarantees, security and investments, and purpose, in financial statements
s.186(5) Unanimous board approval at a meeting; prior consent of the public financial institution if a term loan from it subsists and there is a default
s.186(7) No loan at interest lower than the prevailing yield of 1-, 3-, 5- or 10-year G-sec closest to the loan tenor (c03)
s.186(8) No loans etc. while in default of repayment of deposits or interest
s.186(11) Exemptions for banks, insurance, housing finance and NBFCs in ordinary course, and companies providing infrastructure facilities (Schedule VI) for loans/guarantees/security

Rule 11 of the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014 exempts loans, guarantees and security to wholly-owned subsidiaries (and JV guarantees) from the special resolution requirement of s.186(3), though disclosure still applies.

Inter-corporate deposits (ICDs) are loans for s.186 purposes. Forensic patterns: ICDs placed with promoter-group companies at the start of a quarter and returned after the quarter-end (window dressing of cash), ICDs rolled over indefinitely, and ICDs given just below the s.186(2) limit across several group entities.

Illustration — limit computation

Item (₹ crore) Amount
Paid-up share capital 100
Free reserves 800
Securities premium 300
60% of (100 + 800 + 300) 720
100% of (800 + 300) 1,100
s.186(2) limit (higher) 1,100
Existing loans + guarantees + investments (excluding WOS exempted under Rule 11) 950
Headroom without special resolution 150

A proposed ₹300 crore ICD to a promoter-group NBFC would need a special resolution under s.186(3), interest at least at the G-sec yield for the tenor under s.186(7), and — if any director is interested in the NBFC — the s.185(2) special resolution and use for principal business. It also triggers Reg 23 (s03).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • "Ordinary course" stretched: management may treat recurring purchases from a promoter vendor as ordinary course while prices exceed market; the exemption fails if arm's length fails.
  • Book debts as loans: extended credit to a related customer beyond normal terms can be a loan under s.185 ("loan includes book debts").
  • Layered routing: loans to an unrelated intermediary that on-lends to a promoter entity — economic substance breach that CARO 3(ix)(e)/(f) and SEBI orders have targeted.
  • Private company exemptions: certain exemptions from s.185/186 for private companies do not flow to listed parents' transactions with them.
  • Disclosure vs approval: s.186(4) disclosure in notes is often a generic table without purpose; purpose is mandatory.

Key takeaways

  • s.188 covers commercial RPTs; loans, guarantees and investments are under s.185 and s.186.
  • The s.188 exemption requires both ordinary course and arm's length; audit committee approval and disclosure remain.
  • s.185 prohibits loans to directors and relatives; loans to interested entities need a special resolution and use for principal business.
  • s.186(2) limit is the higher of 60% of capital, free reserves and premium or 100% of free reserves and premium.
  • s.186(7) sets a G-sec floor on loan interest; ICD patterns around quarter-ends are a forensic flag.

Practice Questions

Q1. s.186 limit and approvals [L2]

Omega Ltd has paid-up capital ₹200 crore, free reserves ₹1,200 crore and securities premium ₹100 crore. Outstanding: loans to a WOS ₹300 crore, guarantee for a JV ₹200 crore, ICDs to two promoter-group companies ₹500 crore, investments in mutual funds ₹400 crore (not in group). It proposes a further ₹250 crore ICD to a promoter-group company for 3 years. Compute the s.186(2) limit and headroom, and list every approval and rate condition applicable.

💡 Hint: Decide which existing items count toward the limit before computing headroom.

Q2. Ordinary course test [L1]

For each, state whether the s.188 proviso exemption is available: (a) a paint company buying titanium dioxide from a promoter-group chemical company at prices benchmarked to three independent quotes; (b) the same company buying an office building from the promoter's wife at a valuation 25% above two independent valuers; (c) a hotel company paying rent to the promoter's trust at market rates for a property it has leased for 15 years.

Q3. s.185 interested entity [L2]

A listed company's director holds 30% of a private company that needs a ₹40 crore loan to repay its own debt. Explain whether the loan can be given, the approvals required, and why the stated purpose matters.

Q4. ICD window dressing [L3]

A mid-cap discloses cash of ₹450 crore at 31 March 2025. Monthly data (from a rating report) show ICDs to promoter entities of ₹300–400 crore outstanding in every month except March. Write the reviewer's analysis, the CARO and Companies Act provisions engaged, and the adjustments you would make to average net debt.
