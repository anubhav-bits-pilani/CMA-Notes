Subtopic note

Brand Royalties and Management Fees to Promoter Entities

Scope: Brand royalties and management fees paid to promoter entities; trend as % of revenue and vs dividend payout to minorities

Why it matters

Royalty and management-fee arrangements are the cleanest way for a promoter to take cash out of a listed company ahead of minority shareholders. A dividend is shared pro rata; a royalty paid to the promoter's brand-owning entity goes 100% to the promoter and is tax-deductible for the payer. Indian examples span MNC subsidiaries paying technology and brand royalties to the foreign parent (historically 3–5% of sales for several consumer and auto companies) and domestic groups charging listed affiliates for use of the group name. SEBI responded with Reg 23(1A) (2019), making brand/royalty payments material above 5% of consolidated turnover, so that minorities vote on them. An analyst must test whether the payment is earned (value of the brand or technology, services actually rendered) and how it compares with what minorities receive.

Core notes

Types of payments

Payment Typical basis Recipient Key question
Brand royalty / licence fee % of net sales, sometimes capped at % of PBT Promoter brand-holding entity or foreign parent Does the brand drive pricing power that the listed entity could not build itself?
Technical know-how / technology royalty % of sales of licensed products, or lump sum Foreign parent, promoter R&D entity Is technology still being transferred or is the royalty legacy?
Management / shared services fee Cost allocation plus mark-up, or % of revenue Group holding company Need-benefit test: are services identifiable, needed and not duplicated?
Corporate guarantee commission % of guaranteed amount Promoter entity giving guarantee for the company Is the fee consistent with market guarantee rates?
Rent for promoter-owned premises Per sq ft Promoter or family trust Market rent comparison

Accounting, tax and regulatory frame

  • Accounting: expense in P&L (other expenses), disclosed as related-party transactions under Ind AS 24, with balances payable.
  • Companies Act: s.188 applies (availing services); ordinary course and arm's length exemption may be claimed (s02). Contracts must be disclosed in AOC-2 if material or not at arm's length.
  • LODR: Reg 23 audit committee approval; Reg 23(1A) — payments for brand usage or royalty are material if they exceed 5% of annual consolidated turnover, individually or taken together with previous transactions in the year (c02). Material modifications (rate increase) need shareholder approval with related parties barred from voting.
  • Income-tax: deductible under s.37 if wholly and exclusively for business; transfer pricing provisions (s.92 onwards) apply to international and specified domestic transactions; withholding under s.194J (domestic) or s.195 with DTAA rates (foreign, typically 10%–15%). Historically FEMA caps on royalty were removed in 2009–10.

Metrics

\[ \text{Royalty intensity} = \frac{\text{Royalty} + \text{Management fees to promoter entities}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \]

\[ \text{Royalty-to-PBT} = \frac{\text{Royalty} + \text{Fees}}{\text{PBT before royalty and fees}} \]

\[ \text{Royalty vs minority dividend} = \frac{\text{Royalty} + \text{Fees}}{\text{Dividend paid} \times (1 - \text{Promoter holding \%})} \]

\[ \text{Promoter economic share} = \frac{\text{Royalty} + \text{Fees} + \text{Dividend} \times h}{\text{Royalty} + \text{Fees} + \text{Dividend}} \]

where \( h \) is the promoter's shareholding. Compare the promoter economic share with \( h \): the gap is the value transfer through royalties.

Illustration

An NSE-listed consumer company, promoter (foreign parent) holding 62%:

₹ crore FY21 FY23 FY25
Revenue 12,000 15,000 18,000
Royalty rate 3.0% 3.8% 4.5%
Royalty 360 570 810
PBT after royalty 2,000 2,300 2,500
Dividend paid 1,200 1,400 1,500
Dividend to minorities (38%) 456 532 570
  • Royalty intensity rose from 3.0% to 4.5%; royalty-to-PBT (before royalty) from 15.3% to 24.5%.
  • Royalty / minority dividend: 0.79x → 1.07x → 1.42x.
  • Promoter economic share FY25: \( (810 + 1{,}500 \times 0.62) \div (810 + 1{,}500) = 1{,}740 \div 2{,}310 \approx 75\% \) vs 62% shareholding.
  • Reg 23(1A) test: 810 ÷ 18,000 = 4.5%, below 5% — no shareholder vote needed even though the rate has risen by half. If rate increases continue, the threshold will be crossed around 5%.

Questions to ask: did revenue growth come from brand-led pricing, or from the listed entity's distribution build-out? Did the licensor increase R&D/ad spend for the Indian market? Is the rate benchmarked to independent licences?

Management fees and cost allocations

Management fees are harder to evaluate than royalties because they bundle services. Apply the transfer-pricing need-benefit framework: (1) identify the specific services; (2) test whether the listed company needed them and received benefit; (3) check for duplication with in-house functions; (4) verify the allocation key (headcount, revenue) and the mark-up (typically cost plus 5–10% for low value-adding services). A management fee calculated as a percentage of revenue with no service list is a red flag.

Dividend policy interaction

Promoter-owned brand entities can prefer royalty over dividends because royalties are paid before tax at the company level (and dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands since FY 2020-21). A falling payout ratio alongside rising royalty indicates a shift of distributions from all shareholders to the promoter.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Royalty on exports or on products not using the brand — check the base in the licence agreement.
  • Royalty caps at a % of PBT protect minorities in loss years; floors (minimum royalty) do the opposite.
  • Brand transferred out: promoter moves a brand the listed company built into a private entity and then licenses it back — a classic value-transfer pattern; check historical ownership of trademarks (IP India records).
  • Multiple small payments (brand, technical, IT, management) each below thresholds — aggregate them.
  • One-time "brand acquisition" from the promoter at a high valuation is the capital-account equivalent of royalty.

Key takeaways

  • Royalties and fees are 100% promoter cash and pre-tax for the payer; dividends are shared pro rata.
  • Track royalty intensity, royalty-to-PBT and royalty vs minority dividend over at least five years.
  • Reg 23(1A) makes brand/royalty payments above 5% of consolidated turnover material, needing a minority vote.
  • Management fees need a need-benefit test, service list and allocation key; % of revenue fees are suspect.
  • Compare promoter economic share with promoter holding to quantify value transfer.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute royalty metrics [L2]

A listed auto company (promoter holding 56%) reports revenue ₹1,20,000 crore, royalty to the parent ₹4,200 crore, PBT after royalty ₹13,500 crore and dividend paid ₹3,800 crore for FY 2024-25. Compute royalty intensity, royalty-to-PBT before royalty, royalty vs minority dividend and promoter economic share. State whether Reg 23(1A) approval was required, given consolidated turnover equals revenue.

💡 Hint: Add royalty back to PBT before computing the PBT-based ratio.

Q2. Management fee review [L2]

A listed infrastructure company pays its promoter holding company a "strategic management fee" of 1.5% of revenue (₹90 crore) with no service agreement disclosed. The holding company has 25 employees. Design the evidence request list and the tests you would apply, and explain how you would treat the fee in normalised EBITDA.

Q3. Royalty increase vote [L3]

An MNC subsidiary proposes increasing the royalty from 3.45% to 4.5% of net sales over five years. Consolidated turnover is ₹60,000 crore. Prepare an analysis for an institutional investor's vote: the Reg 23(1A) position each year, the cumulative cash transfer versus a flat rate, the minority dividend comparison, and the questions to put to management before voting.

Q4. Brand moved to promoter entity [L3]

In FY 2021-22 a listed apparel company sold its flagship trademark to a promoter-owned LLP for ₹50 crore and now pays it 2% of sales (₹60 crore in FY 2024-25, growing). Evaluate the transaction under Ind AS 24, s.188, Reg 23 and from a valuation perspective, and state the adjustments you would make in a DCF.
