Roadmap topic: Related Party & Promoter Governance Checks: Inter-corporate deposits (ICDs), brand royalties to promoter entities, and pledged shareholding trends

Overview

Most Indian listed companies are promoter-controlled, so the central governance risk is not managers versus shareholders but the controlling shareholder versus minority shareholders. Value can leave a listed company through channels that are lawful in form: inter-corporate deposits to promoter-group entities, brand royalties and management fees, purchases from related vendors, acquisitions of promoter-owned assets, guarantees, and CSR routed to promoter foundations. Promoter pledges add a second risk: when the promoter's own balance sheet is stretched, the incentive to extract cash from the listed company rises, and share-price falls can trigger forced selling. The cases that defined the last decade — Satyam, DHFL, Coffee Day, Zee/Essel, Gensol — all showed related-party and pledge signals before the accounting collapsed.

For FP&A, credit, equity research and transaction diligence roles, this topic gives you a structured, evidence-based way to answer: who are the related parties, how much flows to them, are the flows approved and priced fairly, and is the promoter under financial stress? The outputs are an RPT outflow table, RPT intensity ratios, a pledge trend, and a graded list of findings with note and page references — exactly what the week's exercise and the Saturday risk memo require. Your audit background helps: you know that approval is a control, not a proof of arm's length, and that the real test is pricing, terms, substance and recoverability.

The learning sequence: s01 builds the related-party universe under Ind AS 24 (and reconciles with the Companies Act and LODR definitions) and covers KMP compensation. s02 sets out the Companies Act gates — s.188, s.185 and s.186 — with c03 on the s.186(7) interest floor. s03 covers LODR Reg 23 approvals, omnibus approvals and the voting bar, with c01 (materiality) and c02 (the 5% royalty test). Then the outflow channels: s04 royalties and management fees, s05 ICDs and group loans (Ind AS 109 fair value, rollovers, ECL), and s07 other channels (vendors, distributors, asset purchases, CSR). s06 and c05 cover promoter pledges and encumbrance. c04 brings the channels together as RPT intensity.

Related concepts to study

IAS 24 and US GAAP ASC 850

IAS 24 is equivalent; US GAAP ASC 850 related party disclosures

Ind AS 24 is substantially converged with IAS 24, with Indian differences such as a wider definition of close family members (including parents and siblings) and the treatment of government-related entities. US GAAP ASC 850 requires disclosure of material related party transactions, the nature of the relationship, amounts and balances, but it is less prescriptive than IAS 24 on categories and on KMP compensation (executive pay disclosure in the US comes from SEC Regulation S-K rather than ASC 850). ASC 850 also prohibits stating that transactions are at arm's length unless substantiated — similar to Ind AS 24.23. When benchmarking an Indian company against US or IFRS peers, expect broadly similar disclosures, but the Indian regulatory overlay (Reg 23 approvals, AOC-2, CARO 3(iii)) gives more evidence than US filings for promoter-type transactions.

Case patterns: Coffee Day, Zee/Essel, Gensol

Case patterns: Coffee Day (group subsidiary cash transfers), Zee/Essel (letters of comfort), Gensol (SEBI 2025 order on loan diversion)

Coffee Day Enterprises: after the founder's death in 2019, an investigation reported that substantial funds had moved from subsidiaries to a promoter-owned entity (Mysore Amalgamated Coffee Estates), reported in the thousands of crores, through intra-group transfers that were not visible as related-party loans in the listed entity's standalone view. Zee/Essel: letters of comfort issued by Zee to lenders of promoter group entities surfaced in 2019–20; SEBI proceedings examined whether these were undisclosed guarantees-in-substance for related parties without Reg 23 approval. Gensol Engineering: the SEBI interim order of April 2025 alleged that term loans from IREDA and PFC for purchasing EVs were diverted to promoter-related entities and used for personal and unrelated purposes. Common lessons: look at subsidiaries, comfort letters and end-use of borrowed funds, not just the related-party note.

CARO cross-checks and M&A diligence

CARO 3(iii), 3(ix)(e) and 3(xiii) cross-checks → w7_t1; governance diligence in M&A → Week 22

CARO 3(iii) reports loans, advances, guarantees and security given, terms, overdues, evergreening and loans repayable on demand with the share going to promoters and related parties — the auditor's direct view of s05. CARO 3(ix)(e) reports whether funds were taken from any entity to meet obligations of subsidiaries, associates or JVs, and 3(ix)(f) loans raised on pledge of securities held in them. CARO 3(xiii) requires the auditor to report whether RPTs comply with s.177 and s.188 and are disclosed as required by accounting standards. Reconcile these clauses with the Ind AS 24 note and Reg 23(9) disclosures; mismatches are findings. In M&A (Week 22), the same checks form the governance workstream of due diligence: identifying RPTs that will continue post-deal, pricing them at arm's length in the valuation, and protecting the buyer through warranties and indemnities.

BRSR governance and proxy advisers

BRSR governance disclosures and proxy-advisor reports (IiAS, InGovern, SES) as secondary sources

The Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR, mandatory for the top 1,000 listed entities) includes governance disclosures — Principle 1 (ethics, transparency and accountability) and, in BRSR Core and leadership indicators, concentration of purchases and sales with related parties, and loans and advances and investments with related parties as a share of totals. These ratios give a standardised cross-check on c04. Proxy advisory firms registered with SEBI — IiAS (Institutional Investor Advisory Services), InGovern and Stakeholders Empowerment Services (SES) — publish voting recommendations on RPT resolutions, royalty increases, director appointments and remuneration, often with detailed critiques of pricing and approval processes. Treat them as secondary sources: useful for issues lists and historical context, but verify every figure against the annual report.

Common pitfalls

Reading only the Ind AS 24 note

Reading only the Ind AS 24 note and missing outflows via non-disclosed vendors or consultants. The related-party note is only as complete as management's list of related parties. Analysts take it as the universe because it is audited and tabulated. Leakage routed through vendors, consultants or intermediaries connected to the promoter (but not formally related) never appears there. Correct treatment: build the universe from multiple sources (s01) — shareholding pattern names, directors' other directorships, AOC-2, Reg 23(9) disclosures, LODR Reg 2(1)(zc)'s "benefit a related party" test — and test large vendors and consultants for connections. In review, detect it by comparing top-vendor concentration and unexplained "legal and professional" or "consultancy" growth with the related-party list.

Encumbrance through NDUs and margin funding

Ignoring encumbrance through NDUs and margin funding that is not labelled "pledge". Analysts often read the "shares pledged" column of the shareholding pattern and stop. Promoter lenders frequently use non-disposal undertakings, negative liens, powers of attorney or margin-trading pledges, which may be reported in separate columns or only in SAST Reg 31 event filings — or at holding-company level, where they escape the listed company's disclosures altogether. Correct treatment: apply the SAST Reg 28(3) definition (any arrangement in the nature of encumbrance), read all Reg 31 event disclosures during the period, and check charge filings of promoter holding companies. In review, detect it by reconciling quarter-end pledge data with event filings and by asking for the encumbrance type breakdown.

Assuming arm's length because the audit committee approved

Assuming arm's length because the audit committee approved the transaction. Audit committee approval under Reg 23 and s.177 is a process control. Committees rely on management-prepared benchmarking, often without independent quotations or valuations, and independent directors may lack information or independence in substance. Analysts treat approval as a conclusion because it is documented and regulatory. Correct treatment: test pricing and terms yourself — compare unit prices and margins with peers, check valuation reports for asset deals, compute the Ind AS 109 day-one difference for loans, and examine voting results where institutional dissent can reveal concerns. In review, flag any memo that cites approval as the only evidence of arm's length.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated governance review of a mid-cap [L3]

Kaveri Consumer Ltd (NSE-listed; consolidated turnover FY 2023-24 ₹5,600 crore; FY 2024-25 revenue ₹6,200 crore; EBITDA ₹720 crore; net worth ₹3,100 crore; promoter holding 51%) reports for FY 2024-25: purchases from a promoter packaging company ₹480 crore; brand royalty to the promoter's trademark LLP at 3% of sales; interest-free ICDs of ₹260 crore to two promoter companies (₹40 crore repaid on 28 March, re-lent on 5 April); acquisition of a promoter-owned warehouse company for ₹210 crore (carrying net assets ₹70 crore; valuer's range ₹110–130 crore); CSR of ₹11 crore paid entirely to the promoter's foundation; promoter encumbrance 38% of holding including NDUs of 12%. Dividend paid ₹250 crore.

  1. Compute RPT intensity on revenue and EBITDA by bucket.
  2. Determine Reg 23 materiality and Reg 23(1A) status for each relationship.
  3. Compute the royalty vs minority dividend ratio and promoter economic share.
  4. Pass the Ind AS 109 entries for the ICDs (3-year tenor, market rate 12%) and the Ind AS 103 common-control entry for the acquisition.
  5. Compute the pledge ratios and assess whether SAST reasons disclosure was required.
  6. Grade each finding by severity with evidence references.

Q2. Statutory compliance matrix [L2]

For the facts in Q1, prepare a compliance matrix showing for each transaction the applicable provisions (s.177, s.185, s.186, s.188, s.192, Reg 23, Reg 23(1A)), the approvals required, the evidence you would seek, and the CARO clause (3(iii), 3(iv), 3(xiii)) under which an auditor would report any lapse. Paid-up capital is ₹100 crore, free reserves ₹2,600 crore, securities premium ₹400 crore; the 3-year G-sec yield is 6.8%.

Q3. Case comparison [L3]

Compare the red-flag patterns in Coffee Day, Zee/Essel and Gensol. For each, identify which disclosure (Ind AS 24 note, CARO clause, Reg 31 pledge filings, contingent liability note, Reg 30 announcements) would have provided the earliest signal, and design a quarterly monitoring dashboard of five metrics that would catch similar patterns.

Q4. Interview case [L2]

An interviewer gives you a company where promoter pledges rose from 15% to 60% of holding in a year, royalty rates were raised from 1% to 2.5% of sales, and the auditor added a KAM on recoverability of loans to group companies. Structure a five-minute verbal answer covering what you would check, in what order, and what conclusion each finding could support.
