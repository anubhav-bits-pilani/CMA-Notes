Roadmap topic: Earnings Quality & Accounting Red Flags: Receivables growing faster than revenue, declining CFO/PAT ratio, frequent changes in depreciation policy, rising capital work-in-progress (CWIP)

Overview

Earnings quality is the degree to which reported profit reflects sustainable economic performance and converts into cash. High-quality earnings are persistent, backed by cash flow, and produced by conservative, consistently applied policies. Low-quality earnings depend on accruals that may reverse, judgements that are stretched, one-off or non-operating gains, or transactions with related parties. Earnings management sits on a spectrum: from legitimate choices within Ind AS, through aggressive but compliant estimates, to fraudulent reporting. The analyst's task is not to prove fraud but to identify where reported numbers are least reliable, quantify the potential overstatement, and adjust forecasts, valuations and credit views accordingly.

For the target roles this is core craft. An equity analyst who misses a receivables build or a useful-life extension will capitalise unsustainable earnings; a credit analyst who relies on PAT rather than CFO will overestimate debt-servicing capacity; an FP&A professional who extrapolates historical DSO creep will publish an optimistic cash forecast; a diligence professional will overpay. Your CMA background in cost and management accounting and audit gives you a head start in recognising how costs are capitalised, how inventories are valued and how provisions are estimated — the same mechanics that generate red flags.

The learning sequence starts with the two most robust quantitative screens: receivables and unbilled revenue growth versus revenue (s01), and cash conversion — CFO/PAT, accruals and the five-year cumulative test (s02), with their formal definitions in the concept notes on the balance sheet accrual ratio (c01), cash flow accrual ratio (c02) and CFO/PAT (c03). It then moves to the policy and capitalisation levers that move expense off the P&L: depreciation policy changes, Ind AS 23 borrowing costs and capitalisation into CWIP and intangibles (s03, c05), and the balance sheet holding areas of CWIP, capital advances and intangibles under development with Schedule III ageing (s04). Revenue recognition risks under Ind AS 115 — bill-and-hold, principal versus agent, variable consideration and channel stuffing — follow (s05, c04). Finally, the softer signals: other income, provision reversals, related party sales and finance-leadership turnover (s06). Each analysis links back to the auditor's report (where KAMs point) and forward to the Beneish M-score (which formalises several of these flags).

Related concepts to study

Sloan accruals, Piotroski F-score and Montier C-score

Sloan accrual anomaly; Piotroski F-score; Montier C-score

Sloan (1996, The Accounting Review) showed that the accrual component of earnings is less persistent than the cash component and that investors overweight it: high-accrual firms subsequently underperform. This underpins the accrual ratios in c01 and c02. Piotroski's F-score (2000) scores nine binary signals — positive ROA, positive CFO, improving ROA, CFO greater than net income, falling leverage, improving current ratio, no equity issuance, improving gross margin and improving asset turnover — and is used to separate strong from weak value stocks. Montier's C-score (2008) counts six "cooking the books" signals: a growing gap between net income and CFO, rising DSO, rising inventory days, rising other current assets relative to revenue, declining depreciation relative to gross PPE, and high total asset growth. The C-score maps almost one-to-one onto s01–s04 of this topic and is easy to build alongside the Beneish model.

Indian case studies

Case studies: Satyam (fictitious cash), DHFL, Coffee Day, Gensol (SEBI 2025 order)

Satyam (2009): the chairman's January 2009 letter admitted that ₹5,040 crore of the ₹5,361 crore cash and bank balances at 30 September 2008 were non-existent, along with fictitious accrued interest of ₹376 crore and overstated debtors of ₹490 crore; the lesson is to test whether cash earns a plausible yield and to rely only on direct bank confirmations. DHFL (2019): a housing finance company where loans to entities linked to promoters, routed through shell companies, were alleged to run into tens of thousands of crore; it became the first financial services company referred to insolvency under the IBC s.227 framework. CFO tests do not work for lenders; loan-book concentration and related-party exposure do. Coffee Day Enterprises: an investigation after the founder's death in 2019 found about ₹3,535 crore owed by a promoter-linked entity (MACEL) from group subsidiaries; SEBI penalised the company in 2023. Gensol Engineering (SEBI interim order, April 2025): term loans of about ₹977 crore from IREDA and PFC, including funds for 6,400 EVs, of which only about 4,704 were procured; funds were allegedly routed via a vendor to promoter-linked entities and personal use, and forged lender letters were reported. Lessons: verify capex against physical assets and vendor identity, and watch capital advances to related vendors.

Beneish M-score link

Topic 6 of this week implements the Beneish eight-variable M-score, which formalises most of this topic's red flags: DSRI (days sales in receivables index) captures s01; GMI (gross margin index) captures margin deterioration that creates pressure to manipulate; AQI (asset quality index) captures growth in non-current assets other than PPE, including capitalised costs and capital advances (s03, s04); SGI (sales growth index) captures growth pressure; DEPI (depreciation index) captures falling depreciation rates (s03, c05); SGAI captures overhead trends; TATA (total accruals to total assets) captures s02 and c01–c02; and LVGI captures leverage. When the M-score flags a company, the qualitative analysis in this topic explains which driver moved and whether it is benign.

Common pitfalls

Flagging growth-phase working capital mechanically

Flagging working-capital build in a growth phase without considering business model

The mistake is to treat any rise in receivables, inventory or a falling CFO/PAT as manipulation, ignoring that fast-growing companies must invest in working capital and that some business models (B2G contracting, project EPC, new distribution channels) carry structurally longer cycles. It happens because screens are mechanical and quick to run. The correct treatment is to separate the working capital explained by growth at constant days from the part explained by rising days, and to check the business model and customer mix (segment data, major customer disclosure, MD&A). Detect in review by asking whether the memo computes days (not just amounts) and whether it explains the build with business evidence before grading severity.

Missing unrecognised depreciation in CWIP

Ignoring CWIP that should have been capitalized, hiding depreciation

Analysts often accept CWIP as "investment for future growth" without asking whether the assets are already in use. Keeping a completed plant in CWIP avoids depreciation and lets borrowing costs continue to be capitalised, overstating profit on both counts. It is missed because CWIP sits in the balance sheet and the P&L shows no symptom. The correct treatment is to test capitalisation timing against Ind AS 16.20 and Ind AS 23.22 using production data, Reg 30 commercial production announcements, trial-run sales and the CWIP ageing schedule, and to quantify the depreciation and interest that would have been expensed. In review, look for an ageing analysis with a hidden-depreciation estimate for CWIP older than one or two years.

Single-year analysis

Relying on a single year rather than multi-year patterns

One year's CFO/PAT, DSO or accrual ratio is dominated by timing: a large March order, a tax refund, an acquisition. Analysts relying on one year either raise false alarms or miss a slow, persistent deterioration — which is how most Indian accounting problems actually developed. The correct practice is to compute every metric for at least five years, use cumulative measures (five-year CFO versus PAT), look at trends and turning points, and compare with peers over the same period. In review, check that the workbook has a five-year time series for each red-flag metric and that conclusions refer to patterns ("third consecutive year") rather than single data points.

Practice Questions

Q1. Five-year earnings quality review [L3]

A listed mid-cap capital goods company reports (₹ crore, FY 2020-21 to FY 2024-25): revenue 2,100, 2,350, 2,700, 3,050, 3,400; PAT 150, 175, 210, 250, 290; CFO 160, 120, 95, 70, 40; trade receivables 520, 640, 820, 1,040, 1,330; unbilled revenue 80, 130, 210, 300, 420; CWIP 90, 180, 340, 560, 790 (of which over 2 years in FY 2024-25: 430); depreciation 70, 74, 76, 75, 72; gross block 1,100, 1,180, 1,260, 1,320, 1,370; other income 12, 18, 31, 49, 72. In FY 2023-24 useful lives of plant were extended, reducing depreciation by ₹18 crore; two CFOs resigned in FY 2024-25. Compute DSO (including unbilled), annual and cumulative CFO/PAT, CWIP to gross block, implied depreciation rate and other income share of PAT for each year. Write a 300-word red-flag summary ranking the top four concerns with severity grades.

💡 Hint: Separate what growth explains from what days creep explains before ranking.

Q2. Accruals and Ind AS 115 integration [L2]

A consumer electronics company's FY 2024-25 data (₹ crore): PAT 420; CFO 150; CFI (–)610 including purchase of liquid funds (–)200 and capex (–)410; average NOA 2,900; Q4 revenue share 38% (prior 30%); rebates as % of gross sales 8.9% (prior 11.2%) on gross sales of ₹5,600 crore; DSO up from 48 to 71 days. Compute the adjusted CF accrual ratio and CFO/PAT, quantify the revenue effect of the rebate ratio change, and explain how the Ind AS 115 variable consideration constraint applies.

Q3. Depreciation and capitalisation adjustment for valuation [L3]

A listed cement company trades at 14x EV/EBITDA. In FY 2024-25 it (i) extended plant lives from 25 to 30 years (depreciation reduced by ₹110 crore), (ii) capitalised ₹95 crore of borrowing costs, of which ₹38 crore relates to a line that began commercial dispatches in November 2024 but remains in CWIP, and (iii) capitalised ₹22 crore of head office costs into CWIP as "pre-operative expenses". Reported EBITDA is ₹1,850 crore and PAT ₹760 crore (tax 25.17%). Compute adjusted EBITDA and PAT, and explain which adjustments affect EV/EBITDA and which affect only P/E.

Q4. Case analysis: Gensol pattern [L3]

Using the facts in the related-concepts case summary for Gensol, design the five red-flag tests from this topic (with the specific notes, schedules and filings you would have used) that could have detected the problem from the annual report and filings before the SEBI order, and explain which would have been most powerful and why.

Q5. Interview: quality of a strong quarter [L2]

An interviewer shows you a company whose Q4 PAT rose 35% on revenue growth of 12%, with other income up 140%, provisions written back of ₹28 crore, and a one-quarter fall in depreciation of 18% after a useful-life review. Walk through how you would decompose the PAT growth into core and non-core components and what you would say about sustainable earnings.
