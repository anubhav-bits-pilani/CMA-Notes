Key concept

CFO/PAT Cash Conversion Ratio

Concept: CFO/PAT = CFO ÷ PAT; sustained ratio below 1 needs explanation

Statement

\[ \text{CFO/PAT}_t = \frac{\text{Net cash flow from operating activities}_t}{\text{Profit after tax}_t} \]

where CFO is as reported under Ind AS 7 (indirect method for most Indian companies) and PAT is profit for the year (consolidated PAT including non-controlling interests when using consolidated CFO). A ratio sustained below 1 over several years requires an explanation in terms of working capital growth, business model or timing; without one, it indicates earnings not supported by cash.

Explanation

CFO starts from profit before tax and adds back non-cash charges (depreciation, amortisation, impairment), removes non-operating items (interest and dividend income, which Ind AS 7.33 places in investing; finance costs, placed in financing), adjusts for working capital changes, and deducts tax paid. For a steady-state manufacturer with no working capital growth, CFO should exceed PAT because depreciation is added back and finance cost is excluded from CFO. A ratio below 1 therefore means working capital is absorbing cash, non-cash income is inflating PAT, or both.

Acceptable reasons for a sub-1 ratio: rapid growth requiring receivables and inventory build in proportion to sales (working capital days stable); a deliberate one-off inventory build ahead of price increases; large tax payments relating to prior years; and project businesses with lumpy milestone collections. Unacceptable or suspect reasons: rising working capital days; accrued but uncollected income (interest on group loans, claims, unbilled revenue); non-cash gains (fair value, deferred tax credits) in PAT; and "other current assets" build-up without explanation.

The measure has limitations: it is meaningless when PAT is negative or near zero; it can be flattered by Ind AS 7 classification (interest paid in financing, lease principal in financing), by factoring receivables, by supplier finance extending payables, and by capitalising operating costs (which moves outflows to CFI). Pair it with FCF/PAT and the accrual ratios (c01, c02), and use multi-year cumulative versions to smooth timing.

Illustration

Year PAT (₹ crore) CFO (₹ crore) CFO/PAT Working capital days
FY 2021-22 150 190 1.27 62
FY 2022-23 180 160 0.89 70
FY 2023-24 215 140 0.65 81
FY 2024-25 255 120 0.47 94

Revenue grew 18% a year, but working capital days rose from 62 to 94, so the decline is not explained by growth alone. Had working capital days stayed at 62, working capital would have been about a third lower; the excess build is the unexplained portion and is the focus of the forensic review (receivables, inventory, advances).

Practice Questions

Q1. Diagnose a low ratio [L1]

A company reports PAT ₹300 crore, depreciation ₹80 crore, fair value gain on investments ₹60 crore (non-cash), increase in receivables ₹140 crore, increase in inventory ₹90 crore, increase in payables ₹50 crore, tax paid ₹95 crore against tax expense ₹100 crore, finance cost ₹40 crore and interest income ₹25 crore. Reconstruct CFO and compute CFO/PAT, identifying the two largest drivers of the gap.

💡 Hint: Start from PBT, not PAT, if you follow the Ind AS 7 indirect method.

Q2. Growth versus quality [L2]

Revenue grows from ₹1,000 crore to ₹1,300 crore; receivable days stay at 75; inventory days (on revenue) rise from 60 to 85; payable days stay at 50. PAT is ₹110 crore and non-cash charges net to ₹40 crore. Compute the working capital investment explained by growth and the portion explained by days creep, and the resulting CFO/PAT.

Q3. Interview question [L1]

Explain in under 120 words why a capital-intensive manufacturer should normally have CFO/PAT above 1, and name two legitimate and two suspect reasons for it to fall below 1.
