Subtopic note

Depreciation Changes, Borrowing Cost and Expense Capitalisation

Scope: Depreciation policy and useful-life changes; capitalisation of borrowing costs (Ind AS 23) and expenses into CWIP/intangibles

Why it matters

Depreciation, borrowing cost and capitalisation policies decide how much of a company's spending appears as an expense today versus an asset to be expensed later. Each is governed by judgement — useful lives, the start and end of capitalisation, what counts as "directly attributable" — and each can lift reported profit without any change in cash. Extending useful lives, capitalising interest on a stalled project, or parking salaries and overheads in CWIP or intangible assets under development are recurrent findings in Indian forensic reviews and NFRA financial reporting quality reviews. For modellers, these policies determine the depreciation and interest lines of the forecast; for valuation, capitalised costs inflate EBITDA and invested capital, distorting both multiples and ROCE.

Core notes

Depreciation policy and useful-life changes

Under Ind AS 16:

  • Useful life and residual value are reviewed at least at each financial year-end; changes are accounted for as a change in accounting estimate under Ind AS 8 (Ind AS 16.51).
  • Depreciation method must reflect the pattern of consumption of economic benefits and is reviewed at least at each year-end; a change is a change in estimate (Ind AS 16.60–61). Revenue-based depreciation is not appropriate (Ind AS 16.62A).
  • Component accounting: significant parts with different useful lives are depreciated separately (Ind AS 16.43–47).
  • Companies Act Schedule II prescribes indicative useful lives (Part C) and a residual value ordinarily not exceeding 5% of original cost. A company may use different lives or residual values, but must disclose the justification, supported by technical advice.

A change in estimate is applied prospectively (Ind AS 8.36–38): remaining carrying amount is depreciated over the revised remaining life from the date of change. Ind AS 8.39 requires disclosure of the nature and amount of the change affecting the current period and, where practicable, future periods.

Red flags: life extensions in a year when profits are under pressure; a change in method from WDV to SLM (common in older Indian companies) presented without quantification; residual values raised above 5% of cost; and depreciation as a percentage of opening gross block falling without a change in asset mix.

\[ \text{Depreciation rate (implied)} = \frac{\text{Depreciation expense}_t}{\text{Average gross block (PPE excl. land and CWIP)}_t} \]

\[ \text{Average implied life (years)} \approx \frac{1}{\text{Depreciation rate}} \]

Borrowing cost capitalisation (Ind AS 23)

Borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset (one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale) form part of the asset's cost (Ind AS 23.8).

Specific borrowings: actual costs less investment income on temporary investment of funds Ind AS 23.12 Check income netting

\[ \text{Capitalised interest share} = \frac{\text{Borrowing costs capitalised}}{\text{Borrowing costs capitalised} + \text{Finance costs expensed}} \]

A rising share while CWIP ages, or capitalisation continuing on projects listed under "temporarily suspended" in the CWIP ageing schedule, is a direct contradiction of Ind AS 23.20.

Capitalisation of expenses into CWIP and intangibles

PPE (Ind AS 16.16–17): cost includes purchase price, directly attributable costs of bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to operate as intended (employee benefits arising directly from construction, site preparation, installation, testing, professional fees), and decommissioning costs. Excluded (Ind AS 16.19): costs of opening a new facility, introducing a new product (advertising), conducting business in a new location or with a new class of customer (staff training), and administration and general overheads. Ind AS 16.20: recognition of costs ceases when the asset is capable of operating as intended; initial operating losses and relocation costs are excluded. Proceeds from sale of items produced before intended use go to profit or loss (Ind AS 16.20A).

Intangibles (Ind AS 38): research expenditure is expensed (Ind AS 38.54); development expenditure is capitalised only if all six criteria in Ind AS 38.57 are demonstrated — technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use or sell, how the asset will generate probable future economic benefits, availability of adequate resources, and ability to measure expenditure reliably. Internally generated brands, mastheads, customer lists and similar items cannot be recognised (Ind AS 38.63). Software development, pharma product development (post-filing), and EV or platform technology are common Indian areas.

Common capitalisation abuses:

  • "Pre-operative expenses pending allocation" in CWIP that include head office salaries, travel and marketing.
  • Development cost capitalised from the research phase, or capitalised for products never launched without impairment.
  • Repairs and maintenance capitalised as "improvements".
  • Software licence subscriptions (cloud SaaS) capitalised; under IFRIC agenda decisions on configuration and customisation costs in cloud computing arrangements, most such costs are expensed unless they create a separately identifiable asset the entity controls.

Illustration

A cement company (₹ crore): FY 2023-24 depreciation 640 on average gross block 12,800 (5.0%); FY 2024-25 depreciation 610 on average gross block 14,100 (4.3%). Note 3 discloses that useful lives of plant and machinery were revised from 25 to 30 years based on technical evaluation, reducing depreciation by ₹96 crore. Borrowing costs capitalised rose from ₹48 crore to ₹132 crore, while finance costs expensed fell from ₹310 crore to ₹270 crore; a ₹1,200 crore line commissioned trial production in October 2024 but remains in CWIP. PAT of ₹820 crore therefore includes about ₹96 crore from the life change and a portion of ₹132 crore of capitalised interest that may relate to a substantially complete asset. Adjusted for both (pre-tax ₹96 crore plus, say, six months' capitalised interest on the completed line of ₹40 crore, tax at 25.17%), PAT would be about ₹718 crore, 12% lower.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Genuine life extensions after technical studies (for example, telecom towers, power plants after refurbishment) are legitimate; check whether peers made similar changes and whether the technical basis is disclosed.
  • Schedule II deviation in either direction (shorter or longer than Part C) needs disclosure; shorter lives are conservative.
  • Ind AS 101 deemed cost at transition carries over historical choices; old revaluations may still affect depreciation.
  • Qualifying assets in inventory: real estate developers capitalise borrowing costs into inventory (projects under development); the same suspension and cessation rules apply.
  • Interest on general borrowings can be capitalised even if the company has large cash balances; this inflates profit where cash is idle.
  • Component of capitalised interest in CFO versus CFI: interest capitalised is usually shown within capex in CFI, so CFO looks higher by that amount.

Key takeaways

  • Useful-life and method changes are changes in estimate, applied prospectively, with Ind AS 8.39 disclosure of the amount; quantify them every time.
  • Track the implied depreciation rate on average gross block across years and peers.
  • Ind AS 23 capitalisation must suspend for stalled projects and cease when assets are substantially complete; compare capitalised interest with the CWIP ageing schedule.
  • Only directly attributable costs can be capitalised under Ind AS 16; development costs need all six Ind AS 38.57 criteria.
  • Adjust PAT, EBITDA and invested capital for aggressive capitalisation before computing multiples and ROCE.

Practice Questions

Q1. Quantify a useful-life change [L2]

A machine costing ₹600 crore was acquired on 1 April 2020 with a useful life of 15 years and nil residual value (straight line). On 1 April 2024 management revises the total useful life to 20 years and residual value to ₹30 crore. Compute depreciation for FY 2023-24 and FY 2024-25 and the effect of the change on FY 2024-25 PAT at a 25.17% tax rate, and draft the Ind AS 8.39 disclosure.

Q2. Borrowing cost capitalisation test [L2]

A company has specific project borrowings of ₹800 crore at 9.5% (temporarily invested surplus earned ₹6 crore during the year) and general borrowings of ₹1,200 crore at a weighted 10.2%. Expenditure on a qualifying asset funded from general borrowings averaged ₹450 crore during the year. Active development was suspended from 1 October 2024 to 31 March 2025 due to a land dispute (not a temporary delay inherent in the process). Compute the amount that may be capitalised for FY 2024-25 and identify which disclosure in the CWIP ageing schedule should be consistent with your answer.

💡 Hint: Suspension under Ind AS 23.20 applies to both specific and general borrowing costs for the suspended period.

Q3. CWIP capitalisation review [L2]

A pre-operative expenditure note shows the following amounts carried in CWIP for a greenfield plant: salaries of project engineers ₹14 crore; salaries of corporate finance staff allocated ₹6 crore; trial-run materials consumed ₹9 crore; trial-run sales ₹5 crore credited to CWIP; advertising for the new product ₹3 crore; consultancy for plant design ₹4 crore; travel of directors ₹1.5 crore. Classify each item under Ind AS 16 and compute the correct amount to remain in CWIP and the amount to be expensed or reclassified.

Q4. Implied depreciation rate screen [L1]

Compute the implied depreciation rate and average implied life for three years and comment: depreciation 210, 225, 214; average gross block excluding land and CWIP 3,000, 3,400, 3,900 (₹ crore).

Q5. Judgement case [L3]

A listed software products company capitalised ₹420 crore of development cost in FY 2024-25 (₹260 crore in FY 2023-24), representing 58% of R&D spend; amortisation was ₹90 crore; EBITDA was ₹610 crore. Two products under development since FY 2021-22 have not launched. Outline the Ind AS 38 tests you would apply, the adjustments to EBITDA and invested capital you would make for a peer comparison with a company that expenses all R&D, and the questions for management.
