Subtopic note

Ind AS 115 Risk Areas: Bill-and-Hold, Gross-Up, Variable Consideration, Stuffing

Scope: Ind AS 115 risk areas: bill-and-hold, principal vs agent gross-up, variable consideration, channel stuffing

Why it matters

Revenue is the largest number in the P&L and the most frequent subject of KAMs, restatements and SEBI orders. Ind AS 115 replaced risk-and-rewards with a control-based five-step model, but the judgement points remain: when control passes (bill-and-hold, consignment, installation), how much revenue is recognised (variable consideration, rebates, returns), and whose revenue it is (principal versus agent). Each can be stretched to accelerate or inflate revenue without changing cash. For equity analysts, gross-up inflates revenue and depresses margin percentages; for credit analysts, accelerated revenue builds receivables that may never convert; for modellers, understanding the revenue policy is essential before applying growth rates.

Core notes

Bill-and-hold (Ind AS 115 B79–B82)

A bill-and-hold arrangement is one where the entity bills the customer but retains physical possession until a later date. Control can transfer only if all of the following are met (B81):

  1. The reason for the arrangement is substantive (for example, the customer has requested it because it lacks space).
  2. The product has been identified separately as belonging to the customer.
  3. The product is currently ready for physical transfer to the customer.
  4. The entity cannot use the product or direct it to another customer.

The entity also considers whether it provides a custodial service that is a separate performance obligation to which part of the price is allocated (B82). Red flags: large period-end billing with goods in the entity's warehouse; "customer request" letters dated after year-end; products not segregated; goods later sold to another customer; bill-and-hold appearing for the first time in a weak quarter.

Principal versus agent (Ind AS 115 B34–B38)

The entity determines whether the nature of its promise is to provide the specified goods or services itself (principal: gross revenue) or to arrange for another party to provide them (agent: net revenue, i.e., fee or commission) (B34–B36). It first identifies the specified good or service and then assesses whether it controls that good or service before transfer to the customer (B34A–B35A). Indicators of control (B37):

  • The entity is primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise (acceptability of the goods or services).
  • The entity has inventory risk before transfer or after transfer of control (for example, on returns).
  • The entity has discretion in establishing the price.

Indicators support, but do not override, the control assessment. Credit risk is no longer an indicator. B38 addresses situations where another party assumes the entity's performance obligations.

Indian risk areas: e-commerce and marketplace platforms; travel and ticketing; trading and "merchant" businesses within manufacturing groups; IT services reselling third-party software and cloud; logistics and freight forwarding; staffing; solar EPC companies procuring modules for customers. Gross-up inflates revenue, revenue growth and sometimes valuation (for revenue-multiple-based valuations) while leaving profit unchanged.

\[ \text{Gross-up effect on margin} = \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Gross revenue}} \;\text{vs}\; \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Net revenue}} \]

Variable consideration (Ind AS 115.50–59)

Consideration that varies (discounts, rebates, refunds, credits, price concessions, incentives, performance bonuses, penalties, rights of return) is estimated using the expected value or most likely amount method, whichever better predicts the amount (115.53). The estimate is included only to the extent it is highly probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue will not occur when the uncertainty is resolved (the constraint, 115.56–58). Rights of return create a refund liability and an asset for the right to recover products (B20–B27).

Red flags: rebate and discount accruals growing slower than revenue in dealer-heavy businesses; reversal of prior-year rebate accruals into revenue; claims and variations recognised in EPC contracts before customer acceptance; volume-tier rebates accrued at a lower tier than the trajectory suggests; returns provisions released.

Channel stuffing

Channel stuffing is pushing excess inventory to distributors or dealers near period-end, often with extended credit, extra discounts, or return rights, to meet targets. Under Ind AS 115 it may be technically recognised if control passes, but the incentives create variable consideration and returns that must be estimated, and side agreements may mean control has not passed. Evidence pattern:

Indicator Where to look
Q4 revenue share rising Reg 33 quarterly results
DSO and receivables from distributors rising Receivables ageing; s01 metrics
Credit notes, returns and discounts spiking in Q1 next year Q1 results; revenue reconciliation (Ind AS 115.126 disaggregation)
Distributor inventory weeks rising Earnings call commentary; channel checks
Trade incentive liabilities moving inconsistently Other current liabilities; refund liabilities
Revenue from related-party distributors Ind AS 24 note

Ind AS 115 disclosures to read: disaggregation of revenue (115.114), reconciliation of contract price to revenue recognised (an Indian addition requiring adjustments for discounts, rebates, refunds and credits, Ind AS 115.126AA), contract balances and their changes (115.116–118), and significant judgements (115.123).

Illustration

A listed consumer appliances company reports FY 2024-25 revenue of ₹6,400 crore, up 14%, with Q4 revenue of ₹2,310 crore (36% of the year, against 29% in FY 2023-24). Trade receivables rose 41%. The Ind AS 115.126AA reconciliation shows gross price ₹7,150 crore less discounts and rebates ₹750 crore (10.5% of gross, versus 12.8% prior year). Q1 FY 2025-26 revenue fell 9% year-on-year with "higher channel inventory" cited on the call. Had rebates stayed at 12.8% of gross, revenue would have been about ₹165 crore lower. Together, the indicators suggest Q4 loading with under-accrued incentives; the analyst should normalise revenue and margin and look for a KAM on rebate accruals.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Customer-requested bill-and-hold is common in capital goods and defence; documentation and segregation evidence decide legitimacy.
  • Consignment arrangements (B77–B78) look like sales to distributors but control has not passed; revenue is recognised only on sale to end customers.
  • Principal for some components, agent for others in bundled contracts; each specified good or service is assessed separately.
  • Significant financing component (115.60–65) in extended-credit sales: revenue should be at the cash selling price with interest recognised separately; ignoring it overstates revenue.
  • Constraint and new products: limited history makes "highly probable" harder to support; aggressive estimates appear in early years.
  • Government incentives (PLI, export incentives) are not revenue from contracts with customers; some companies present them in revenue from operations, inflating "revenue".

Key takeaways

  • Bill-and-hold requires all four B81 criteria; custodial services may be a separate performance obligation.
  • Principal versus agent turns on control before transfer; indicators in B37 support the assessment; gross-up inflates revenue, not profit.
  • Variable consideration is estimated and constrained to amounts highly probable of no significant reversal.
  • Channel stuffing shows up in Q4 revenue share, receivables, Q1 reversals and rebate accrual ratios.
  • Read the Ind AS 115 disaggregation, the Indian 115.126AA price reconciliation, contract balances and significant judgements every year.

Practice Questions

Q1. Bill-and-hold test [L2]

On 28 March 2025 a capital equipment maker billed ₹85 crore for two turbines completed and tested, at the customer's written request dated 20 March 2025 because its site is not ready. The turbines are tagged with the customer's name in the maker's yard, but the maker's sales team has offered one of them to another customer for faster delivery. The contract includes storage for six months at no extra charge. Evaluate whether revenue can be recognised at 31 March 2025 under B79–B82 and what, if any, portion should be deferred.

💡 Hint: Test each B81 criterion separately, then consider B82.

Q2. Principal versus agent and margins [L2]

An e-commerce company reports revenue of ₹4,800 crore, including ₹3,100 crore gross value of third-party sellers' goods where it does not hold inventory, sellers set prices and sellers handle returns; its commission on these is 12%. Gross profit reported is ₹980 crore. Recompute revenue and gross margin on an agent basis for the third-party sales and comment on how this affects EV/revenue comparisons with peers.

Q3. Rebate accrual analysis [L2]

Gross sales and rebate accruals for three years (₹ crore): 5,200 / 610; 5,900 / 690; 6,800 / 640. Volume-tier rebate schemes were unchanged. Compute rebates as % of gross sales, the revenue effect if the FY 2022-23 ratio had been maintained in later years, and state the questions you would put to the auditor's KAM response.

Q4. Channel stuffing case [L3]

A listed FMCG company's Q4 FY 2024-25 revenue grew 22% year-on-year against 6% for the first nine months. Receivables from distributors rose 55%, distributors were offered 60-day credit instead of 21 days for March purchases, and Q1 FY 2025-26 shows a 14% decline with "realignment of trade inventory". Write a 250-word forensic finding including the Ind AS 115 analysis (variable consideration, significant financing component, and whether control passed), the quantified effect, and a severity grade.
