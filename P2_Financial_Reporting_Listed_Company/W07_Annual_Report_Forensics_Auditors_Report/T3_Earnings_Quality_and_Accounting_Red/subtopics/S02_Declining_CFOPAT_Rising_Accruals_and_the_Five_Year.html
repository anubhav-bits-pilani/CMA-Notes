Subtopic note

Declining CFO/PAT, Rising Accruals and the Five-Year Cash Test

Scope: Declining CFO/PAT and rising accruals; cumulative 5-year CFO vs PAT

Why it matters

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. The gap between reported profit and operating cash flow — accruals — is where both legitimate timing differences and manipulation live. Sloan (1996) showed that firms with high accruals subsequently earn lower returns because accrual-driven earnings are less persistent. In India, the companies that later collapsed or restated (Satyam, DHFL, several infrastructure and realty groups) typically showed years of reported profits without matching cash generation. A falling CFO/PAT ratio and a cumulative five-year CFO well below cumulative PAT are among the most robust red flags because they are hard to fake for long: eventually receivables, inventory or capitalised costs must convert to cash or be written off.

Core notes

Measures

\[ \text{CFO/PAT} = \frac{\text{Net cash from operating activities}}{\text{Profit after tax}} \]

\[ \text{Cumulative cash conversion (5-year)} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{5} \text{CFO}_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{5} \text{PAT}_t} \]

\[ \text{Total accruals} = \text{PAT} - \text{CFO} \]

Balance sheet and cash flow accrual ratios scaled by average net operating assets are defined in the concept notes (c01, c02). Variants used in practice:

  • CFO/EBITDA (pre-tax, pre-interest): avoids distortion from interest and tax classification choices; Indian companies typically show 70–90% in steady state for manufacturing.
  • FCF/PAT, where \( \text{FCF} = \text{CFO} - \text{Capex} \): captures capitalisation of costs that move outflows from CFO to CFI.
  • Adjusted CFO: CFO less interest paid (Ind AS 7.33, unlike the IAS 7 choice, requires entities other than financial institutions to classify interest paid as financing and interest and dividends received as investing, so Indian CFO is structurally higher than that of IFRS or US GAAP peers that show interest in operating), less lease principal payments (Ind AS 116 moves these to financing, flattering CFO).

Ind AS 7 classification issues that distort CFO

Choice Effect on CFO Adjustment
Interest paid in financing activities (mandatory for non-financial entities under Ind AS 7.33) CFO higher than IFRS/US GAAP peers reporting interest in operating Deduct interest paid for cross-border comparability
Lease payments (principal) in financing under Ind AS 116 CFO higher than pre-2019 Deduct lease principal and interest
Interest and dividends received in investing (Ind AS 7.33) CFO lower for cash-rich companies Standardise when comparing with peers using other classifications
Factoring / supplier finance Factoring raises CFO; supplier finance delays outflows Reverse non-recourse factoring and supplier-finance extension of payment terms
Capitalising expenses into CWIP/intangibles Outflow moved from CFO to CFI Use FCF/PAT
Advances from customers CFO higher (timing) Track contract liabilities

IASB added supplier finance arrangement disclosures to IAS 7 and IFRS 7 in 2023; check whether and from when the corresponding Ind AS amendments have been notified by MCA before relying on such disclosures in an Indian filing.

Interpreting CFO/PAT

  • Above 1 is normal for capital-intensive firms (depreciation is a non-cash charge in PAT) and for businesses with negative working capital (FMCG, quick-service retail).
  • Below 1 for one year can be working-capital investment in growth, a large Q4 sale, or tax timing.
  • Sustained below 1, particularly with falling ratio and rising receivables/inventory/other current assets, indicates profit that is not converting. For a manufacturing company, cumulative five-year CFO/PAT below about 0.7 deserves explanation; below 0.5 is a serious flag.
  • Negative CFO with positive PAT for two or more years is a severe flag outside financial services.

Banks and NBFCs are excluded from this analysis because lending is an operating activity; loan growth produces negative CFO by design.

Illustration

A listed infrastructure developer (₹ crore):

Year PAT CFO CFO/PAT Capex FCF
FY 2020-21 310 240 0.77 180 60
FY 2021-22 360 190 0.53 260 (70)
FY 2022-23 420 150 0.36 310 (160)
FY 2023-24 480 95 0.20 390 (295)
FY 2024-25 540 (60) (0.11) 420 (480)
Total 2,110 615 0.29 1,560 (945)

Five-year cumulative CFO is 29% of cumulative PAT, and the trend is monotonic. Reported profits of ₹2,110 crore have been financed by ₹945 crore of negative free cash flow plus dividends, which means net debt has absorbed the gap. Where did the accruals go? Typically unbilled revenue, receivables from related parties, claims recognised in revenue, and capital advances. This is a priority finding: the analyst should trace each balance sheet build-up and treat profit as unverified until the cash appears.

Excel implementation

CFO_PAT: =IFERROR(CFO / PAT, "n.m.")
Cum5_CFO_PAT: =SUM(OFFSET(CFO_t, 0, -4, 1, 5)) / SUM(OFFSET(PAT_t, 0, -4, 1, 5))
Flag: =IF(AND(Cum5_CFO_PAT < 0.7, SLOPE(CFO_PAT_Range, Year_Range) < 0), "LOW AND FALLING CASH CONVERSION", "")

Prefer INDEX-based ranges over OFFSET in production models for auditability (OFFSET is volatile).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Exceptional non-cash gains (fair value gains, gains on deconsolidation, deferred tax credits) inflate PAT; use PAT before exceptional items or EBITDA-based measures.
  • Losses: CFO/PAT is meaningless when PAT is negative; use CFO/EBITDA or absolute CFO.
  • Working capital in a growth phase can legitimately depress CFO for several years (see topic pitfall); check whether working capital days, not just amounts, are rising.
  • Project and real estate businesses (Ind AS 115 over-time recognition) have lumpy CFO; use five-year or full-project-cycle measures.
  • Tax refunds and one-off collections can lift CFO temporarily; check notes for large one-offs.
  • Group cash pooling: standalone CFO may look poor because cash is swept to the parent; consolidated CFO is more informative.

Key takeaways

  • CFO/PAT below 1 for one year is a question; sustained and falling below 1 is a red flag.
  • Five-year cumulative CFO/PAT smooths timing noise and is hard to manipulate.
  • Standardise Ind AS 7 classification choices (interest, leases, factoring, supplier finance) before comparing.
  • Use FCF/PAT to capture costs capitalised into CWIP or intangibles.
  • Always trace where the accruals went: receivables, unbilled, inventory, advances, capitalised costs.
  • Exclude banks and NBFCs; use EBITDA-based or absolute measures when PAT is negative.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute and interpret cash conversion [L1]

Five years of PAT (₹ crore): 120, 140, 165, 190, 220; CFO: 135, 110, 95, 80, 60. Compute annual CFO/PAT, the five-year cumulative ratio, and total accruals for each year. State your conclusion in two sentences.

Q2. Standardise CFO [L2]

Company A (Ind AS) reports CFO of ₹840 crore; interest paid of ₹160 crore and lease payments of ₹110 crore (principal ₹80 crore, interest ₹30 crore) are in financing activities, and non-recourse factoring reduced receivables by ₹140 crore during the year. Company B (an IFRS-reporting overseas peer) reports CFO of ₹690 crore with interest paid (including lease interest) in operating activities, lease principal of ₹60 crore in financing, and no factoring. Both have PAT of ₹700 crore. Compute standardised CFO (after all interest, all lease payments and excluding factoring) and CFO/PAT for both and explain which company has better cash conversion.

💡 Hint: Treat factoring proceeds as a financing inflow for analysis.

Q3. Trace the accruals [L2]

Over three years a company's cumulative PAT was ₹1,350 crore and cumulative CFO ₹420 crore. Over the same period: trade receivables rose ₹380 crore, unbilled revenue ₹290 crore, inventories ₹110 crore, loans and advances to related parties ₹160 crore (classified as operating), trade payables rose ₹90 crore, and non-cash income from fair value gains was ₹70 crore. Build a reconciliation of the PAT–CFO gap and identify the two items you would investigate first.

Q4. Interview case [L3]

An interviewer gives you a listed specialty retailer with PAT CAGR of 28% over five years, cumulative CFO/PAT of 0.62, inventory days rising from 95 to 140, and store count up from 210 to 480. Present a balanced view: the case that the cash shortfall is growth-driven, the case that it is an earnings-quality problem, and the three data points that would decide between them.
