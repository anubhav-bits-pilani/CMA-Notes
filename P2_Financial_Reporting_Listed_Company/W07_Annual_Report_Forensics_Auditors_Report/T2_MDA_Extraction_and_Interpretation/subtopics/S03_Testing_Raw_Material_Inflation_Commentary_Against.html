Subtopic note

Testing Raw Material Inflation Commentary Against Gross Margin

Scope: Raw material inflation commentary vs actual gross margin; pricing actions and pass-through lag

Why it matters

Raw material commentary is the most common MD&A explanation for margin movement, and the most often abused. "Unprecedented input cost inflation" is used to excuse margin compression; "benign raw material environment" is rarely credited as the reason margins expanded. For an analyst building a forecast, the relationship between input prices, the company's pricing actions and the lag between them drives quarterly margin estimates in sectors such as FMCG, paints, auto components, cables, tyres, chemicals and steel. Testing the commentary against the reported gross margin, peer margins and commodity indices tells you whether management's explanation is true, partial or misleading, and it gives you the pass-through parameters for the model.

Core notes

Defining gross margin from a Schedule III P&L

Indian P&Ls are presented by nature, so gross margin must be constructed:

\[ \text{Gross margin \%} = \frac{\text{Revenue from operations} - \text{COGS}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \times 100 \]

\[ \text{COGS} = \text{Cost of materials consumed} + \text{Purchases of stock-in-trade} + \text{Changes in inventories of FG, WIP and stock-in-trade} \]

Consistency points: use revenue net of GST (Schedule III revenue is net of GST; excise duty, where it existed pre-2017, needs adjustment for comparisons); decide whether to include power and fuel, packing materials or freight (some companies include packing in materials, others in other expenses); and keep the definition constant across years and peers. Contribution margin (revenue less all variable costs) is often a better analytical metric than gross margin for process industries.

Price, volume and cost bridges

A gross profit bridge between two periods:

Component Formula
Volume effect \( (V_1 - V_0) \times \text{GP per unit}_0 \)
Price effect \( (P_1 - P_0) \times V_1 \)
Material cost effect \( -(C_1 - C_0) \times V_1 \)
Mix and other Balancing figure

where \( V \) is volume, \( P \) is realisation per unit and \( C \) is material cost per unit. If volume and realisation are not disclosed, use earnings call data or segment commentary.

Pass-through and lag

Pass-through ratio measures how much of a cost change is recovered through price:

\[ \text{Pass-through} = \frac{\Delta \text{Realisation per unit}}{\Delta \text{Material cost per unit}} \]

A ratio of 1 (rupee for rupee) maintains gross profit per unit but compresses gross margin % when costs rise. To hold margin %, realisation must rise by the same percentage as material cost per unit, i.e. \( \frac{\Delta P}{P_0} = \frac{\Delta C}{C_0} \), which requires a rupee pass-through of \( \frac{P_0}{C_0} \), greater than 1. This is why companies that pass through costs "fully" in rupee terms still report lower margin percentages in inflationary years.

Pass-through lag arises from contract structures and market power:

  • Formula-linked contracts (auto components to OEMs, cables to utilities, packaging to FMCG): prices reset quarterly or half-yearly against a commodity index with a one-quarter lag.
  • Discretionary pricing (FMCG, paints, consumer durables): price increases are taken in steps after cost increases, limited by competition and demand elasticity; grammage cuts are an alternative.
  • Spot-linked businesses (steel, chemicals): prices move with the market, sometimes leading cost.
  • Inventory effect: with weighted average or FIFO costing (Ind AS 2 prohibits LIFO), inventory bought at lower prices flows through COGS after a lag, so rising prices initially improve margins (inventory gains) and falling prices hurt (inventory losses). Management may label inventory gains as "operational efficiency".

Testing the commentary

  1. Index test: compare the company's material cost per unit (or material cost as % of revenue) with the relevant commodity index (crude derivatives, TiO2, rubber, copper, palm oil) averaged over the period, shifted for inventory holding days.
  2. Peer test: if peers with similar input baskets show stable margins while the company's fell, the explanation is incomplete — look at mix, pricing power or one-offs.
  3. Symmetry test: did management credit raw material deflation when margins expanded? Asymmetric attribution is a narrative-bias flag.
  4. Quarterly test: use Reg 33 quarterly results to observe the lag; a margin trough one quarter after the cost peak followed by recovery supports a lag story.
  5. Inventory test: a sharp rise in inventory days around a cost upcycle may indicate stockpiling (legitimate) or inventory valuation gains (temporary).

Illustration

A tyre company states: "Natural rubber prices rose 22% during the year; we took price increases of 6% which partially offset inflation." Data (₹ crore): revenue 9,800 → 10,600; cost of materials consumed 5,880 → 6,890; volume up 2%. Gross margin fell from 40.0% to 35.0%. Implied realisation growth \( \frac{1.0816}{1.02} - 1 = 6.0\% \), consistent with the claim. Material cost per unit rose \( \frac{6{,}890 / 1.02}{5{,}880} - 1 = 14.9\% \), less than the 22% rubber increase because rubber is only part of the basket and inventory lagged. The narrative is broadly true, but a 14.9% cost increase passed through as 6% means a rupee pass-through of about 0.67 per unit (\( \frac{0.06 \times P_0}{0.149 \times C_0} \) with \( C_0 = 0.6 P_0 \)). For FY 2025-26 modelling, assume further price increases or margin recovery only if management guides to them and peers act similarly.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Hedging gains and losses on commodities may sit in other income, other expenses or cost of materials depending on hedge accounting under Ind AS 109; reclassify for a consistent gross margin.
  • Purchases of stock-in-trade for trading-heavy companies distort margin when trading share changes; separate manufacturing and trading margins.
  • Government levies and export incentives (RoDTEP, duty drawback) may be in other operating revenue, inflating "gross margin".
  • Mix shift (premiumisation) can mask cost inflation; volume and value growth together reveal it.
  • Related-party input purchases from promoter entities at non-market prices distort the cost story (see related party topic).
  • Forex: imported inputs priced in US dollars make rupee depreciation part of "raw material inflation"; separate the two where disclosed.

Key takeaways

  • Construct gross margin consistently from Schedule III lines; decide and document treatment of packing, power and freight.
  • Full rupee pass-through still compresses margin %; maintaining margin % requires proportional price increases.
  • Pass-through lags depend on contract structure, market power and inventory costing.
  • Test commentary with index, peer, symmetry, quarterly and inventory tests.
  • Use the tested pass-through ratio and lag as explicit model parameters.

Practice Questions

Q1. Construct gross margin [L1]

From the following P&L lines (₹ crore) compute gross margin for both years: revenue from operations 6,240 and 6,930; cost of materials consumed 3,520 and 3,980; purchases of stock-in-trade 410 and 520; changes in inventories (–)60 and 45. State the sign convention you used for changes in inventories.

Q2. Evaluate a margin explanation [L2]

A paint company's MD&A says "gross margin declined 290 bp due to raw material inflation". Data: crude-linked raw material index up 8% on average; titanium dioxide prices down 5%; company volume growth 12%, value growth 7%; peers' gross margins declined 40–80 bp. Evaluate the explanation, identify the likely missing factor, and propose two further data points to confirm it.

💡 Hint: Compare volume and value growth before blaming input costs.

Q3. Model a pass-through lag [L3]

An auto component maker passes through steel cost changes to OEMs with a one-quarter lag at 100% in rupee terms. Steel is 45% of revenue at the base quarter; revenue is ₹1,000 crore per quarter at constant volume; base gross margin 32%. Steel prices rise 10% in Q1, stay flat in Q2, and fall 8% in Q3. Build a quarterly table for Q1–Q4 showing revenue, material cost, gross profit and gross margin %, and explain the pattern you observe.

Q4. Inventory gain identification [L2]

A chemicals company holds 75 days of raw material inventory and uses weighted average costing. Input prices rose 18% over the last two quarters of FY 2024-25 and its gross margin expanded 220 bp while peers were flat. Management calls this "operational excellence". Explain how inventory costing could account for the expansion, and how you would treat it in a normalised margin for valuation.
