Roadmap topic: Beneish M-Score & Altman Z-Score: Implementing quantitative earnings manipulation and bankruptcy risk scoring models in Excel

Overview

The Beneish M-score and the Altman Z-score are the two quantitative screens most often used next to a qualitative forensic review. The M-score estimates how likely it is that reported earnings have been manipulated. It is built from eight year-on-year indices that mirror the red flags of Week 7 topic 3: receivables growing faster than sales, margins falling, soft assets rising, high growth, slowing depreciation, rising SG&A intensity, accruals and leverage. The Z-score family (Z, Z' and Z'') estimates bankruptcy or distress risk from liquidity, cumulative profitability, earning power, the equity cushion and asset turnover. One model asks whether the numbers can be trusted, the other whether the company can survive. Together they make a compact quantitative layer for a credit or equity risk memo.

These scores come up in the target roles in four ways. Bank and NBFC early-warning frameworks use them in credit monitoring. Research desks run them as forensic screens across coverage universes. Internal audit and FP&A teams use them for counterparty and vendor risk. In interviews you will often be asked to build one in Excel on the spot. The models came from US data, so most of the work for Indian companies is in mapping and judgement. Schedule III uses a nature-of-expense format, so SG&A has to be approximated with a proxy. Ind AS 116 lease liabilities and ROU assets must be treated the same way in every variable. "Other equity" is not retained earnings. Financial companies are outside both models altogether. A score that ignores these points is easy to attack, while a score that documents them and shows the drivers carries weight.

Work through the material in this order. s01 sets out the eight Beneish variables and the Schedule III mapping for years t and t−1. c01, c03 and c04 give the equation and the variable definitions, and c02 gives the cut-offs. s02 covers the three Altman variants, with c05 (Z) and c06 (Z''). s03 fixes the Indian proxies. s04 specifies the Excel build: input block, variable block, score, flags, trend and peers. s05 sets the limits: excluded sectors and false positives in high-growth companies. s06 makes the result defensible through driver decomposition and tests of alternative proxies. The output feeds the week's exercise (an M-score for two years) and the Saturday memo, where the scores support the qualitative findings rather than replace them.

Related concepts to study

Piotroski F-score, Ohlson O-score, Montier C-score

Piotroski F-score, Ohlson O-score, Montier C-score as complementary screens

The Piotroski F-score (2000) adds nine binary signals on profitability (positive ROA, positive CFO, improving ROA, CFO greater than net income), leverage and liquidity (falling leverage, improving current ratio, no equity issuance) and efficiency (improving gross margin, improving asset turnover). Scores of 8–9 indicate strength. The Ohlson O-score (1980) is a logit bankruptcy model with nine variables, including size, leverage, working capital, current liabilities to current assets, net income trends and funds from operations. It is an alternative to Altman when you want a probability estimate. The Montier C-score (2008) counts six "cooking the books" signals: a widening gap between net income and CFO, rising DSO, rising DSI, rising other current assets, falling depreciation relative to gross PPE, and high asset growth. It overlaps with Beneish but is simpler and binary. If M and C agree, the flag becomes more credible.

Retained earnings proxy under Ind AS

Retained earnings proxy under Ind AS: "Other equity" includes OCI and other reserves; use retained earnings component where disclosed

Schedule III Division II shows a single "Other equity" line on the balance sheet. The statement of changes in equity and the other equity note split it into securities premium, general reserve, retained earnings (surplus), capital reserve, capital redemption reserve, debenture redemption reserve, share-based payment reserve, and OCI components (FVOCI equity reserve, cash flow hedge reserve, foreign currency translation reserve, remeasurement of defined benefit plans). Altman's X2 is meant to capture cumulative earned profit. Use the retained earnings component. Adding the general reserve is defensible because it came from profit transfers under the old Companies Act regime. Exclude securities premium, capital reserves and OCI reserves. Apply the same choice every year and to every peer, and note any Ind AS transition adjustments taken directly to retained earnings.

Model hygiene and dashboards

Model hygiene (inputs/calcs/outputs separation, checks) → Week 9; score dashboard → Week 12

The s04 build is a small version of the modelling standards in Week 9. Inputs are hard-coded with their source. The calculation sheets hold only formulas, with one consistent formula per row. Outputs are linked, and a checks sheet returns one master TRUE/FALSE. Proxy switches are named cells, and coefficients sit in a labelled block rather than inside formulas. In Week 12 the same data is unpivoted in Power Query into a long table of company, line item, fiscal year and value. The variables are then written as DAX measures, which allows a dashboard with score trends, zone flags, driver tornado charts and peer rankings. Keep the Excel workbook as the audit trail behind the dashboard so that every number can be traced back to an annual report page.

Qualitative red flags behind score drivers

Qualitative red flags to explain score drivers → w7_t3

A score shows where to look, and the explanation comes from Week 7 topic 3. A high DSRI points to the receivables and unbilled revenue analysis and to the Ind AS 115 risk areas (bill-and-hold, variable consideration, channel stuffing). A high TATA calls for the CFO/PAT and accrual-ratio analysis over several years. A high AQI points to CWIP, capital advances, capitalised borrowing costs and intangibles under development. A high DEPI calls for a review of changes in useful life and depreciation method under Ind AS 16 and Ind AS 8. Each score driver in the memo should be paired with the qualitative evidence and the note or page reference that explains it. Drivers you cannot explain become questions for management.

Common pitfalls

Applying the models to financials or using original Z for non-manufacturers

Applying either model to banks/NBFCs or using the original Z for non-manufacturing Indian firms. Analysts run a screen across a whole index or a whole loan book and include banks, NBFCs and insurers. For these companies the variables have no meaning: receivables are the loan book, there is no gross margin and leverage is the business model. Analysts also apply the original Z to IT, retail or infrastructure companies because it is the best-known formula. In those companies X5 (asset turnover) and market-value X4 distort the result. The cause is usually automation without a sector filter. Use a sector exclusion list, apply Z'' to non-manufacturers and use CAMELS or ECL metrics for financial companies. In review, check the sector of every scored company and which Altman variant was used.

Using total Other equity as RE or inconsistent ROU depreciation

Using total "Other equity" as retained earnings, or omitting depreciation of ROU assets inconsistently in DEPI. Taking total other equity for X2 is convenient because it is a single line on the balance sheet. It overstates cumulative profit for companies with large securities premium, which is typical after IPOs and QIPs, so Z looks safer than it is. In DEPI, analysts often put ROU assets into PPE but take depreciation from the PPE note only. That lowers the depreciation rate and raises DEPI, or the reverse happens if the treatment differs between the two years. Take X2 from the retained earnings component in the other equity note. Treat ROU assets, ROU depreciation and lease liabilities consistently across AQI, DEPI and LVGI in both years. In review, reconcile X2 to the statement of changes in equity and the depreciation input to the PPE and ROU notes.

Treating a score breach as proof of fraud

Treating a score breach as proof of fraud rather than a trigger for investigation. An M-score above −1.78, or a Z in the distress zone, is easy to headline, and junior analysts or media reports sometimes present it as a finding of manipulation. The models are probabilistic. They were estimated on old US samples, they produce false positives for growing companies, and they miss whole categories of fraud. Report a breach as a trigger and decompose the drivers. Test alternative proxies, compare with peers and look for the qualitative evidence before rating severity. Use careful language such as "consistent with" or "warrants investigation". In review, reject any memo that relies only on the score, and check that each driver is linked to note-level evidence.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full scoring case for a mid-cap manufacturer [L3]

Narmada Polymers Ltd (NSE-listed, consolidated, ₹ crore) reports for FY23/FY24/FY25: revenue 2,400/2,900/3,800; trade receivables 360/470/860; contract assets 0/0/0; COGS proxy 1,680/2,060/2,780; employee benefits 150/170/200; other expenses 380/450/560 (of which power and fuel 120/140/170, freight 90/110/140); depreciation (incl. ROU) 110/115/112; total current assets 1,100/1,350/1,900; PPE incl. ROU 1,300/1,380/1,450; CWIP 80/200/520; intangibles under development 20/60/180; total assets 2,600/3,150/4,350; current liabilities 700/850/1,250; non-current borrowings 400/520/850; non-current lease liabilities 60/60/70; PAT from continuing operations 190/230/310; CFO 170/150/40; retained earnings 600/790/1,050; securities premium 200/200/600; PBT before exceptional items 260/310/410; finance costs 40/50/80; book equity 1,300/1,500/2,050; total liabilities 1,300/1,650/2,300; market cap 3,000/4,200/6,500.

  1. Compute the eight Beneish variables and M for FY24 and FY25 using the refined SGA proxy and PPE including ROU (CWIP as a soft asset).
  2. Recompute M for FY25 with CWIP inside PPE and comment.
  3. Compute Z and Z'' for FY25, and recompute Z using total other equity for X2.
  4. Decompose the FY25 M-score drivers and link each to a qualitative red flag you would investigate.
  5. Write a 150-word conclusion with severity rating.

Q2. Sector screen design [L3]

You are asked to run M-score and Altman screens across the Nifty Midcap 150 for a fund's risk committee. Design the process: sector exclusions and variant selection, data sourcing and mapping rules, proxy switches, checks, thresholds, peer normalisation and the output format. Explain how you would prevent false positives from high-growth sectors dominating the flagged list.

Q3. Retail versus manufacturing comparison [L2]

A listed apparel retailer (₹ crore): current assets 2,200; current liabilities 2,600 (including current lease liabilities 400); retained earnings 500; securities premium 1,800; total assets 7,000 (ROU assets 2,500); EBIT 520; market cap 15,000; book equity 2,900; total liabilities 4,100; revenue 8,400. A listed tyre manufacturer: current assets 4,000; current liabilities 3,200; retained earnings 3,500; total assets 9,000; EBIT 900; market cap 8,000; book equity 4,800; total liabilities 4,200; revenue 10,500.

  1. Compute Z and Z'' for both.
  2. Explain which variant you would rely on for each and why.
  3. Show the effect of using total other equity for X2 on the retailer's scores.

Q4. Interview build test [L2]

In a 30-minute interview test you are given two years of Schedule III statements for a listed FMCG company. Describe, step by step, how you would structure the Excel sheet. List the eight formulas, explain how you would flag results against both cut-offs, and say what checks you would show the interviewer to demonstrate that the score is reliable.
