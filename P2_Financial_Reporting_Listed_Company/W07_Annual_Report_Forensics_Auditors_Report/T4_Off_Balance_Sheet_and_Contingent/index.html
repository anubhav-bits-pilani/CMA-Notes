Roadmap topic: Off-Balance-Sheet & Contingent Liabilities: Dissecting corporate guarantees, disputed tax demands (GST/Income Tax), and pending litigations in Notes to Accounts

Overview

Reported debt is the floor of a company's obligations, not the ceiling. Indian annual reports carry a second layer of exposures in the notes: claims not acknowledged as debt (income tax, GST, customs, civil and arbitration claims), corporate guarantees for subsidiaries and related parties, export obligations, take-or-pay contracts, NCI put options, letters of credit and supplier finance. Some of these are partly on the balance sheet at a fraction of their exposure (financial guarantees at fair value), some are disclosure-only (contingent liabilities), and some are neither recognised nor labelled as contingent (supplier finance presented as trade payables). This topic teaches you to find each layer, test management's classification, and convert it into analytical adjustments.

For FP&A, credit and equity roles in India, this matters in three places. In credit appraisal, lenders and rating analysts compute contingent liabilities as a share of net worth and add stressed guarantees to adjusted debt. In equity valuation, probability-weighted exposures become debt-like items in the EV-to-equity bridge and drive downside scenarios. In forensic and audit review, the boundary between provision and contingent liability is an earnings lever — the classification of one arbitration award or tax demand can swing EBITDA and covenant headroom. Your CMA audit and GST/TDS background gives you an edge: you can read a demand order's section and forum and judge its real probability.

The learning sequence runs from rules to reading to quantification. Start with s01 (Ind AS 37 recognition and measurement) and concepts c01 (the three conditions of para 14) and c02 (para 86 disclosure unless remote). Then s02 shows how Schedule III structures the note into claims, guarantees and commitments. s03 and c03 handle corporate guarantees under Ind AS 109 — the main standalone off-balance-sheet risk. s04 (income tax under Ind AS 12 Appendix C, stay deposits and Vivad se Vishwas 2024) and s05 (GST s.73/74, s.128A, GSTAT and pre-deposit) cover the two largest dispute classes. s06 collects the remaining exposures — LCs, EPCG, take-or-pay, NCI puts and supplier finance. Finally c04 and c05 turn everything into ratios and probability-weighted exposure for the memo and the model.

The output of this topic, used in the week's exercise and Saturday case test, is a contingent liability table with gross amounts, forum, deposits, interest flag, probability view and note/page reference, rolled into CL%NW, CL/EBITDA and a probability-weighted adjustment.

Related concepts to study

ASC 450 vs Ind AS 37 probability thresholds

US GAAP ASC 450 "probable" threshold is higher than Ind AS 37 "more likely than not"

Under ASC 450-20, a loss contingency is accrued when a loss is probable — defined as "likely to occur", which practice reads at roughly 70–80% — and reasonably estimable. "Reasonably possible" items are disclosed and "remote" items are not. Ind AS 37 sets probable at more than 50%, so the same lawsuit at 60% probability is a provision under Ind AS but only a disclosure under US GAAP. Measurement also differs: when no amount in a range is a better estimate, ASC 450 accrues the low end of the range, whereas Ind AS 37 uses the expected value or most likely outcome (and the midpoint for a continuous range). When comparing an Indian company with a US-listed peer (ADR filers, US subsidiaries of Indian IT companies), expect US GAAP numbers to show lower litigation provisions and larger disclosed contingencies for identical facts. Adjust peer comparisons accordingly.

CARO 3(vii)(b) cross-check

CARO 3(vii)(b) disputed dues cross-check → w7_t1

CARO 2020 clause 3(vii)(b) requires the auditor to report statutory dues (GST, provident fund, ESI, income tax, customs, duty of excise, VAT, cess and others) that have not been deposited on account of a dispute, with the amount and the forum where the dispute is pending. This is the auditor's independent tabulation of most of the tax lines in "claims not acknowledged as debt". Reconcile the two: CARO amounts are typically net of deposits and exclude non-statutory claims, while the contingent liability note may be gross and include civil suits. Unreconciled differences — a GST dispute in CARO but missing from the note, or a forum in CARO more advanced than the note implies — are findings. The forum column is also your best evidence for the probability view in c05. See Week 7 topic 1 c02 for the clause mechanics.

Debt-like items in valuation and downside cases

Debt-like adjustments in EV bridge → Week 16; scenario/downside cases → Week 17

In a DCF, enterprise value is converted to equity value by deducting net debt and debt-like items: unfunded employee benefit obligations, NCI put liabilities, invoked or stressed guarantees, long-dated provisions, and the probability-weighted expected loss from contingent liabilities not provided. Week 16 builds this bridge; this topic supplies the inputs with evidence. Large, correlated or binary disputes are better modelled as scenarios than as expected values — for example, a telecom AGR-type sector ruling or a GST valuation dispute that could exceed net worth. Week 17 develops downside cases where the dispute is lost, cash is paid with interest over a schedule, and the liquidity and covenant consequences flow through the 3-statement model.

Common pitfalls

Adding entire contingent liabilities to debt

Adding entire contingent liabilities to debt without probability assessment. Analysts under time pressure add the full disclosed total to net debt "to be conservative". That treats a demand the company has already won three times at ITAT the same as an invoked guarantee, overstates leverage, and can push a sound company into a distress classification. It happens because the note gives one total and probability work is slow. Correct treatment: classify each line by nature, forum and precedent, apply probabilities (c05), subtract amounts already provided, treat correlated matters as scenarios, and keep the gross figure as a stress ratio (c04). In review, look for an adjusted debt figure that equals reported debt plus the exact note total — that signals no assessment was done.

Ignoring guarantees for subsidiaries in standalone analysis

Ignoring guarantees for subsidiaries in standalone analysis. When analysing a holding company's standalone accounts (for its own debt, dividend capacity or a holdco discount), reviewers often take standalone borrowings and ignore the guarantees note, assuming "the subsidiary's debt is in the consolidated accounts". But standalone creditors rank alongside the guaranteed lenders once a guarantee is invoked. People miss it because the guarantee liability on the balance sheet is tiny (Ind AS 109 fair value). Correct treatment: list guarantees by beneficiary, test each beneficiary's debt-service capacity, and compute look-through leverage (s03). Detect it in review by comparing the guarantees note and CARO 3(iii) totals with the leverage figure used.

Missing interest and penalty in tax demands

Missing interest and penalty components in tax demands. Many notes state "demands excluding interest, if any" or show only the tax component. For income tax, interest under s.234B/234C/220 accrues at 1% per month; for GST, interest under s.50 at 18% p.a. plus penalty of 10% (s.73) or 100% (s.74). On a five-year-old dispute, interest alone can be 60–90% of the tax. The omission occurs because demand orders separate the components and because some companies choose to present only principal. Correct treatment: gross up each demand for interest to the reporting date (and to expected resolution for valuation) and for penalty where levied, noting the amnesty schemes that can waive them (s.128A, DTVSV 2024). Detect it by reading the footnote wording and comparing with CARO 3(vii)(b) and Reg 30 announcements, which often state total demand.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated contingent liability review of a cement company [L3]

Shree Infra Cements Ltd (NSE-listed) reports for FY 2024-25 (standalone, ₹ crore): net worth 4,200; EBITDA 900; net debt 1,800. Notes show: (a) income tax demands 380, net of 76 deposited, excluding interest, relating to s.80-IA claims for AY 2015-16 to AY 2021-22 (company won at ITAT for AY 2015-16 and AY 2016-17; department appeals pending in HC); (b) GST demands 140 under s.73 for FY 2017-18 to FY 2019-20, interest 95, penalty 14; company did not opt for s.128A; (c) CCI penalty 520, NCLAT upheld for peers, appeal in SC, 10% deposited; (d) corporate guarantees 1,100 for a subsidiary SPV whose interest cover is 1.1x; (e) capital commitments 650; (f) EPCG export obligation 60, exports on track. Provision held: none for any item.

  1. Build the gross extraction table with deposits and interest flags.
  2. Compute CL%NW and CL/EBITDA on reported and gross bases.
  3. Assign probabilities with evidence and compute probability-weighted exposure.
  4. Identify any item you believe should have been provided under Ind AS 37 and the EBITDA impact.
  5. Draft the severity grading for each finding.

Q2. Guarantee and supplier finance in a holding company [L3]

Vardhan Holdings Ltd's standalone balance sheet shows borrowings of ₹600 crore, trade payables of ₹1,400 crore (of which ₹700 crore under a bank vendor-financing programme with 150-day terms vs 45-day normal terms), and a financial guarantee liability of ₹8 crore. The Ind AS 107 note shows a maximum credit exposure from guarantees of ₹2,500 crore for three subsidiaries; one subsidiary reported a default under CARO 3(ix) in its own audit report. Net worth is ₹3,000 crore; CFO is ₹420 crore.

  1. Reassess the guarantee liability under Ind AS 109.4.2.1(c) given the default.
  2. Reclassify the vendor-financing balance for analysis and recompute adjusted CFO and adjusted debt.
  3. Compute look-through leverage using your stated probabilities.
  4. List the disclosures and auditor-report elements (KAM, CARO 3(iii), 3(xxi)) you would check.

Q3. Tax dispute strategy and accounting [L2]

An IT services company has: a ₹210 crore transfer-pricing demand (tax effect) for AY 2019-20 pending before ITAT, 20% paid for stay; a ₹45 crore penalty-only dispute; and a ₹30 crore GST s.73 demand for FY 2018-19 with interest ₹20 crore and penalty ₹3 crore.

  1. Apply Ind AS 12 Appendix C to the TP matter assuming the ITAT ruled against the company for AY 2018-19 on the same comparables, using an expected-value approach with outcomes of your choice.
  2. Compute the DTVSV 2024 settlement amounts (declarations filed by 31 December 2024) for the TP and penalty matters, assuming the TP appeal is a new appeal filed by the company.
  3. Compute the s.128A cash outflow for the GST matter if opted.
  4. Pass journal entries if the company settles everything, and state the normalised EBITDA and PAT adjustments.

Q4. Credit memo excerpt [L3]

Using the facts of Q1, write a 300-word excerpt for a credit memo to a bank's credit committee, covering exposure quantification, classification challenges, the guarantee look-through, and the covenant implications if net debt to EBITDA is capped at 3.0x. Cite the note numbers you would reference (assume notes 36–40).
