Subtopic note

LCs, EPCG Obligations, Take-or-Pay, NCI Puts and Supplier Finance

Scope: Other: LC exposures, export obligations under EPCG, take-or-pay contracts, NCI put options, supplier finance

Why it matters

Beyond tax and litigation, five other exposures regularly distort reported leverage of Indian companies. Letters of credit and buyer's credit sit in non-fund-based limits; EPCG export obligations are duty liabilities waiting to crystallise; take-or-pay contracts are fixed charges that behave like debt; NCI put options are acquisition consideration recorded (or not) as liabilities; and supplier finance (reverse factoring, TReDS, acceptances) can convert trade payables into bank debt without any change in presentation. A forensic reviewer has to find each one across the notes and decide whether it belongs in adjusted net debt, the EV bridge, or a downside case.

Core notes

1. Letter of credit (LC) exposures

An LC is the bank's undertaking to pay a supplier against documents; the company is obliged to reimburse the bank. Three states:

State Accounting Where it shows
LC opened, goods not shipped Executory; no liability Sometimes in commitments; bank's non-fund-based limit utilised
Goods received, LC not yet due (usance LC) Trade payable to supplier, or payable to bank if bank has paid Trade payables / "acceptances"
Bank paid supplier; company pays bank later (buyer's credit, LC devolvement) Financing liability Should be in borrowings or other financial liabilities

Red flag: large usance LCs or buyer's credit classified as trade payables, keeping debt low and inflating CFO (the financing inflow is hidden as working-capital). Check sanction details in the borrowings note, CARO 3(ii)(b) returns to banks (which list non-fund-based utilisation) and the credit rating rationale (agencies disclose LC/BG limits). A devolved LC — bank paid because the company could not — is a liquidity distress signal.

2. Export obligations under EPCG

Under the Foreign Trade Policy 2023, the Export Promotion Capital Goods scheme allows import of capital goods at zero customs duty subject to an export obligation of 6 times the duty saved within 6 years (with block-wise targets and a maintained average export obligation). The importer executes a bond with customs. If the obligation is not met, duty saved becomes payable with interest (15% p.a. under the scheme provisions).

Accounting:

  • ITFG and common practice treat the duty foregone as a government grant (Ind AS 20) related to fulfilling the export obligation: capital goods are recognised at cost including duty saved, with a corresponding deferred income liability released to P&L as exports are made (some companies release over the asset's life instead — check policy).
  • The unfulfilled obligation is disclosed as a contingent liability (bond amount or duty saved) under Schedule III "other money for which the company is contingently liable".
  • If fulfilment is no longer probable (exports collapsed, product discontinued), a provision for duty plus interest is required (Ind AS 37.14).

Analytical check: compare remaining export obligation with current export revenue run-rate and the remaining period; a company exporting ₹100 crore a year with ₹900 crore obligation due in 2 years has a probable liability.

3. Take-or-pay contracts

Long-term purchase contracts with minimum offtake (LNG, coal linkage, power purchase agreements, captive power SPVs, pipeline capacity, cloud capacity reservations). The buyer pays for the minimum quantity whether or not it takes delivery.

  • Ind AS 116: if the contract conveys the right to control an identified asset (e.g. a dedicated regasification unit or captive plant), it contains a lease — recognise ROU asset and lease liability.
  • Ind AS 109: if net settlement is possible and the own-use exemption fails, it may be a derivative.
  • Ind AS 37 onerous contract: if unavoidable costs exceed expected benefits (demand fall, cheaper spot supply), provide for the lower of cost of fulfilling and penalty for exiting (Ind AS 37.68).
  • Otherwise, it is a commitment: disclose under "other commitments". Analysts may capitalise the minimum payments as debt-like (similar to pre-Ind AS 116 operating lease capitalisation) for comparability.

4. NCI put options

When a parent writes a put allowing non-controlling shareholders of a subsidiary to sell their shares to it (common in acquisitions of 51–76% with a put on the balance), the parent has an obligation to deliver cash.

  • Consolidated: recognise a financial liability at the present value of the redemption amount (Ind AS 32.23), usually debiting equity (the "anticipated acquisition" or "present access" approach depends on policy; whether NCI continues to be recognised is a policy choice). Subsequent remeasurements go to P&L or equity per policy.
  • Standalone: the written put is a derivative over the subsidiary's equity, measured at fair value.
  • If the exercise price is formula-based (multiple of EBITDA), the liability swings with subsidiary performance.

Many companies present this within "other financial liabilities"; for an EV bridge it is debt-like and must be added — and the related NCI must not also be added at market value (double count).

5. Supplier finance arrangements

One or more finance providers pay the entity's suppliers early, and the entity pays the finance provider on or after the original due date (reverse factoring, TReDS for MSME suppliers, vendor financing, bank "channel finance" on the purchase side).

  • Presentation: remains a trade payable if the terms are not substantially different from normal trade credit; reclassify to borrowings if the payment terms are extended materially beyond normal, or the bank becomes the creditor and the original liability is extinguished (Ind AS 109 derecognition).
  • Cash flow: if presented as a trade payable, the outflow to the bank is operating; if borrowings, the bank's payment to suppliers is a non-cash transaction and the company's repayment is financing — a large effect on CFO.
  • Disclosure: the IASB amended IAS 7 and IFRS 7 in May 2023 (effective 1 January 2024) to require disclosure of terms, carrying amounts of liabilities in the arrangement, the amount suppliers have already been paid, the range of payment due dates, and non-cash changes. MCA's notification of the equivalent Ind AS 7/107 amendments should be verified for the year under review; where not yet notified, disclosure is voluntary.
  • MSME angle: s.43B(h) of the Income-tax Act (from AY 2024-25) disallows payments to micro and small enterprises beyond 45 days; TReDS and supplier finance usage rose in response. The MSMED Act disclosure in Schedule III shows delays.

Detection: days payables outstanding rising sharply while the borrowings line is flat; "acceptances" or "dues to banks under vendor financing" in trade payables; finance costs rising faster than borrowings (because discount charges are borne by the buyer).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Bank guarantees issued on the company's behalf (performance, customs, bid) are counter-indemnified by the company; they are contingent only if the underlying obligation might be breached.
  • EPCG obligations of subsidiaries appear only in consolidated notes.
  • Take-or-pay for power from a promoter-owned captive plant is also a related-party transaction (Week 7 topic 5); pricing may transfer value.
  • NCI put with price at fair value has limited economic dilution but still a funding need; with a fixed or floor price it is a leveraged bet.
  • Supplier finance unwinding is a liquidity risk: if banks withdraw the programme, the company must pay suppliers on normal terms, causing a sudden working-capital outflow.

Key takeaways

  • Devolved LCs and buyer's credit inside trade payables are hidden borrowings; check sanction letters, CARO 3(ii) and rating rationales.
  • EPCG obligation is 6 times the duty saved within 6 years; unfulfilled amounts are contingent until fulfilment is improbable, then a provision with interest.
  • Take-or-pay may be a lease, a derivative, an onerous contract or a commitment — test in that order.
  • NCI written puts are financial liabilities at PV of redemption in consolidated statements; treat as debt-like.
  • Supplier finance can move financing outflows into CFO; IAS 7/IFRS 7 2023 disclosures help, subject to Ind AS notification.

Practice Questions

Q1. EPCG probability test [L2]

A textile exporter imported looms in FY 2020-21 under EPCG, saving duty of ₹40 crore. The export obligation is 6 times duty saved over 6 years. Cumulative exports counted to date (31 March 2025) are ₹90 crore; annual exports are ₹35 crore and falling. Compute the remaining obligation, assess whether a provision is needed, and state what amount you would provide if interest at 15% p.a. runs from the date of import on the duty proportionate to the shortfall.

💡 Hint: Compare the remaining obligation with the realistic exports achievable in the remaining period.

Q2. Supplier finance and CFO [L2]

A retailer's trade payables rose from ₹1,200 crore to ₹2,100 crore while revenue grew 10%; ₹800 crore of payables are under a bank-led vendor financing programme with payment terms extended from 60 to 180 days. CFO is ₹950 crore. Explain how you would reclassify for analysis and recompute adjusted CFO and adjusted net debt.

Q3. NCI put in the EV bridge [L2]

Acquirer Ltd bought 60% of Target Pvt Ltd and wrote a put on the remaining 40% exercisable in 2027 at 12 times FY 2026-27 EBITDA. Target's expected FY27 EBITDA is ₹50 crore; discount rate 12%; valuation date 31 March 2025. Compute the indicative liability and explain how the put and the NCI should appear in the consolidated EV-to-equity bridge.

Q4. Take-or-pay classification [L3]

A fertiliser company has a 15-year LNG take-or-pay contract with a minimum of 1 MMTPA at an oil-linked price, and a separate 20-year contract for exclusive use of one storage tank at a third-party terminal. Spot LNG prices are now 30% below contract prices. Discuss the classification of each under Ind AS 116, Ind AS 109 and Ind AS 37 and the disclosures you would expect.

Q5. LC devolvement red flag [L1]

A rating rationale mentions "instances of LC devolvement in Q3 FY25, regularised within 30 days". The annual report shows no default under CARO 3(ix). Explain why this matters and what further evidence you would seek.
