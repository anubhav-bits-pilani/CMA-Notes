Subtopic note

Ind AS 37: Provision vs Contingent Liability vs Remote

Scope: Ind AS 37 provisions vs contingent liabilities vs remote; probability and measurement judgements

Why it matters

Every litigation, tax demand, warranty claim, guarantee and environmental notice in an annual report sits in one of three buckets: recognised as a provision (it hits profit and the balance sheet), disclosed as a contingent liability (only a note), or treated as remote (nothing at all). The bucket is a management judgement, and the auditor tests it. For a forensic reader, the classification is where earnings management hides: a ₹300 crore demand kept in the contingent liability note instead of provisions flatters EBITDA, net worth and leverage covenants. In credit work, lenders ask for the "probable but not provided" pool; in equity work, analysts adjust the EV bridge for debt-like items. You need to be able to re-perform management's classification from the note narrative.

Core notes

The three-way classification

Ind AS 37 defines a provision as a liability of uncertain timing or amount (Ind AS 37.10). A contingent liability is either (a) a possible obligation arising from past events whose existence will be confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within the entity's control, or (b) a present obligation that is not recognised because an outflow is not probable or the amount cannot be measured reliably (Ind AS 37.10).

Assessment Present obligation? Outflow Treatment
Provision Yes (more likely than not) Probable (more likely than not) Recognise; disclose movement (37.84–85)
Contingent liability (type b) Yes Possible but not probable Disclose (37.86)
Contingent liability (type a) Possible only — Disclose unless remote
Contingent liability (rare) Yes, probable outflow Cannot be reliably estimated Disclose (37.26: extremely rare)
Remote Either Remote No recognition, no disclosure

Step 1 — Is there a present obligation from a past event?

A present obligation exists when, taking account of all available evidence, it is more likely than not that a present obligation exists at the reporting date (Ind AS 37.15–16). For lawsuits, the evidence includes expert opinion and events after the reporting period. The obligating event must leave no realistic alternative but to settle — legal obligation (contract, legislation) or constructive obligation (established pattern of practice, published policy) (Ind AS 37.10, 37.17). Future operating losses and costs the entity can avoid by its own actions are never provisions (Ind AS 37.18–19, 37.63).

Step 2 — Is an outflow probable?

"Probable" in Ind AS 37 means more likely than not, i.e. probability above 50% (Ind AS 37.23). Where there are many similar obligations (warranties), probability is assessed for the class as a whole (Ind AS 37.24).

Step 3 — Can the amount be reliably estimated?

Except in extremely rare cases, an entity can determine a range of outcomes and therefore a reliable estimate (Ind AS 37.25–26).

Measurement judgements

The provision is the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the obligation at the reporting date — the amount the entity would rationally pay to settle or transfer it (Ind AS 37.36–37).

  • Large population (warranties): expected value, i.e. probability-weighted outcomes (Ind AS 37.39). Where a continuous range is equally likely, use the midpoint.
  • Single obligation (one lawsuit): the individual most likely outcome, adjusted upward or downward if other outcomes are mostly higher or lower (Ind AS 37.40).
  • Risk and uncertainty are considered, but do not justify excessive provisions (Ind AS 37.42–43).
  • Discounting where the time value of money is material, using a pre-tax rate reflecting current market assessments and specific risks (Ind AS 37.45–47). The unwinding is a finance cost.
  • Future events such as changes in law are reflected only where there is sufficient objective evidence they will occur (Ind AS 37.48–50).
  • Reimbursements (insurance, indemnity from a seller) are a separate asset, recognised only when virtually certain; the P&L may show the expense net (Ind AS 37.53–54).
  • Remeasurement: provisions are reviewed every reporting date and reversed if outflow is no longer probable (Ind AS 37.59); used only for the expenditure for which originally recognised (Ind AS 37.61).

Worked illustration

A cement company faces a ₹40 crore claim by a contractor for termination. Legal counsel at 31 March 2025 assesses: 60% probability of losing; if lost, most likely award ₹25 crore, with a 20% chance of the full ₹40 crore.

  • Present obligation? Counsel says it is more likely than not that a breach occurred → yes.
  • Probable outflow? 60% > 50% → yes, recognise a provision.
  • Measurement (single obligation): most likely outcome ₹25 crore; other outcomes are skewed higher, so management may justify a provision somewhat above ₹25 crore. Expected value of the loss scenario alone would not be the basis for a single claim.

Disclosure

For provisions, each class needs opening/closing balances, additions, amounts used, unused amounts reversed and unwinding of discount (Ind AS 37.84), plus a description, expected timing and uncertainties (Ind AS 37.85). For contingent liabilities not remote: a brief description and, where practicable, an estimate of the financial effect, the uncertainties about amount or timing, and the possibility of any reimbursement (Ind AS 37.86). In extremely rare cases where disclosure would seriously prejudice the entity's position in a dispute, only the general nature and the reason for non-disclosure is given (Ind AS 37.92).

Contingent assets

Contingent assets are not recognised; they are disclosed where an inflow is probable, and recognised only when realisation is virtually certain (Ind AS 37.31–35, 37.89). This asymmetry matters when companies net a counter-claim against a demand — they should not.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Probability migration: as a case moves from a tribunal to the High Court with adverse orders at each level, the probability rises. Watch for amounts that stay in contingent liabilities after two adverse appellate orders.
  • Splitting a single demand: management may provide for the principal and disclose interest and penalty as contingent. That can be legitimate if counsel views penalty as unlikely, but it needs evidence.
  • Industry-wide disputes (e.g. AGR for telecom before the Supreme Court's 2019 ruling): many companies disclosed as contingent until the judgment, after which the full amount became a provision overnight. Ask whether there is adverse precedent in the same issue for other companies.
  • Onerous contracts are provisions, not contingencies (Ind AS 37.66–69); since the 2022 amendment the cost of fulfilling includes both incremental costs and an allocation of other directly related costs.
  • Restructuring provisions need a detailed formal plan and a valid expectation raised in those affected (Ind AS 37.72).
  • Practicability of estimate: "not practicable to estimate" is overused; push back where the demand order states an amount.
  • Events after the reporting period: a court judgment after 31 March but before approval of financial statements is an adjusting event if it confirms a condition existing at year-end (Ind AS 10).

Key takeaways

  • Three tests in sequence: present obligation (more likely than not), probable outflow (more likely than not), reliable estimate; fail the second and it is a contingent liability, fail both and judge remoteness.
  • Ind AS 37 "probable" is above 50% — lower than US GAAP's "probable" threshold.
  • Single obligations use the most likely outcome; populations use expected value; discount where material.
  • Reimbursements are separate assets and need virtual certainty; counter-claims are not netted.
  • The classification boundary is a key earnings lever — re-perform it using legal status and precedent.
  • Disclose contingent liabilities with financial effect unless remote; seriously prejudicial exemption is rare.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify five matters [L1]

A listed FMCG company has at 31 March 2025: (a) a product liability suit for ₹12 crore where counsel rates the loss probability at 70%; (b) an income-tax demand of ₹45 crore where the company won the identical issue at ITAT for an earlier year; (c) a consumer forum claim of ₹0.2 crore with probability 5%; (d) a customs show-cause notice of ₹8 crore with probability 40%; (e) a counter-claim the company has filed against a supplier for ₹6 crore which counsel thinks is 80% likely to succeed. Classify each as provision, contingent liability, remote, or contingent asset and state the disclosure required.

Q2. Measuring a single obligation [L2]

A steel company is sued for ₹60 crore for environmental damage. Counsel's assessment at 31 March 2025: 25% no liability, 45% liability of ₹30 crore, 30% liability of ₹60 crore. Settlement is expected in three years; the pre-tax discount rate is 9%. Compute the amount to be recognised under the most likely outcome approach, explain whether an adjustment is warranted under Ind AS 37.40, and pass the journal entries for initial recognition and the first year's unwinding.

💡 Hint: Decide first whether a present obligation and probable outflow exist, then apply the single-obligation rule before discounting.

Q3. Reimbursement from insurer [L2]

A chemical company recognises a ₹15 crore provision for a factory fire third-party claim. Its insurer has accepted the claim in principle for ₹10 crore subject to survey. Draft the balance sheet and P&L presentation, and explain how the answer changes if the insurer has only acknowledged receipt of the claim.

Q4. Reviewer's challenge on classification [L3]

You are reviewing a mid-cap infrastructure company. A ₹220 crore arbitration award went against it in February 2025; the company has filed a challenge under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act and shows the full amount under contingent liabilities. Net worth is ₹1,400 crore. Write the questions you would put to the CFO and the auditor, and explain what evidence would justify continued contingent treatment.

Q5. Interview: why no provision? [L1]

An interviewer asks: "If a company is 45% likely to lose a ₹100 crore case, why doesn't it provide ₹45 crore?" Answer in under 150 words, covering the recognition threshold and the single-obligation measurement rule.
