Subtopic note

Schedule III Contingent Liabilities and Commitments Note

Scope: Schedule III contingent liabilities and commitments note: claims not acknowledged as debt, guarantees, capital commitments

Why it matters

The contingent liabilities and commitments note is the single place where an Indian company lists exposures that are not on the balance sheet. Schedule III prescribes its skeleton, Ind AS 37 prescribes its content, and CARO 3(vii)(b) and the auditor's KAMs often point to the same items. For forensic work you extract this note into a table every year, classify each line, and compute it against net worth and EBITDA. For modelling, capital commitments drive the capex forecast and guarantees drive debt-like adjustments; claims not acknowledged as debt drive downside scenarios.

Core notes

The prescribed structure

Schedule III (Division II for Ind AS companies; Division I for AS companies) requires a note on contingent liabilities and commitments "to the extent not provided for", classified as:

Head Sub-head Typical content
(i) Contingent liabilities (a) Claims against the company not acknowledged as debt Income tax, GST, customs, excise, service tax, VAT/entry tax demands under appeal; civil suits; labour claims; arbitration; electricity/water cess disputes
(b) Guarantees Corporate guarantees to banks for subsidiaries/associates/JVs/third parties; bank guarantees issued on the company's behalf (in some presentations)
(c) Other money for which the company is contingently liable Bills discounted with recourse, export obligations, letters of comfort, liquidated damages, indemnities
(ii) Commitments (a) Estimated amount of contracts remaining to be executed on capital account and not provided for Purchase orders for plant, civil contracts, software implementation
(b) Uncalled liability on shares and other investments partly paid Partly paid shares in JV, commitments to AIFs
(c) Other commitments Take-or-pay, purchase obligations, lease commitments not yet commenced, commitments to fund subsidiaries

Schedule III also requires disclosure of proposed dividends and arrears of cumulative preference dividends in the notes; those are not contingent liabilities but often sit near this note.

"Claims against the company not acknowledged as debt"

This is the largest bucket for most Indian companies, and the phrase means the company disputes the claim. Key points for reading it:

  • Gross vs net: many companies disclose the demand net of amounts paid under protest or pre-deposits. Others disclose gross and show deposits separately under "other non-current assets — balances with government authorities". Read the footnote to know which.
  • Inclusion of interest and penalty: Ind AS 37.86 requires an estimate of financial effect, which logically includes interest and penalty where demanded. Many notes say "excluding interest, if any" — a red flag for understatement (see the related pitfall in this topic).
  • Granularity: better disclosures split by statute (Income Tax, GST, Customs, others), by forum and by year. CARO 3(vii)(b) gives the forum-wise table for statutory dues not deposited, which you should reconcile.
  • Narrative: Ind AS 37.86 requires a brief description of nature, uncertainties and reimbursement possibility. The standard line "the company has been advised that the demand is not tenable" is not a probability assessment.

Guarantees

The guarantees line in standalone financial statements usually captures corporate guarantees given by the company to lenders of subsidiaries, joint ventures, associates and sometimes promoter entities. Since Ind AS 109 requires a financial guarantee to be recognised at fair value, the note should disclose the full guaranteed amount (or the outstanding loan covered, as the company states) while the balance sheet carries only the guarantee liability (see s03). Bank guarantees issued by banks at the company's request (performance or bid guarantees to customers or government) are commonly disclosed separately; they represent non-fund-based limits. In consolidated statements, guarantees given for subsidiaries' borrowings drop out because the borrowing itself is consolidated — only guarantees for associates, JVs and outsiders remain.

Capital commitments

Capital commitments are the unexecuted portion of contracts placed on capital account, net of advances paid. They are a forecasting input, not a risk exposure in themselves. Compare:

  • Capital commitments vs management's capex guidance in MD&A (Week 7 topic 2).
  • Capital commitments vs CWIP ageing (Week 7 topic 3) — a large CWIP with tiny commitments suggests stalled projects.
  • Capital advances (non-current assets) vs commitments — capital advances paid to related parties with little progress are a diversion red flag.

Other commitments

Uncalled capital on partly-paid investments, undrawn commitments to AIFs, and long-term purchase commitments (take-or-pay, PPA minimum offtake, sponsor support undertakings) belong here. Ind AS 116 also requires disclosure of leases committed but not commenced (Ind AS 116.59).

Illustration of reading the movement

A pharma company's claims not acknowledged as debt went from ₹380 crore to ₹610 crore while provisions for litigation fell from ₹45 crore to ₹20 crore. That pattern — contingent rising, provisions falling — needs the provision rollforward (Ind AS 37.84): if ₹25 crore was "reversed as no longer required" rather than "utilised", ask whether the matters moved to the contingent note. The reversal added ₹25 crore to EBITDA.

Presentation linkages

  • Contingent liabilities must be disclosed in both standalone and consolidated statements; the consolidated note includes the group's share of associates' and JVs' contingent liabilities (Ind AS 112).
  • SEBI LODR Reg 30 requires disclosure of material litigation and disputes; compare event disclosures through the year against the year-end note.
  • The auditor may cover large matters in a KAM (litigation and tax provisioning is a common KAM in India).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Bills discounted with recourse: under Ind AS 109 these generally fail derecognition, so the receivable and borrowing stay on balance sheet — they should not be in contingent liabilities at all. Old-format notes still show them.
  • Letters of comfort: whether a letter of comfort is a guarantee depends on wording; if it creates a legal obligation to pay, it is a financial guarantee under Ind AS 109.
  • Disclosure "not practicable": allowed by Ind AS 37.91 only with a statement that it is not practicable; it should not apply to a quantified demand order.
  • Matters in the "remote" category sometimes include very large demands; check Reg 30 announcements and the auditor's KAMs for matters missing from the note.
  • Commitments net of advances: confirm whether capital advances are netted; double counting with CWIP is common in analyst models.

Key takeaways

  • Schedule III splits the note into contingent liabilities (claims, guarantees, other) and commitments (capital, uncalled, other), all "to the extent not provided for".
  • "Claims not acknowledged as debt" is a dispute bucket; read gross vs net of deposits and whether interest is included.
  • Guarantees in standalone accounts are often the largest hidden leverage; in consolidation, subsidiary guarantees disappear because the debt is already inside.
  • Capital commitments are forecasting inputs and must reconcile with capex guidance and CWIP.
  • Build a multi-year extraction table with forum, deposit, interest flag, probability view and page reference.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify the note lines [L1]

Classify each into the correct Schedule III sub-head: (a) ₹35 crore service-tax demand under appeal at CESTAT; (b) ₹500 crore corporate guarantee given to SBI for a step-down subsidiary's term loan; (c) ₹120 crore balance of a turnkey contract for a new plant, net of ₹30 crore advance paid; (d) ₹10 crore uncalled amount on partly paid shares of a JV; (e) ₹18 crore export obligation under EPCG licences; (f) a 10-year take-or-pay gas supply agreement with minimum annual payment of ₹40 crore.

Q2. Extract and analyse movement [L2]

A company discloses claims not acknowledged as debt of ₹250 crore (FY23), ₹310 crore (FY24) and ₹470 crore (FY25), "net of ₹60 crore, ₹75 crore and ₹90 crore paid under protest" respectively. Provision for litigation was ₹40 crore, ₹38 crore and ₹15 crore, and the FY25 rollforward shows ₹22 crore "reversed as no longer required". Net worth is ₹2,000 crore, ₹2,150 crore and ₹2,300 crore. Build the gross exposure table and list three questions for management.

💡 Hint: Add back deposits to get gross demand, then compare the growth in gross demand with the provision reversals.

Q3. Capital commitments vs CWIP [L2]

CWIP is ₹900 crore, of which ₹600 crore is more than 3 years old per the Schedule III ageing table. Capital commitments are ₹50 crore and capital advances are ₹220 crore, of which ₹150 crore is paid to a promoter-group EPC contractor. MD&A says the expansion will commission "in the coming year". Write a 200-word reviewer comment on what the three disclosures together suggest.

Q4. Consolidated vs standalone guarantees [L1]

A parent's standalone note shows guarantees of ₹1,200 crore: ₹900 crore for wholly-owned subsidiaries, ₹200 crore for a 50:50 JV and ₹100 crore for a promoter-owned company. Which amounts should appear in the consolidated contingent liabilities note and why?
