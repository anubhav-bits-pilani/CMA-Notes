Subtopic note

GST Disputes: s.73/74 Demands, s.128A Waiver, GSTAT and Pre-deposit

Scope: GST disputes: s.73/74 demands, s.128A waiver, GSTAT appeals, pre-deposit

Why it matters

From FY 2023-24 onwards, the first wave of GST adjudication for FY 2017-18 to FY 2019-20 produced a large volume of demand orders — ITC mismatches with GSTR-2A/2B, s.16(4) time-barred ITC, ITC on supplier non-payment (s.16(2)(c)), classification and valuation of related-party supplies, and e-way bill disputes. These now dominate the GST line of contingent liabilities. Because GST is not an income tax, the recognition test is Ind AS 37 (s01). The amnesty in s.128A, the operationalisation of the GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) and the pre-deposit rules decide how much cash is locked up and how much exposure could crystallise. As a CMA with GST compliance experience, you can read the nature of each demand and judge probability better than most analysts.

Core notes

Demand provisions

Feature s.73 (no fraud) s.74 (fraud, wilful misstatement, suppression)
Scope Tax not paid/short paid, erroneous refund, ITC wrongly availed/utilised for reasons other than fraud Same, by reason of fraud or wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax
Time limit for order 3 years from due date of annual return for the year 5 years from due date of annual return
Show cause notice At least 3 months before the order time limit At least 6 months before
Penalty 10% of tax or ₹10,000, whichever is higher 100% of tax (reduced to 15%/25%/50% for payment at SCN/order stages)
Interest s.50: 18% p.a. (on net cash liability for delayed payment; 24% for excess ITC claimed in specified cases) Same

From FY 2024-25 onwards, s.74A (inserted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024) replaces s.73/74 with a single procedure and common time limit (notice within 42 months and order within 12 months of notice, from the due date of the annual return), retaining differential penalties for fraud and non-fraud cases. Demands for FY 2017-18 to FY 2023-24 continue under s.73/74.

s.128A — waiver of interest and penalty

Inserted with effect from 1 November 2024, s.128A waives interest and penalty for demands raised under s.73 relating to FY 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20, if the full tax demanded is paid by the notified date (31 March 2025, per Notification 21/2024-CT; later dates apply for certain s.74-to-s.73 conversions). Conditions:

  • Applies to notices/orders/appellate orders under s.73, and to s.74 cases that an appellate authority or court directs to be redetermined under s.73.
  • Excludes erroneous refunds.
  • Any pending appeal must be withdrawn; no refund of interest or penalty already paid.
  • Application in prescribed form (SPL-01/SPL-02 under Rule 164).

Accounting effect: where a company opted in, the tax portion becomes a liability (or is paid) and is charged to P&L or capitalised as ITC reversal per its nature, while interest and penalty previously disclosed (or provided) fall away. A provision for interest and penalty, if earlier recognised, is reversed — creating a one-off gain that should be stripped out of normalised EBITDA. In the contingent liability note, FY25 should show a sharp drop in GST exposure for FY18–FY20 cases; if it does not, ask why the company did not opt in.

Appeal hierarchy

  1. Adjudicating authority (order under s.73/74).
  2. Appellate Authority — Commissioner (Appeals)/Joint Commissioner (Appeals) under s.107, within 3 months (condonable by 1 month).
  3. GSTAT — Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal under s.109/112, with a Principal Bench in New Delhi and State Benches. Constitution was delayed for years; benches were notified and the tribunal began functioning in 2025, with transitional timelines for filing backlog appeals (check the current CBIC notification). Until it functioned, assessees filed writs in High Courts or waited, and demands sat at the "appeal to be filed before GSTAT" stage.
  4. High Court (s.117) on substantial questions of law; Supreme Court (s.118); questions of place of supply go directly to the Principal Bench.

Pre-deposit

Appeal Pre-deposit Cap (as amended by Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024)
s.107(6) — first appeal Full admitted amount + 10% of disputed tax ₹20 crore each of CGST and SGST (₹40 crore IGST)
s.112(8) — GSTAT Additional 10% of remaining disputed tax ₹20 crore each of CGST and SGST (₹40 crore IGST)
Penalty-only orders 10% of penalty (Finance Act, 2025 amendment) —

Payment of the pre-deposit results in an automatic stay of recovery of the balance (s.107(7), s.112(9)). The pre-deposit is paid through the electronic cash or credit ledger (DRC-03 or via the appeal form); it is an asset ("balances with government authorities — GST paid under protest") if recovery is probable, and it is refunded with interest if the appeal succeeds (s.115).

Reading GST contingent liabilities

  • Split by financial year and section (73 vs 74); s.74 cases imply allegations of suppression and carry higher penalty and reputational risk.
  • Identify nature: ITC mismatches (often resolved by documentation), s.16(4) time-bar (largely cured by the retrospective s.16(5) relief for FY 2017-18 to 2020-21 where returns were filed by 30 November 2021), classification (high stakes, sector-wide, e.g. online gaming, ice-cream parlours, coated fabrics), related-party valuation (Rule 28) and cross-charge/ISD disputes for multi-state companies.
  • Compare with Reg 30 disclosures of GST orders through the year — listed companies announce orders above the materiality threshold, often with "no material impact" boilerplate.
  • Reconcile to CARO 3(vii)(b), which lists GST dues not deposited on account of dispute by forum.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Sector-wide classification disputes (e.g. the ₹1 lakh crore-plus online gaming notices in 2023) can exceed net worth; probability depends on pending constitutional challenges, not company-specific facts.
  • Interest on wrongly availed but unutilised ITC is not payable after the 2022 retrospective amendment to s.50(3); demands including such interest are partly unsustainable.
  • Bunching of orders near limitation dates is common; many orders are passed hastily and remanded — this lowers outflow probability but raises procedural uncertainty.
  • Reimbursement from customers under contractual tax clauses (EPC contracts) may offset exposure; treat as a separate asset only if virtually certain (Ind AS 37.53).
  • Cash locked in pre-deposits across hundreds of cases can be material to working capital for distributors and retailers.

Key takeaways

  • GST demands follow Ind AS 37, not Ind AS 12 Appendix C.
  • s.73 (non-fraud, 3 years, 10% penalty) and s.74 (fraud, 5 years, 100% penalty) differ in risk; s.74A governs FY 2024-25 onwards.
  • s.128A waives interest and penalty for FY18–FY20 s.73 demands if tax was paid by the notified date; expect FY25 provision reversals and drops in disclosed exposure.
  • Pre-deposit is 10% at each appellate stage, capped, with automatic stay; it is an asset, not an expense.
  • GSTAT operationalisation moves a backlog of cases forward; track forum-wise progression year on year.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify and measure GST exposures [L2]

A consumer durables company has three GST orders: (a) s.73, FY 2018-19, ITC mismatch ₹12 crore tax, interest ₹9 crore, penalty ₹1.2 crore, appeal before Commissioner (Appeals); (b) s.74, FY 2019-20, alleged fake invoices ₹20 crore tax, interest ₹14 crore, penalty ₹20 crore, appeal to be filed before GSTAT; (c) s.73, FY 2021-22, classification of a product ₹30 crore tax, interest ₹12 crore, penalty ₹3 crore. Identify which qualify for s.128A, compute the cash required to opt in where eligible, and compute the pre-deposits paid for (a) and (b) at the appellate stages reached.

💡 Hint: Check the section and the financial year for eligibility, then apply 10% at each stage of appeal.

Q2. Provision reversal after amnesty [L1]

A company had provided ₹18 crore for interest and penalty on FY 2017-18 GST demands. It paid the tax of ₹25 crore in March 2025 under s.128A. Pass the journal entries and explain how the analyst should treat the reversal in normalised EBITDA.

Q3. Reg 30 vs year-end note reconciliation [L2]

During FY 2024-25, a company announced under Reg 30 four GST orders totalling ₹140 crore, each stating "the company will file an appeal; no material impact expected". The year-end note shows GST claims not acknowledged as debt rising from ₹60 crore to ₹110 crore. List the possible reasons for the gap and the documents you would request.

Q4. Sector-wide dispute memo [L3]

An NSE-listed online gaming platform received GST notices of ₹8,000 crore against net worth of ₹1,800 crore, arising from the change in valuation basis to full face value of bets, with the matter pending before the Supreme Court. The company discloses it as a contingent liability, saying outflow is not probable. Write a one-page memo on how you would treat this in a credit and an equity analysis, including scenario construction.
