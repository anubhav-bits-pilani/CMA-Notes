Roadmap topic: Cash Flow Statement Forensics: CFO-to-EBITDA conversion ratio, Capex intensity (Maintenance vs Growth Capex), and Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) vs Equity (FCFE)

Overview

Normalised EBITDA (t2) tells you what the business earns on an accrual basis; this topic tests whether those earnings turn into cash, how much of that cash must be reinvested just to stand still, and what is left for lenders and shareholders. The cash flow statement under Ind AS 7 is the starting point, but in India it has three structural features that make it unreliable if read naively: interest paid sits in financing for non-financial entities (CFO is "pre-interest"), Ind AS 116 pushes all capitalised lease payments into financing (CFO is "pre-rent"), and working capital can be shaped by factoring, bill discounting and supply chain finance. On top of that, the investing section reports cash capex, which can differ sharply from economic investment because of capital creditors, buyer's credit, capital advances and acquisitions.

For the target roles this is daily work. An equity analyst uses CFO/EBITDA conversion and FCF yield to screen quality; a credit analyst at a bank or rating agency builds cash accruals after interest and lease payments; an FP&A team forecasts cash conversion and capex budgets split between maintenance and growth; a transaction diligence team presents an EBITDA-to-cash bridge in a QoE report. In valuation, FCFF and FCFE are the numerators of the DCF, and their definitions must be consistent with how leases, surplus cash and debt-like items are treated in the EV bridge (t3).

The learning sequence is: first reconstruct CFO from the P&L and balance sheets (s01) and understand Ind AS 7 classification rules (s02) and the Ind AS 116 effect (s03). Then build the CFO-to-EBITDA bridge (s04) and read conversion over several years (c01). Next analyse investment: maintenance vs growth capex (s05, c05), cash vs accrual capex and acquisitions (s06), and capex intensity (c04). Then test working-capital engineering (s07) and tax cash flows (c06). Finally assemble FCFF via the EBIT route (c02) and the CFO route (c03), and move to FCFE (c04).

Related concepts to study

IAS 7 choice, IFRS 18 and US GAAP ASC 230

IAS 7 allows choice for interest/dividends; IFRS 18 removes the choice from 2027; US GAAP ASC 230 puts interest paid in operating

Under IAS 7.33 an IFRS reporter may classify interest paid as operating or financing and interest/dividends received as operating or investing. IFRS 18 (effective for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027) amends IAS 7 so that, for entities without a specified main business activity of investing or providing finance, interest paid is financing and interest and dividends received are investing — broadly converging with the Ind AS 7 position India already applies. IFRS 18 also mandates operating profit as the starting point for the indirect method. US GAAP ASC 230 classifies interest paid and interest/dividends received as operating (with limited exceptions), so US CFO is after interest. When building peer sets with global comparables, restate everyone to "CFO before interest" or "CFO after interest" explicitly; never take vendor CFO figures at face value.

Supplier finance disclosures (2023 amendments)

IAS 7/IFRS 7 supplier finance arrangement disclosures (2023 amendments); check Ind AS notification status

The IASB issued Supplier Finance Arrangements amendments to IAS 7 and IFRS 7 in May 2023, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024. They require disclosure of the terms of reverse-factoring programmes, the carrying amount of liabilities in the programme and the portion already paid by finance providers, the range of payment due dates compared with non-programme payables, non-cash changes, and liquidity risk concentration. In India, Ind AS amendments become applicable only when MCA notifies them; check the latest Companies (Indian Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules for whether and from which date corresponding changes to Ind AS 7 and Ind AS 107 apply. Until applicable, Indian reporters may disclose little, so use s07's detection checklist on borrowings, payables and contingent liability notes.

Cash flow schedule, FCF build and DCF

Everything here becomes the historical columns and the logic of the model. In Week 14 the cash flow statement is derived from P&L and balance sheet movements — the s01 reconstruction in reverse — and the FCF schedule separates CFO, capex (maintenance/growth), lease payments and debt flows. Checks built here (CFO reconstruction residual, 44A reconciliation, capex roll-forward) become model integrity checks. In Week 15 the FCFF definition (c02/c03) must match the discount rate (WACC without lease debt for rent-adjusted FCFF) and the EV bridge (t3), and terminal-year reinvestment must be tied to growth and ROIC rather than to an arbitrary capex percentage.

Capital budgeting and NPV

Capex decisions and NPV linkage → Week 21: capital budgeting

Growth capex is justified only if the projects earn more than their cost of capital. Week 21 evaluates individual projects with NPV, IRR and payback; this topic supplies the company-level evidence. If growth capex over five years is ₹5,000 crore and incremental NOPAT is ₹300 crore, incremental ROIC is 6% — likely below WACC, so growth destroys value despite rising EBITDA. Conversely, FP&A teams use the maintenance/growth split to structure capex approvals: maintenance budgets are tied to asset registers and depreciation, while growth projects carry business cases with post-implementation reviews comparing actual cash returns with the approved NPV.

Common pitfalls

Comparing Ind AS CFO with US GAAP CFO without adjustment

Comparing CFO of an Ind AS reporter (interest in financing) with a US GAAP filer (interest in operating) without adjustment

Mistake: placing an Indian company's reported CFO next to a US peer's and concluding the Indian company has better cash conversion. Why it happens: data vendors label both "Cash from operations", and analysts rarely read the classification policy. Correct treatment: restate both to the same basis — deduct interest paid from Ind AS CFO (and add interest/dividend received if your basis is operating), or add back after-tax interest to US CFO; also align lease treatment (ASC 842 operating lease cost stays in US CFO). Detection: in review, check that the comparables sheet has an explicit "CFO basis" row and that the leverage company's conversion gap shrinks after restatement.

Treating all or no capex as maintenance

Treating all capex as maintenance (understates FCF potential) or none as maintenance (overstates sustainable FCF)

Mistake: computing "sustainable FCF" as CFO minus total capex (ignoring that growth capex is discretionary), or as CFO alone (ignoring that assets wear out). Why it happens: companies do not disclose the split, and analysts default to the extreme that supports their thesis. Correct treatment: estimate maintenance capex using at least two methods — D&A on owned assets, management guidance, capacity or per-store unit economics — and present sustainable FCF as a range. Detection: maintenance capex should be stable relative to revenue or asset base; if the estimate swings with the expansion cycle, or is far below depreciation for a mature asset base, it is mis-specified.

Ignoring lease payments for lease-heavy retailers

Ignoring Ind AS 116 lease payments when computing FCF for lease-heavy retailers like Trent

Mistake: computing FCF = CFO − capex for Trent, DMart, Westlife or PVR INOX without deducting lease principal and interest, which sit in financing. Why it happens: the textbook formula uses CFO directly and leases look like "debt service". Correct treatment: either deduct lease payments in FCF and exclude lease liabilities from net debt (rent-adjusted approach), or keep FCF before lease payments and deduct lease liabilities as debt in the EV bridge — with ROU additions considered as investment. Detection: compare the FCF yield with peers on both bases; a retailer with an FCF yield several times its PAT yield is a red flag that lease payments were missed.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated case: fashion retailer cash forensics [L3]

A NSE-listed fashion retailer reports for FY2023–FY2025 (₹ crore): revenue 8,000; 11,500; 15,200. EBITDA (post-Ind AS 116) 1,280; 1,840; 2,500. Reported CFO 1,050; 1,310; 1,900. Lease principal paid 300; 410; 520. Lease interest paid 180; 240; 300. Interest paid on borrowings 30; 40; 35. Purchase of PPE 600; 900; 1,150. Store count (opening FY2023: 520) closing 640; 790; 950; new-store fit-out capex ₹4.5 crore per store; refurbishment every 6 years at ₹1.5 crore per store. Capital creditors rose by ₹20, ₹45 and ₹70 crore. Tax rate 25.168%. (a) Compute reported and rent-adjusted CFO/EBITDA conversion each year and cumulatively. (b) Estimate maintenance capex by the per-store method. (c) Compute FCFF under the rent-adjusted approach and sustainable FCFF. (d) Explain how capital creditors affect your interpretation of capex.

Q2. Integrated case: manufacturing company with working-capital engineering [L3]

An auto ancillary company's FY2025 data (₹ crore): PBT 640; finance cost 110; D&A 220; interest income 25; profit on sale of land 60; ECL charge 15. Trade receivables (gross) 1,050 → 980 despite 22% revenue growth; non-recourse factored receivables outstanding 120 → 460. Inventories 700 → 820; trade payables 640 → 900, of which supplier finance payables 0 → 230 (tenor extended from 60 to 150 days, interest-bearing). Current tax 160; tax paid 175. (a) Reconstruct CFO. (b) Adjust CFO for factoring and SCF. (c) Compute reported and adjusted CFO/EBITDA. (d) Draft a 150-word note to the credit committee on cash quality and adjusted net debt.

💡 Hint: Remove the land sale profit and interest income from the operating section before working capital.

Q3. FCFF and FCFE for an Ind AS reporter [L2]

A cement company reports FY2025 (₹ crore): EBIT 2,100 (normalised); D&A on owned assets 780; CFO 2,450; interest paid 260 (financing); capex 3,000 including growth capex of 2,300 per management; NWC increased by 90; new borrowings 1,800; repayments 900; tax rate 25.168%. (a) Compute FCFF via the EBIT route and the CFO route and explain the difference. (b) Compute FCFE. (c) Compute capex intensity on revenue of ₹16,000 crore and sustainable FCFF using both maintenance proxies.

Q4. Cross-GAAP peer comparison [L2]

Compare an Indian jewellery retailer (Ind AS CFO ₹3,400 crore; interest paid ₹480 crore; lease payments ₹350 crore; EBITDA ₹5,200 crore post-Ind AS 116) with a US jeweller (US GAAP CFO USD 820 million; interest paid USD 60 million; operating lease cost USD 210 million inside CFO; EBITDA USD 1,100 million, struck after the single operating lease cost). Restate both to a "CFO after interest and leases" basis and compute comparable conversion ratios. Discuss two remaining non-comparabilities (for example, gold metal loans and customer schemes).
