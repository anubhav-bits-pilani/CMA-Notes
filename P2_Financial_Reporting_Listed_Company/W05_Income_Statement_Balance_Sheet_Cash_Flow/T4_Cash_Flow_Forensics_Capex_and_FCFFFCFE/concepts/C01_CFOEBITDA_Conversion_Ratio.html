Key concept

CFO/EBITDA Conversion Ratio

Concept: CFO/EBITDA conversion = CFO ÷ EBITDA; cumulative 3–5 years is more reliable than one year

Statement

\[ \text{CFO/EBITDA conversion} = \frac{\text{CFO}}{\text{EBITDA}} \times 100\% \]

\[ \text{Cumulative conversion}_{t-n+1 \ldots t} = \frac{\sum_{i=t-n+1}^{t} \text{CFO}_i}{\sum_{i=t-n+1}^{t} \text{EBITDA}_i} \]

where CFO is net cash from operating activities (after taxes paid, before interest for an Ind AS non-financial entity), EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation under a documented definition, and \( n \) is the number of years (typically 3–5).

Explanation

EBITDA is an accrual measure; CFO is its cash counterpart after working capital and taxes. A conversion ratio of 70–90% is typical for a healthy Indian non-financial company: the gap is roughly taxes paid (about 20–25% of PBT, i.e. 12–18% of EBITDA for many companies) plus working-capital investment in a growing business. Ratios persistently below 50% suggest revenue recognised ahead of cash, inventory build-up, capitalisation of operating costs or aggressive EBITDA definitions. Ratios above 100% can be legitimate (negative working capital models such as FMCG with dealer advances, or jewellers with customer schemes) or engineered (factoring, SCF, tax refunds).

Why cumulative? Single-year CFO is noisy: festive-season timing around 31 March, a large customer paying a day late, inventory pre-buying before a price increase, tax refunds or one-off advances. Summing three to five years lets timing differences reverse and reveals the structural conversion rate. Use the sum of CFO over the sum of EBITDA, not the average of yearly ratios — the latter overweights small-EBITDA years.

Consistency conditions:

  • Post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA pairs with reported Ind AS CFO (lease payments in financing); rent-adjusted EBITDA pairs with lease-adjusted CFO.
  • Ind AS CFO excludes interest paid; a US GAAP CFO includes it. Use "CFO before interest" for cross-GAAP peers.
  • If EBITDA excludes other income, CFO should exclude operating cash from items classified as other income, or the ratio is biased upward.
  • Exceptional cash items (VRS paid, litigation settlement) depress CFO but are excluded from normalised EBITDA; decide whether to normalise the numerator too.

Illustration

₹ crore FY2021 FY2022 FY2023 FY2024 FY2025 Cumulative
EBITDA 300 520 610 700 820 2,950
CFO 410 210 480 520 700 2,320
Yearly conversion 137% 40% 79% 74% 85% 79%

FY2021 looks exceptional (working capital released in a COVID year) and FY2022 alarming (inventory rebuild), but the five-year cumulative 79% shows a normal business. The simple average of yearly ratios would be 83% — overstated because the low-EBITDA year carries the highest ratio.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute cumulative conversion [L1]

EBITDA (₹ crore) FY2022–FY2025: 1,100; 1,240; 1,380; 1,560. CFO: 980; 760; 1,190; 1,050. Compute yearly conversion, the simple average of yearly ratios and the cumulative ratio, and explain which you would report.

Q2. Consistent basis for a retailer [L2]

A retailer reports post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA of ₹2,100 crore and CFO of ₹1,750 crore. Lease principal paid ₹520 crore, lease interest paid ₹260 crore. A peer reports rent-adjusted EBITDA of ₹900 crore and lease-adjusted CFO of ₹610 crore. Compare their conversion on a like-for-like basis.

💡 Hint: Put both companies on the same lease basis for numerator and denominator before comparing.

Q3. Interview: above-100% conversion [L2]

A jewellery retailer's three-year cumulative CFO/EBITDA is 118%. Give three legitimate and three questionable explanations, and the note disclosures that would distinguish them.
