Key concept

FCFE from FCFF and Capex Intensity

Concept: FCFE = FCFF − Interest × (1 − t) + Net borrowing; Capex intensity = Capex ÷ Revenue

Statement

\[ \text{FCFE} = \text{FCFF} - \text{Interest} \times (1 - t) + \text{Net borrowing} \]

\[ \text{Capex intensity} = \frac{\text{Capex}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \times 100\% \]

where:

  • \( \text{FCFF} \) = free cash flow to the firm (unlevered, after operating taxes);
  • \( \text{Interest} \) = interest paid on borrowings (and on lease liabilities if leases are treated as debt);
  • \( t \) = marginal tax rate (25.168% under s.115BAA), so \( \text{Interest} \times (1 - t) \) is the after-tax cost of debt service;
  • \( \text{Net borrowing} \) = new borrowings raised − repayments (if leases are debt, include lease principal repaid as a negative and new leases as non-cash borrowing only if ROU additions are in capex);
  • \( \text{Capex} \) = purchase of PPE and intangibles for the period, gross or net of disposal proceeds as documented.

Explanation

FCFE is the cash available to equity shareholders after servicing and raising debt — the theoretical maximum dividend plus buyback capacity. It is discounted at the cost of equity to give equity value directly. Moving from FCFF to FCFE subtracts what lenders receive (after-tax interest, because the company gets the tax shield) and adds what lenders provide (net new borrowing).

Directly from Ind AS CFO for a non-financial company:

\[ \text{FCFE} = \text{CFO} - \text{Interest paid} - \text{Capex} + \text{Net borrowing} \left(- \text{Lease payments}\right) \]

Because taxes paid in CFO already reflect the interest shield, this version subtracts gross interest.

Cautions: FCFE in a single year can be inflated by borrowing; a company that borrows to pay dividends shows high FCFE but is not creating value. FCFE suits banks and stable-leverage companies; FCFF suits companies with changing capital structures. Dividends received from subsidiaries and treasury income are additional equity cash flows at the standalone level but must not be double counted with consolidated FCFE.

Capex intensity normalises investment for size. Indian benchmarks (indicative, vary by cycle): FMCG 2–4%, IT services 2–3%, apparel retail 5–10%, cement 10–20% in expansion years, telecom and power 20%+. Compare intensity with D&A ÷ revenue: intensity persistently above depreciation intensity implies a growing asset base; below implies under-investment or a harvesting phase. Split into maintenance and growth intensity where possible (s05, c05).

Illustration

FY2025 (₹ crore): FCFF 707.6; interest paid 80; \( t \) = 25.168%; new term loans 200; repayments 140; revenue 9,600; capex 480.

Line Amount
FCFF 707.6
Less interest × (1 − 25.168%) = 80 × 0.74832 (59.9)
Add net borrowing (200 − 140) 60.0
FCFE 707.7
Capex intensity = 480 ÷ 9,600 5.0%

If the company paid dividends of ₹900 crore in the same year, payout exceeded FCFE by about ₹190 crore — funded from surplus cash, which is sustainable only while that cash lasts.

Practice Questions

Q1. FCFF to FCFE [L1]

FCFF ₹1,150 crore, interest paid ₹210 crore, tax 25.168%, borrowings raised ₹400 crore, repaid ₹650 crore. Compute FCFE and state whether the company can sustain a dividend of ₹700 crore.

Q2. Direct FCFE from Ind AS CFO [L2]

CFO ₹2,050 crore; interest paid ₹180 crore (financing); capex ₹900 crore; proceeds from borrowings ₹500 crore; repayment of borrowings ₹300 crore; lease principal ₹150 crore; lease interest ₹70 crore; revenue ₹14,500 crore; D&A excluding ROU ₹520 crore. Compute FCFE (leases treated as operating cost), capex intensity and depreciation intensity, and interpret the gap between the two intensities.

💡 Hint: Gross interest is subtracted here because the tax shield is already inside taxes paid.

Q3. Judgement: high FCFE, weak business [L3]

A promoter-controlled company reports FCFE exceeding PAT for three years and pays out 100% of FCFE as dividends. Net debt has risen from ₹800 crore to ₹2,100 crore over the same period while capex intensity fell from 9% to 3%. Evaluate the quality of FCFE and the sustainability of the dividend policy.
