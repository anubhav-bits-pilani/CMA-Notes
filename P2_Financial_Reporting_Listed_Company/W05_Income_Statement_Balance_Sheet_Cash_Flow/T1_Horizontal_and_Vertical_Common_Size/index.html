Roadmap topic: Horizontal (Trend) & Vertical (Common-Size) Analysis: Indexing 3–5 years of P&L and Balance Sheet items to detect structural margin compression or asset bloat

Overview

Horizontal (trend) analysis indexes each line of the financial statements to a base year to show how it has moved over time; vertical (common-size) analysis expresses each line as a share of a single denominator — revenue from operations for the P&L, total assets for the balance sheet. Together they are the first analytical pass over any set of Schedule III statements. They turn three to five years of numbers into a small number of questions: which costs are rising faster than revenue (structural margin compression), and which assets are rising faster than revenue (asset bloat)?

For the target roles this topic is foundational. An FP&A analyst uses the same mechanics for budget-vs-actual and cost-as-%-of-revenue planning; an equity or credit analyst builds the trend and common-size sheet before writing a single line of an initiation report or credit note; a financial-reporting analyst uses it as an analytical review before sign-off; and in modelling, these tables are the historical columns that anchor forecast assumptions. The technique is simple — the value lies in getting clean, comparable inputs and in reading the output correctly.

The learning sequence follows the workflow. Start with base-year indexing of the Schedule III Div II P&L lines (s01) and the common-size P&L and balance sheet with the correct denominators (s02). Before trusting any series, restate comparatives for regroupings and Ind AS 8 restatements (s03) and identify structural breaks — Ind AS 116, GST, acquisitions and demergers (s04). With clean data, diagnose margin compression — gross margin vs cost creep, operating leverage vs mix (s05) — and asset bloat in receivables, inventory, CWIP, capital advances and loans (s06). Finally, choose the right growth measure — YoY, CAGR or index — and keep the view current with an LTM built from Reg 33 quarterly results (s07).

The key concepts formalise the tools: the trend index (c01), the common-size percentage (c02), CAGR (c03), the nature-of-expense format and COGS build (c04), the Ind AS 1 limits on company subtotals (c05) and the Schedule III 2021 ageing and ratio disclosures that supply ready-made inputs (c06). Topic 2 then normalizes the earnings that these tables describe; Topic 3 reclassifies the balance sheet that the common-size view summarises.

Related concepts to study

IFRS/IAS 1 function-of-expense vs Ind AS nature method

IFRS/IAS 1 allows function-of-expense ("cost of sales") presentation; Ind AS Div II mandates nature method, so global gross margins are not like-for-like

Under IAS 1.99 a company may classify expenses by nature or by function; most global consumer and industrial companies report "cost of sales", which includes production labour, factory overheads and production depreciation. Ind AS 1 removed the function option, and Schedule III Div II requires nature lines. An Indian "gross margin" derived as revenue minus materials, purchases of stock-in-trade and inventory change is therefore a material margin, typically 10–20 percentage points higher than an IFRS gross margin for a manufacturer. When benchmarking against global peers, either rebuild an Indian cost of sales by adding conversion costs from the expense notes, or compare EBITDA and EBIT margins, which are less sensitive to classification. See c04.

IFRS 18 categories and MPM disclosures

IFRS 18 (effective 2027) mandates operating/investing/financing P&L categories and MPM disclosures; no Ind AS equivalent notified yet

IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements replaces IAS 1 for IFRS reporters for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027. It requires income and expenses to be classified into operating, investing, financing (plus income tax and discontinued operations) categories, with mandatory subtotals "operating profit" and "profit before financing and income taxes". It also requires management-defined performance measures (MPMs) used in public communication to be disclosed in a single note with reconciliations and tax/NCI effects, and tightens aggregation/disaggregation rules. For Indian analysts, IFRS-reporting peers will report a defined operating profit from 2027 while Ind AS reporters will not; ICAI's work on a corresponding Ind AS should be tracked, as no equivalent had been notified by MCA at the time of writing. Treasury income placed in "investing" under IFRS 18 aligns with excluding other income from core EBITDA here.

Excel tables, INDEX/XLOOKUP and Power Query stacking

Excel structured tables and INDEX/XLOOKUP for multi-year layout → Week 9; Power Query to stack XBRL/annual-report extracts → Week 10

A robust trend sheet stores raw data in a long (tidy) table — company, basis (standalone/consolidated), year, line item, value, source — and builds wide index and common-size views with XLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH, so adding a year or company does not break formulas. Structured table references (Data[Value]) make formulas self-documenting. Week 9 covers these patterns. Week 10 shows how Power Query can import MCA XBRL filings, NSE results files or annual-report extracts, unpivot years into rows, map company-specific line names to a standard chart of lines, and stack multiple companies and years into one table — the exact data layer needed for the Titan vs Trent exercise this week.

Historical columns of the 3-statement model

Feeds historical columns of the 3-statement model → Week 13

The cleaned, restated, break-adjusted series built in this topic become the historical columns of the integrated 3-statement model in Week 13. Common-size ratios become forecast drivers (COGS %, employee % with a fixed/variable split, other expense sub-lines), working-capital days from s06 become balance-sheet drivers, and CAGRs and LTM figures anchor near-term revenue assumptions. Any inconsistency left in the history — an unrestated regrouping, a mixed standalone/consolidated year, an Ind AS 116 break — propagates into the forecast as a spurious trend. That is why the restatement and break discipline of s03–s04 matters more than the indexing arithmetic.

Common pitfalls

Abnormal base year treated as the starting point for growth

Indexing a base year that is abnormal (COVID FY2020-21) and calling the rebound "growth"

FY2020-21 had lockdown-hit revenue for most consumer, retail and industrial businesses. Indexing from it, or computing a CAGR from it, makes the FY2022–FY2023 rebound look like structural growth, and cost ratios in the base year (fixed costs over depressed revenue) make later years look like margin expansion. People do it because the base defaults to the first column available. Correct treatment: start from FY2019-20 (pre-COVID, though Q4 was partly affected) or FY2021-22 onwards, or present two bases; label any CAGR that spans FY2021 as including a rebound. Detection in review: look at the first YoY figure — growth above 40% for a mature company, or a base-year margin far below the rest, signals an abnormal base.

Total income used as the denominator

Using total income as the denominator, letting treasury other income distort margin percentages

The Schedule III P&L totals revenue and other income into "total income", so it is tempting to use it. For cash-rich Indian companies, other income (interest, FVTPL gains on debt funds, dividends) can be 5–15% of revenue and is volatile with interest rates; using total income dilutes every cost ratio and, if EBITDA includes other income, inflates margins. Correct treatment: use revenue from operations as the only P&L denominator; show other income as a memo line below EBITDA; strip it from core EBITDA (Topic 2). Detection: recompute the reported margin on revenue from operations — if the analyst's figures do not match, check the denominator; compare margins of a cash-rich and a debt-funded peer for implausible gaps.

Mixed standalone/consolidated figures or ignored restatements

Mixing standalone and consolidated figures across years, or ignoring restated prior-year comparatives

Data vendors and hurried analysts sometimes pull consolidated figures for recent years and standalone for older years (when the company had no subsidiaries or did not publish consolidated quarterly results before FY2019-20), or paste each year from its own annual report, missing later restatements. The series then contains perimeter and accounting breaks that look like performance. Correct treatment: choose one basis and apply it to all years; source each year from the latest report presenting it; log every restatement and regrouping (s03). Detection: tie each year's revenue and PAT to the latest report's comparative; check that consolidated PAT includes NCI and share of associates in all years; look for step changes that coincide with report years rather than business events.

Practice Questions

Q1. Titan-style integrated trend and common-size case [L3]

A jewellery and watch company reports consolidated figures (₹ crore):

FY2022 FY2023 FY2024 FY2025
Revenue from operations 28,800 40,600 51,100 60,500
Other income 230 330 470 520
COGS (derived) 21,600 30,050 38,600 46,500
Employee benefits expense 1,300 1,640 1,980 2,400
Other expenses 2,300 3,450 4,600 5,500
D&A 360 420 520 640
Finance costs 220 300 560 820
Inventories 15,600 19,800 26,800 33,500
Trade receivables 440 640 900 1,400
Total assets 21,800 27,500 36,900 45,200

The FY2024 annual report notes a regrouping of ₹45 crore of credit-card commission from finance costs to other expenses for FY2023 only; FY2022 contains ₹38 crore of the same item in finance costs. In FY2024 the company acquired an additional stake in a subsidiary (already consolidated). Average gold price rose 15%, 13% and 22% in FY2023–FY2025.

(a) Restate the series for the regrouping. (b) Build index and common-size P&L tables, and inventory and receivable days. (c) Decompose the EBITDA margin change FY2022–FY2025 in bps. (d) Assess whether the inventory and receivables trends indicate bloat, considering gold prices. (e) Compute revenue CAGR and comment on the base year.

💡 Hint: Move the commission out of finance costs for FY2022 before indexing; express inventory both in days and relative to the gold price change.

Q2. Retailer structural break and LTM [L3]

An apparel retailer reports (₹ crore, standalone): FY2019 revenue 2,100, EBITDA 190, rent in other expenses 160; FY2020 revenue 2,450, EBITDA 420, lease payments in CFS 245 (variable rent in other expenses 20); FY2025 revenue 9,800, EBITDA 1,650, lease payments 610. H1 FY2025 revenue 4,300, EBITDA 700, lease payments 290; H1 FY2026 revenue 5,200, EBITDA 860, lease payments 345.

(a) Compute reported and rent-adjusted EBITDA margins for FY2019, FY2020 and FY2025. (b) Compute LTM September 2025 revenue and rent-adjusted EBITDA. (c) Compute revenue CAGR FY2019–FY2025 and FY2020–FY2025 and explain which better reflects the business. (d) Explain how you would present the Ind AS 116 break in a chart.

Q3. Asset bloat forensic review [L3]

A mid-cap engineering company's revenue index (FY2021 = 100) reached 138 in FY2025. Indices for trade receivables 196, contract assets 260, inventory 151, CWIP 410, capital advances 620 and loans to related parties 1,450. The receivables ageing shows > 1 year balances rising from 8% to 27% of the total while the ECL allowance fell from 4.5% to 3.0% of gross receivables. Reported CFO cumulatively over four years was 42% of cumulative EBITDA.

(a) Compute bloat ratios and rank the red flags. (b) Identify the Schedule III 2021 disclosures, Ind AS notes and CARO 2020 clauses you would review for each. (c) Draft five questions for the audit committee. (d) Explain how each issue would affect the historical columns and working-capital assumptions of a 3-statement model.

Q4. Cross-border peer comparison [L2]

You must compare an NSE-listed paints company (Ind AS, nature of expense; revenue ₹21,000 crore, materials and traded goods ₹12,300 crore, employee cost ₹1,450 crore, other expenses ₹3,350 crore, of which ₹520 crore relates to factory power, stores and contract labour; factory depreciation ₹380 crore of total D&A ₹700 crore) with a European peer reporting under IFRS by function (gross margin 41%, EBITDA margin 17%). From 2027 the European peer will report under IFRS 18.

(a) Compute the Indian company's Schedule III-derived gross margin and an IFRS-style gross margin. (b) Compute EBITDA margin and explain why it is more comparable. (c) Explain what changes for the comparison when the peer adopts IFRS 18 and why no equivalent change occurs for the Ind AS reporter yet.
