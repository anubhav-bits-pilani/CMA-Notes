Subtopic note

Common-Size P&L on Revenue from Operations and BS on Total Assets

Scope: Common-size P&L as % of revenue from operations (not total income) and Balance Sheet as % of total assets

Why it matters

Vertical analysis converts every line into a share of one denominator, removing size so that a ₹500 crore company can be compared with a ₹50,000 crore one, and a company can be compared with itself across years. It is the backbone of peer benchmarking tables in equity research, of credit appraisal notes (cost structure, asset mix) and of FP&A budgeting, where cost lines are planned as % of revenue. The choice of denominator is not cosmetic: using the wrong one silently corrupts every margin in the table.

Core notes

Common-size P&L: denominator is revenue from operations

\[ \text{Common-size \%}_{\text{P\&L line}} = \frac{\text{Line item}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \times 100 \]

Schedule III Div II shows Revenue from operations and Other income separately and then a Total income line. The denominator must be revenue from operations because:

  • Margins are a measure of how much of each rupee earned from customers is retained. Other income (interest on deposits, FVTPL gains on liquid funds, dividends, profit on sale of investments) is not earned from customers and is not driven by the operating cost base.
  • Cash-rich Indian companies carry large treasury portfolios. A company with ₹1,000 crore revenue and ₹150 crore other income would show an employee-cost ratio of 8.7% of total income versus 10.0% of revenue — the treasury book makes the operations look leaner than they are.
  • Other income is volatile (mark-to-market on debt funds moves with yields), so a total-income denominator injects treasury volatility into every cost ratio.

Show other income as a separate line below EBITDA, expressed as % of revenue for information, and never fold it into the denominator.

Revenue from operations itself should be net of GST (GST is collected on behalf of government, Ind AS 115.47) and net of trade discounts, volume rebates and expected returns (variable consideration). Pre-July 2017 revenue included excise duty — see s04.

Common-size balance sheet: denominator is total assets

\[ \text{Common-size \%}_{\text{BS line}} = \frac{\text{Line item}}{\text{Total assets}} \times 100 \]

Total assets equals total equity and liabilities, so every line on both sides is expressed against one figure. This reveals:

  • Asset mix: fixed-asset intensity (PPE + CWIP + ROU), working-capital intensity (inventories + receivables), and treasury share (cash + current investments + other bank balances).
  • Funding mix: equity vs borrowings vs lease liabilities vs trade payables vs customer advances.

Alternative denominators exist for specific purposes — capital employed or invested capital (see Topic 3), or revenue (inventory and receivables as % of revenue, i.e., a days-like view). Use them as supplementary schedules, not as the main common-size balance sheet.

Building the tables

Common-size P&L row Formula
Revenue from operations 100.0%
COGS (materials + purchases of stock-in-trade + change in inventories) COGS ÷ Revenue
Gross margin 1 − COGS%
Employee benefits expense ÷ Revenue
Other expenses (and key sub-lines) ÷ Revenue
EBITDA (excl. other income) ÷ Revenue
D&A ÷ Revenue
EBIT (excl. other income) ÷ Revenue
Other income (memo) ÷ Revenue
Finance costs ÷ Revenue
Exceptional items ÷ Revenue
PBT, Tax, PAT ÷ Revenue 
Common-size P&L (cell D20):   =D5/D$4            (row 4 = revenue from operations)
Common-size BS  (cell D60):   =D45/D$58          (row 58 = total assets)
Check row:                    =ROUND(SUM(D46:D57)-D58,0)=0

Format as percentages with one decimal; display basis points change (bps) in an adjacent column, since margin movements are discussed in bps (1% = 100 bps).

Illustration (₹ crore)

Amount % of revenue % of total income
Revenue from operations 2,000 100.0% 88.9%
Other income 250 12.5% 11.1%
Total income 2,250 — 100.0%
Employee benefits expense 240 12.0% 10.7%
Other expenses 420 21.0% 18.7%
COGS 1,100 55.0% 48.9%
EBITDA excl. other income 240 12.0% 10.7%

Using total income, EBITDA including other income is 490 ÷ 2,250 = 21.8% — almost double the true operating margin of 12.0%. A peer with no treasury book and the same economics would look much weaker.

On the balance sheet side, suppose total assets are ₹3,000 crore with inventories ₹600 crore (20.0%), trade receivables ₹300 crore (10.0%), net PPE + ROU ₹900 crore (30.0%), cash and liquid investments ₹1,000 crore (33.3%). A third of the balance sheet is treasury — so ROA on total assets is understated relative to operating ROA, a point picked up again in Topic 3.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Other operating revenue: scrap sales, export incentives and PLI grants presented within revenue from operations are part of the denominator. If they are material and non-recurring, show a sub-total "revenue from sale of products and services" and use it as an alternative denominator (see Topic 2 s05).
  • Agent vs principal: a company acting as agent (Ind AS 115.B34–B38) reports commission only; a peer acting as principal grosses up. Common-size margins become non-comparable — consignment and marketplace models in retail are the usual cause.
  • Gold and commodity pass-through: jewellery revenue includes the gold value; gross margin % is low and moves with gold prices even if the making-charge margin is stable. Supplement with margin per gram or EBITDA per ₹ of non-gold value where disclosed.
  • Total assets inflated by treasury or held-for-sale assets: show an "operating total assets" denominator as a second view.
  • Negative equity or large DTA: common-size percentages still work; the interpretation (a DTA of 15% of total assets) becomes the analytical point.
  • Quarterly vs annual: common-size on a single quarter is distorted by seasonality (festive quarter for jewellery); compare like quarters or use LTM (see s07).

Key takeaways

  • P&L denominator = revenue from operations (net of GST, discounts and returns), never total income.
  • Show other income as a memo line below EBITDA so treasury earnings do not flatter cost ratios.
  • BS denominator = total assets; the same figure covers both asset mix and funding mix.
  • Express margin moves in basis points and add a check row that the common-size BS sums to 100%.
  • Watch agent/principal, commodity pass-through and treasury-heavy balance sheets before comparing peers.

Practice Questions

Q1. Denominator error [L1]

A company reports revenue from operations ₹1,600 crore, other income ₹180 crore, employee benefits expense ₹192 crore and EBITDA (excluding other income) ₹256 crore. Compute employee cost and EBITDA as % of revenue from operations and as % of total income, and state which pair should appear in a peer table.

Q2. Common-size balance sheet [L2]

From the following ₹ crore balances prepare a common-size balance sheet and comment on asset and funding mix: PPE 820, CWIP 140, ROU assets 610, goodwill 90, non-current investments 300, inventories 1,150, trade receivables 210, current investments 480, cash and cash equivalents 95, other bank balances 160, other current assets 145; equity 2,050, non-current borrowings 150, lease liabilities (total) 700, trade payables 840, other current liabilities 400, provisions 60.

💡 Hint: Confirm both sides total the same figure before computing percentages.

Q3. Agent vs principal comparability [L2]

Retailer A sells consignment goods as principal and reports revenue ₹3,000 crore with gross margin 35%. Retailer B sells similar goods but acts as agent for 40% of its gross merchandise value, reporting revenue ₹2,100 crore. Explain how their common-size gross margins and other-expense ratios will differ even with identical economics, and how you would restate them for comparison.

Q4. Gold price pass-through [L3]

A jeweller's gross margin fell from 23.5% to 21.8% in a year in which the average gold price rose 28% and making charges per gram were unchanged. Draft three lines of analyst commentary explaining why the common-size gross margin may overstate the deterioration, and propose two supplementary metrics.

Q5. Interview: why not total income? [L1]

An interviewer asks why you used revenue from operations rather than total income when the Schedule III statement itself totals income first. Give a concise, technically grounded answer that mentions Indian treasury practice.
