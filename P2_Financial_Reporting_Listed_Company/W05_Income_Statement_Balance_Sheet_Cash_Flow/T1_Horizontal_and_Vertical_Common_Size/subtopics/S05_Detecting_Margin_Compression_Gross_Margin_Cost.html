Subtopic note

Detecting Margin Compression: Gross Margin, Cost Creep and Leverage

Scope: Detecting margin compression: gross margin vs employee/other-expense creep; operating leverage vs mix effects

Why it matters

An EBITDA margin that falls 200 bps is a headline; why it fell is the analysis. The same 200 bps can come from input-cost inflation not passed on (gross margin), a hiring spree ahead of growth (employee creep), rising advertising or freight (other-expense creep), revenue falling against a fixed cost base (negative operating leverage) or a shift towards lower-margin products, channels or geographies (mix). Each cause has a different implication for forecasts, valuation and management questions: cost-pass-through issues reverse when commodity prices normalise; mix effects are structural; operating deleverage reverses with volume. FP&A teams perform the same decomposition monthly in variance analysis.

Core notes

Step 1: Decompose the EBITDA margin

Using the common-size P&L (revenue from operations = 100):

\[ \text{EBITDA margin} = \text{Gross margin} - \text{Employee \%} - \text{Other expenses \%} \]

Change in margin (in bps) equals change in gross margin minus change in employee % minus change in other-expense %. Build a waterfall:

bps change FY2024 → FY2025 Amount
EBITDA margin FY2024 15.0%
Gross margin change −120 bps
Employee cost % change −60 bps
Advertising % change −40 bps
Freight and power % change +20 bps
Other expenses % change (rest) 0 bps
EBITDA margin FY2025 13.0%

Step 2: Gross margin diagnosis

Gross margin = 1 − COGS ÷ revenue. Compression causes:

  • Input cost inflation not passed through — check commodity prices (palm oil, crude derivatives, steel, gold) against realisation per unit.
  • Pricing pressure / discounting — revenue per unit falling, often visible in volume vs value growth commentary.
  • Mix shift to lower gross-margin products or channels (e.g., e-commerce, modern trade, exports).
  • Inventory write-downs (Ind AS 2.34 — recognised as expense in the period) embedded in changes in inventories or COGS.
  • Make-vs-buy shifts: moving to purchased stock-in-trade lowers gross margin but may reduce employee cost and D&A — EBITDA margin may be unchanged.

Step 3: Cost creep

Employee cost and many other expenses are semi-fixed. "Creep" is when they grow faster than revenue for several consecutive periods without a matching benefit.

  • Employee creep: compare employee-cost index with revenue index and headcount (BRSR discloses employee numbers). Separate wage inflation (cost per employee) from headcount growth. Strip ESOP and Labour Code past-service cost first.
  • Other-expense creep: index the major sub-lines from the other expenses note — advertising and sales promotion, freight, power and fuel, rent (post-Ind AS 116, only short-term and variable), royalty, IT/outsourcing, legal and professional, CSR (2% of average net profit under s.135, mechanically linked to past profit, not revenue).

Step 4: Operating leverage vs mix effect

Operating leverage is the sensitivity of EBIT (or EBITDA) to revenue, driven by the fixed share of costs:

\[ \text{DOL} = \frac{\%\Delta\,\text{EBIT}}{\%\Delta\,\text{Revenue}} \approx \frac{\text{Contribution}}{\text{EBIT}} \]

where contribution = revenue − variable costs. With high fixed costs, a revenue decline compresses margins mechanically (negative operating leverage), and a recovery expands them.

Mix effect is the change in margin caused by a change in the composition of revenue at unchanged segment or product margins. Decompose using segment data (Ind AS 108) or product disclosures:

\[ \Delta m = \underbrace{\sum_i (w_{i,1} - w_{i,0})\, m_{i,0}}_{\text{mix}} + \underbrace{\sum_i w_{i,1}\,(m_{i,1} - m_{i,0})}_{\text{rate (within-segment margin)}} \]

where \(w_i\) is segment \(i\)'s share of revenue, \(m_i\) its margin, subscript 0 = prior year, 1 = current year.

Illustration: mix vs rate

Segment FY2024 rev share FY2024 margin FY2025 rev share FY2025 margin
Jewellery 80% 12.0% 86% 11.8%
Watches 15% 14.0% 10% 14.2%
Eyecare 5% 8.0% 4% 7.8%
Group margin 12.10% 11.88%

Mix effect = (0.86−0.80)×12.0 + (0.10−0.15)×14.0 + (0.04−0.05)×8.0 = 0.72 − 0.70 − 0.08 = −0.06 pp. Rate effect = 0.86×(−0.2) + 0.10×(+0.2) + 0.04×(−0.2) = −0.172 + 0.020 − 0.008 = −0.16 pp. Total −0.22 pp, matching 11.88% − 12.10%. Here the compression is mostly within-segment rate (jewellery margin), not mix.

Step 5: Separate fixed and variable costs

To distinguish operating leverage from genuine inefficiency, classify costs:

Typically variable Typically semi-fixed / fixed
Materials, purchases of stock-in-trade, freight outward, commissions, royalty on sales, packing Employee cost, fixed rent (now in D&A and finance cost), IT, insurance, depreciation

Regress quarterly costs on quarterly revenue over 8–12 quarters (Excel =SLOPE() and =INTERCEPT()) to estimate the variable rate and fixed base; or use the high-low method as a quick check.

Variable cost ratio:  =SLOPE(CostRange,RevenueRange)
Fixed cost per qtr:   =INTERCEPT(CostRange,RevenueRange)
DOL at current level: =(Rev-Rev*VarRatio)/(Rev-Rev*VarRatio-Fixed)

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Commodity pass-through businesses (jewellery, edible oils): gross margin % falls when commodity prices rise even if absolute margin per unit is intact. Analyse margin per unit or per gram, not %.
  • Seasonality: festive quarters dilute fixed costs; compare like quarters or LTM.
  • Investment phase: employee and advertising creep for a new format (e.g., a new retail concept) is deliberate. Check management guidance and store additions before calling it inefficiency.
  • Reclassification between COGS and other expenses (freight inward vs outward, packing materials) changes gross margin without changing EBITDA; check regrouping notes (s03).
  • Ind AS 116 break: rent moving out of other expenses makes other-expense % fall in FY2020 — not efficiency.
  • Mix decomposition with changing segment definitions: restate prior-year segments before decomposing.

Key takeaways

  • Decompose EBITDA margin change in bps into gross margin, employee and other-expense contributions.
  • Diagnose gross margin through price, input cost, mix, write-downs and make-vs-buy.
  • Cost creep = semi-fixed costs indexing above revenue for consecutive periods without a strategic reason.
  • Separate operating leverage (fixed-cost absorption) from mix (composition) and rate (within-segment margin) effects.
  • For commodity pass-through and seasonal businesses, analyse per-unit and LTM margins.

Practice Questions

Q1. Margin waterfall [L1]

A company's common-size P&L shows: FY2024 gross margin 42.0%, employee cost 9.5%, other expenses 17.5%; FY2025 gross margin 41.2%, employee cost 10.3%, other expenses 17.1%. Build the bps waterfall from FY2024 EBITDA margin to FY2025 EBITDA margin.

Q2. Mix vs rate decomposition [L2]

A two-segment company reports: Segment A revenue FY2024 ₹3,000 crore (EBIT margin 18%), FY2025 ₹3,150 crore (17.5%); Segment B revenue FY2024 ₹1,000 crore (EBIT margin 6%), FY2025 ₹1,850 crore (6.5%). Compute group margin in both years and decompose the change into mix and rate effects.

💡 Hint: Compute revenue weights first; the two effects must sum to the total change.

Q3. Operating leverage estimate [L2]

Quarterly revenue (₹ crore) for 8 quarters: 820, 760, 900, 1,050, 880, 800, 960, 1,120. Corresponding employee plus other expenses: 262, 254, 274, 296, 270, 259, 283, 306. Estimate the variable cost ratio and fixed cost per quarter, and compute DOL at quarterly revenue of ₹1,000 crore assuming a gross margin of 45%.

Q4. Investment phase or creep? [L3]

An apparel retailer's employee cost index (base FY2022 = 100) is 182 in FY2025 against a revenue index of 151; advertising index is 210. During the period it opened 380 stores of a new value format, with management guiding to store-level breakeven in 18 months. Write a short note to your portfolio manager assessing whether this is cost creep or planned investment, listing the data you would request and the metric you would track quarterly.

Q5. Interview: gold price and margin [L2]

A jeweller's EBITDA margin fell 90 bps while EBITDA in ₹ crore grew 15% and gold prices rose 20%. Explain to an interviewer why this may not be margin compression in an economic sense, and how you would present it in a peer table.
