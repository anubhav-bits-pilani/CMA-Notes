Subtopic note

Detecting Asset Bloat: Working Capital, CWIP, Advances and Loans

Scope: Detecting asset bloat: receivables, inventory, CWIP, capital advances and loans growing faster than revenue

Why it matters

Asset bloat is the balance-sheet counterpart of margin compression: assets growing faster than the revenue they are supposed to generate. It depresses asset turnover and ROCE, consumes cash (CFO falls behind EBITDA), and is a classic forensic red flag — channel stuffing shows up in receivables, obsolete stock in inventory, capitalised expenses or stalled projects in CWIP, and fund diversion in capital advances and loans to related parties. Lenders monitor it through drawing-power statements and stock audits; equity analysts through working-capital days and the CFO-to-EBITDA bridge; auditors through the 2021 Schedule III ageing disclosures and CARO 2020 reporting.

Core notes

The core test

For each asset line, compare its growth (or index) with revenue growth over the same period:

\[ \text{Bloat ratio}_t = \frac{\text{Asset index}_t}{\text{Revenue index}_t} \times 100 \]

A ratio persistently above 100 means the asset is growing faster than revenue. Pair this with turnover or days metrics:

Metric Formula Notes
Receivable days Trade receivables ÷ Revenue from operations × 365 Use average receivables for flows; include unbilled revenue (contract assets) for a full picture
Inventory days Inventories ÷ COGS × 365 COGS per Schedule III build; for jewellers also grams of gold held
Payable days Trade payables ÷ COGS (or purchases) × 365 Offset to above; supply chain finance can distort (Topic 4)
Fixed asset turnover Revenue ÷ (Net PPE + ROU) Exclude CWIP to see productivity of assets in use
CWIP to gross block CWIP ÷ Gross PPE Rising ratio with flat capitalisation = stalled projects
Capital advances to capex Capital advances ÷ annual capex High and rising = money parked with suppliers

Receivables

Growth faster than revenue signals: extended credit to push sales (channel stuffing), collection problems, disputes, or revenue recognised early. Read:

  • The ageing schedule (Schedule III 2021) — undisputed vs disputed, considered good / significant increase in credit risk / credit impaired, buckets from due date (< 6 months, 6 months–1 year, 1–2, 2–3, > 3 years).
  • The ECL allowance (Ind AS 109) — allowance falling as % of gross receivables while ageing worsens is a red flag.
  • Related-party receivables (Ind AS 24 note) — sales to group distributors.
  • Contract assets / unbilled revenue (Ind AS 115) — moving receivables into unbilled can hide ageing.

Inventory

Inventory growing faster than COGS signals slow-moving or obsolete stock, overproduction to absorb fixed overheads (inflating profit through overhead absorption under Ind AS 2), or a strategic build (pre-festive season, new stores). Check: inventory by category (RM, WIP, FG, stock-in-trade), write-down amounts (Ind AS 2.36(e)), stock-in-transit, and the bank's stock statement reconciliation (Schedule III 2021 requires disclosure where quarterly returns to banks do not agree with books).

CWIP

CWIP that grows while capitalisation (additions to PPE) stays flat indicates projects that are delayed, stalled or being used to park costs. Schedule III 2021 requires a CWIP ageing schedule (projects in progress vs temporarily suspended; < 1, 1–2, 2–3, > 3 years) and a completion schedule for projects overdue or over their original budget. Ind AS 23.20–21: capitalisation of borrowing costs must be suspended during extended periods when active development is suspended — continued capitalisation inflates both CWIP and profit.

Capital advances

Capital advances (other non-current assets) are amounts paid to suppliers of PPE before delivery. Large balances relative to capex, ageing without capitalisation, or advances to related parties are red flags for fund diversion. Treat persistent capital advances as potential "soft assets" (Topic 3 s05).

Loans and other financial assets

Loans to related parties, inter-corporate deposits and "other advances" growing faster than revenue warrant scrutiny. Schedule III 2021 requires disclosure of loans to promoters, directors, KMPs and related parties that are repayable on demand or without specified terms; CARO 2020 clause 3(iii) requires reporting on loans and guarantees given. Section 186 limits apply (Topic 5).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Growth businesses: inventory for new stores is opened before sales mature; compare per-store inventory and like-for-like metrics rather than group totals.
  • Gold price: a jeweller's inventory value rises with gold prices; analyse grams or hedge-adjusted values. Gold metal loans may fund the inventory (Topic 3 s06).
  • Factoring and bill discounting: derecognised receivables (Ind AS 109 derecognition) make receivable days look healthy; check the factoring disclosure.
  • Year-end window dressing: closing balances can be managed; use averages and quarterly Reg 33 half-year balance sheets (half-yearly balance sheet is published with Q2 results).
  • Large single projects: a greenfield plant legitimately inflates CWIP for 2–3 years. The question is whether it is on budget and on time (completion schedule).
  • Advance to suppliers of inventory vs capital advances: different buckets — the former is working capital, the latter capex.

Key takeaways

  • Bloat = asset index persistently above revenue index; quantify with a bloat ratio and days/turnover metrics.
  • Use the 2021 Schedule III ageing schedules for receivables and CWIP and the ECL allowance trend.
  • Inventory growing faster than COGS can inflate profit through overhead absorption.
  • Capital advances and related-party loans growing without capitalisation are fund-diversion red flags.
  • Confirm through CFO lagging EBITDA and adjust for growth, gold price and factoring effects.

Practice Questions

Q1. Bloat ratios and days [L1]

Revenue from operations (₹ crore): FY2023 3,200, FY2024 3,520, FY2025 3,760. COGS: 2,080, 2,320, 2,520. Trade receivables: 420, 530, 660. Inventories: 610, 700, 840. Compute bloat ratios (base FY2023), receivable days and inventory days on closing balances for each year.

Q2. CWIP ageing interpretation [L2]

A company's CWIP is ₹840 crore: projects in progress < 1 year ₹220 crore, 1–2 years ₹160 crore, 2–3 years ₹130 crore, > 3 years ₹210 crore; temporarily suspended > 3 years ₹120 crore. Borrowing costs capitalised during the year were ₹48 crore. Identify the red flags, the Ind AS 23 issue, and the effect on reported EBITDA, PBT and ROCE if ₹30 crore of the capitalised borrowing cost related to suspended projects.

💡 Hint: Consider Ind AS 23 suspension of capitalisation and where the interest should have been recognised.

Q3. Capital advances and related parties [L3]

A mid-cap manufacturer shows capital advances of ₹310 crore (FY2022: ₹35 crore), of which ₹240 crore is paid to an EPC contractor whose director is a relative of the promoter. Annual capex has averaged ₹150 crore. Draft five due-diligence questions and list the disclosures (Schedule III, Ind AS 24, CARO 2020, s.188) you would examine.

Q4. Receivables vs factoring [L2]

Two distributors each have revenue ₹2,400 crore. Distributor X shows receivables ₹200 crore and discloses ₹260 crore of receivables factored without recourse at year-end. Distributor Y shows receivables ₹430 crore and no factoring. Compute reported and factoring-adjusted receivable days for X and explain how a bloat analysis comparing X and Y should be adjusted.

Q5. Build a bloat dashboard [L3]

Specify an Excel or Power BI dashboard that, for a 5-year series, shows revenue index vs indices of receivables, inventories, CWIP, capital advances and related-party loans, flags any line with a bloat ratio above 120 for two consecutive years, and links each flag to the relevant note (ageing, ECL, CWIP completion schedule). List the measures and the data tables required.
