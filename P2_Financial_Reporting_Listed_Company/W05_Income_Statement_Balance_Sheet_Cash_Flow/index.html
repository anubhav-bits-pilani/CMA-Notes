How this week fits

Week 5 opens Phase 2, Financial Reporting & Listed Company Analysis. Weeks 1–4 rebuilt the technical accounting base — Ind AS 115 revenue, Ind AS 116 leases, Ind AS 109 financial instruments and ECL, Ind AS 12 deferred tax, Schedule III presentation and consolidation mechanics. This week turns that knowledge around: instead of preparing statements, you dissect them as an analyst, lender or FP&A reviewer would, to answer two questions — what does this business earn on a recurring basis, and how much cash does it really generate?

The six topics form one workflow. Topic 1 (trend and common-size analysis) makes multi-year statements comparable and highlights margin compression and asset bloat. Topic 2 normalizes EBITDA and PAT. Topic 3 rebuilds the balance sheet into operating invested capital, surplus cash, non-operating assets and debt-like claims. Topic 4 reconciles EBITDA to CFO and derives FCFF and FCFE. Topic 5 separates standalone and consolidated performance. Topic 6 applies the same lens to segments.

What comes next depends on this week's output. Week 6 computes ROIC and returns on the invested capital built here; Weeks 9–10 automate the data layer in Excel and Power Query; Weeks 13–14 use the normalized history as the historical columns of the 3-statement model; Weeks 15–16 use core EBITDA, net debt and FCF in DCF and relative valuation; Week 22 revisits normalization as quality-of-earnings work in M&A.

Schedule III Division II formats and 2021 additional information

Schedule III Div II formats, notes structure and 2021 additional regulatory information

  • Balance sheet: non-current assets (PPE, CWIP, investment property, goodwill, other intangibles, intangibles under development, biological assets, financial assets — investments, trade receivables, loans, others — DTA, other non-current assets); current assets (inventories, financial assets — investments, trade receivables, cash and cash equivalents, bank balances other than cash, loans, others — current tax assets, other current assets); assets held for sale. Equity (share capital, other equity), NCI in CFS; liabilities split non-current/current with financial liabilities (borrowings, lease liabilities, trade payables with MSME split, other financial liabilities), provisions, deferred tax liabilities, other liabilities.
  • P&L: revenue from operations, other income, expenses by nature (materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade, changes in inventories, employee benefits, finance costs, D&A, other expenses), profit before exceptional items and tax, exceptional items, PBT, tax, continuing and discontinued operations, OCI (items not reclassified / reclassified), total comprehensive income, EPS.
  • 2021 amendments (FY2021-22 onwards): current maturities of long-term debt in short-term borrowings; security deposits in other financial assets; ageing schedules for receivables, payables, CWIP and intangibles under development; 11 ratios with explanations for changes above 25%; promoter shareholding; title deeds; loans to related parties repayable on demand; quarterly bank returns vs books; wilful defaulter; struck-off companies; layering; fund-routing through intermediaries; undisclosed income; CSR; crypto.

Ind AS 115, 116, 109 and 12 essentials

Ind AS 115, 116, 109 (ECL, FVTPL), 12 (deferred tax) basics → Weeks 1–4

  • Ind AS 115: five-step model; variable consideration and returns reduce revenue; principal vs agent (gross vs net); contract assets (conditional rights) and contract liabilities (advances, deferred revenue, loyalty points); significant financing component.
  • Ind AS 116: ROU asset and lease liability at PV of payments; exemptions for short-term and low-value leases; variable payments not linked to an index stay in operating expenses; depreciation and front-loaded interest replace rent; most Indian adopters used the modified retrospective approach from 1 April 2019 without restating FY2018-19.
  • Ind AS 109: classification by business model and SPPI; mutual fund units fail SPPI and are FVTPL; equity investments FVTPL or irrevocable FVOCI; ECL simplified approach with provision matrix for trade receivables.
  • Ind AS 12: deferred tax on temporary differences using enacted/substantively enacted rates; DTA recognised only when probable; MAT credit presented within DTA; tax-rate reconciliation (Ind AS 12.81(c)); remeasurement on rate change (e.g., s.115BAA switch) through P&L.

Ind AS 7 indirect method and classification choices

Ind AS 7 indirect method mechanics and classification choices

  • Indirect method: PBT → add back non-cash items (D&A, impairment, ESOP cost, unrealised forex, ECL) → remove investing/financing items in P&L (interest income, dividend income, FVTPL gains, profit on sale of PPE/investments, finance costs) → working-capital changes (receivables, inventories, payables, other operating items) → cash generated from operations → income taxes paid → CFO.
  • Classification for non-financial entities under Ind AS 7.33: interest paid in financing; interest and dividends received in investing; dividends paid in financing (Ind AS 7.34); taxes in operating unless specifically identifiable with investing/financing.
  • Ind AS 116 in CFS: lease principal in financing; lease interest per interest-paid policy (financing); short-term, low-value and variable payments in operating.
  • Cash equivalents (Ind AS 7.6–7): short-term, highly liquid, insignificant risk — typically ≤ 3 months' original maturity; bank overdrafts repayable on demand may be netted (Ind AS 7.8).
  • Ind AS 7.44A–44E: reconciliation of liabilities arising from financing activities (cash and non-cash changes — new leases, forex, fair value).

Ind AS 110/28 consolidation basics

Ind AS 110/28 consolidation basics, NCI and elimination entries

  • Control (Ind AS 110.7): power over the investee, exposure to variable returns, ability to use power to affect returns. Subsidiaries consolidated line by line from acquisition date.
  • NCI: measured at fair value or proportionate share of net assets at acquisition (Ind AS 103.19); share of profit and OCI attributed; presented within equity separately from owners' equity; total comprehensive income attributed even if NCI becomes negative.
  • Eliminations: intra-group balances, transactions, unrealised profit in inventory and PPE (with deferred tax on eliminations under Ind AS 12); dividends from subsidiaries eliminated against retained earnings.
  • Associates and JVs (Ind AS 28, Ind AS 111): equity method — cost plus share of post-acquisition profit and OCI less dividends; share of profit presented as a single line; losses stop at zero unless obligations exist; unrealised profit on upstream/downstream transactions eliminated to the extent of the investor's interest.
  • Separate financial statements (Ind AS 27): investments in subsidiaries, associates and JVs at cost or under Ind AS 109.

Cross-cutting concepts

One definition set across all years and companies

One consistent definition set (EBITDA, net debt, invested capital) applied to all years and companies

Every metric this week — EBITDA, core PAT, net debt, invested capital, FCF — is analyst-defined. Ind AS does not define EBITDA; companies' "capital employed" and "net debt" vary; brokers' definitions differ again. The only way to produce comparable numbers is to write one definition set before collecting data and apply it mechanically to every company and every year. The definition set should cover: EBITDA (other income excluded; exceptional and non-recurring items reversed after a recurrence test; lease basis; operating forex included; ESOP kept; associates excluded), net debt (borrowings including current maturities and CP; lease liabilities if and only if EBITDA is post-116; free cash and liquid investments net of earmarked and operating cash; debt-like items listed), and invested capital (operating NWC, net PPE, ROU if post-116, goodwill and intangibles shown with and without, CWIP policy). The pairing rules matter as much as the definitions: whatever is excluded from EBITDA must be added or deducted in the EV bridge, and whatever is in invested capital must not be in net debt. In practice, put the definitions on the first tab of the workbook and reference them in every schedule's header; when a company's disclosure forces a deviation, log it in the adjustments register.

Accrual earnings must reconcile to cash

Accrual vs cash earnings: P&L normalization must reconcile to the CFO bridge

Normalization is not complete until it reconciles to cash. Core EBITDA is an accrual number; CFO is a cash number; the bridge between them (EBITDA − ΔNWC − taxes paid ± provisions and other non-cash items = CFO) reveals whether the earnings are backed by cash. Two consistency rules link the P&L work to the cash flow work. First, every non-cash adjustment in the P&L bridge must have a counterpart in the CFO bridge — an impairment added back in core EBITDA is already a non-cash add-back in the indirect method; a provision reversal deducted from core EBITDA appears as a non-cash item in CFO. Second, cash one-offs must be treated symmetrically: if a VRS payment is removed from core EBITDA as non-recurring, the cash paid should be shown as a separate non-recurring outflow in the normalized CFO, not silently left inside working capital. Over 3–5 years, cumulative CFO ÷ cumulative EBITDA for a healthy Indian consumer company typically sits around 70–90% (after taxes and working capital); persistently lower conversion signals aggressive revenue recognition, capitalisation or working-capital build. Build the P&L bridge and the CFO bridge in the same workbook, from the same adjustments register, and add a check that the sum of non-cash adjustments is consistent across both.

Ind AS 116 and other income as the two largest swing factors

Ind AS 116 and other income are the two largest swing factors in Indian retail/consumer EBITDA

For Indian retailers and consumer companies, two items dwarf all others in moving EBITDA between definitions. Ind AS 116 shifts fixed rent below EBITDA: for a lease-heavy apparel or value retailer, lease payments are 5–9% of revenue, so post-116 EBITDA margins can exceed pre-116 margins by that much. Other income — interest on deposits, FVTPL gains on liquid and debt funds, dividends, gains on sale of investments — is large for cash-rich consumer companies, often 2–6% of revenue and sometimes more than 20% of PBT; and it moves with interest rates (debt-fund gains spike when yields fall). A peer table that mixes bases on these two items can show margin differences of 10 percentage points between companies with identical store economics. The discipline: (a) always exclude treasury other income from core EBITDA and add the treasury assets in the EV bridge; (b) present both post-116 and rent-adjusted EBITDA for any company where lease payments exceed 2% of revenue, with matching net debt; (c) in commentary, decompose margin changes into these two effects first, before discussing gross margin or cost creep.

Titan vs Trent comparability adjustments

Exercise: Titan vs Trent common-size tables need jewellery gold-loan and retail lease adjustments for comparability

Titan (jewellery-dominated, with watches, eyecare and other segments) and Trent (Westside, Zudio, Star via an equity-accounted JV) are both Tata-group consumer retailers, but their balance sheets and P&Ls differ structurally. Titan's revenue embeds the gold value, so gross margins are low and move with gold prices; its inventory is very large, funded partly by gold metal loans and customer purchase-scheme advances. Trent's model is lease-heavy, so Ind AS 116 inflates its EBITDA and puts large lease liabilities on the balance sheet, while its working capital is light. Common-size tables therefore need explicit adjustments: for Titan, a stated policy on gold metal loans (debt vs operating NWC offset) and customer advances (operating contract liabilities with disclosure), inventory analysed with gold-price context, and margin per non-gold rupee where disclosure allows; for Trent, rent-adjusted EBITDA alongside reported EBITDA, lease liabilities in net debt only for the post-116 view, and JV profit excluded from EBITDA. Both companies' common-size balance sheets should be shown on total assets and on operating assets (excluding treasury). Without these adjustments the comparison mostly measures accounting and business-model differences, not performance.

Source hierarchy for data

Source hierarchy: annual report notes > Reg 33 results > investor presentations > data vendors

Use the most authoritative source available and record it for every number. Audited annual report notes come first: they carry full disclosure (other income breakup, exceptional items, lease note, tax reconciliation, related parties, ageing schedules), restated comparatives and the auditor's report. Reg 33 quarterly results (limited-reviewed; audited for the full year) are next: timely and in a standard format, with segment data and exceptional items, but limited notes — use them for LTM and for quarters after the latest annual report. Investor presentations and earnings-call transcripts provide KPIs (same-store sales growth, store counts, grams sold, pre-116 EBITDA) but are unaudited and company-defined — use them for operational drivers and to cross-check, never as the primary source of financial line items. Data vendors (terminals, screener sites) are convenient for screening but frequently mix standalone and consolidated, miss restatements and apply their own classifications — use them only for sanity checks. When sources conflict, the annual report prevails; record conflicts in the log. For older years, prefer the latest annual report's comparative over the original report.
