Key concept

Restriction on Number of Layers of Subsidiaries

Concept: Companies (Restriction on Number of Layers) Rules 2017: subsidiary layers capped at two (with exceptions)

Statement

Under the proviso to Sec 2(87) of the Companies Act 2013 and the Companies (Restriction on Number of Layers) Rules, 2017 (effective 20 September 2017), a company (other than an exempt class) shall not have more than two layers of subsidiaries. For computing the number of layers, one layer consisting of one or more wholly owned subsidiaries is not taken into account (a proviso to Rule 2(1)) — only one such WOS layer is excluded, not every WOS in the chain.

Exempt classes include banking companies, NBFCs registered with RBI, insurance companies and government companies. The restriction does not prevent a company from acquiring a company incorporated outside India that has subsidiaries beyond two layers, as permitted under the laws of that country.

Explanation

The rule targets the use of long chains of shell companies to obscure ownership and divert funds — a concern highlighted after several corporate frauds. Related provisions:

  • Sec 186(1): investments through not more than two layers of investment companies.
  • Companies that had more layers on the commencement date were required to file a return (Form CRL-1) and were not permitted to add further layers until they complied; existing layers were not forced to collapse.
  • Form CRL-1 disclosures of layers made the structures visible to the Registrar.

Analytical relevance:

  • Group mapping: when reading AOC-1 and Ind AS 112 disclosures, layers beyond two in India suggest grandfathered structures, exempt entities or WOS chains; map them to understand where cash and debt sit.
  • Structural subordination deepens with each layer (s05): creditors of the top company are further from the operating assets.
  • Governance flags: complex structures with many step-down entities, especially with related-party transactions, warrant forensic attention (SEBI and NFRA scrutiny).
  • M&A and restructuring: groups restructure to comply, often through mergers of intermediate holding companies (Week 22).

Legislative note: the restriction applies to the companies covered, with the WOS carve-out read from the rules; confirm the precise counting method against the current text of the rules and any MCA clarifications before advising on a specific structure.

Illustration

Indian company A owns 100% of B; B owns 60% of C; C owns 70% of D; D owns 51% of E (all Indian, non-exempt).

Entity Relationship Counted as layer?
B WOS of A No (WOS excluded)
C 60% subsidiary of B Layer 1
D 70% subsidiary of C Layer 2
E 51% subsidiary of D Layer 3 — exceeds limit unless grandfathered or otherwise exempt

A would need to restructure (e.g. merge D into C or hold E directly) before creating E.

Connections

Links to s05 (structural subordination and parent-level debt), c04 (Sec 186 investment layers), s03 (AOC-1 listing of step-down subsidiaries) and the related item on Ind AS 103 business combinations in Week 22. Also relevant for CARO and statutory audit procedures on group structure.

Practice Questions

Q1. Count the layers [L1]

Company P owns 100% of Q and 80% of R. R owns 100% of S; S owns 65% of T; T owns 55% of U. All are Indian non-exempt companies incorporated after 2017. Count the layers on each chain and state whether any chain breaches the rules.

Q2. Foreign acquisition [L2]

An Indian listed company acquires 100% of a UK company that has three layers of subsidiaries in the US and Europe. Explain whether the layering rules are breached and what disclosures you would expect in the consolidated statements and AOC-1.

💡 Hint: Check the specific carve-out for companies incorporated outside India.

Q3. Governance review [L3]

A mid-cap company has 34 subsidiaries, several with nominal capital, arranged in four layers; three of them receive large ICDs from the parent and have no revenue. As an investor-side analyst, design a review covering company law compliance, Sec 186, related-party transactions and the financial statement adjustments you would make.
