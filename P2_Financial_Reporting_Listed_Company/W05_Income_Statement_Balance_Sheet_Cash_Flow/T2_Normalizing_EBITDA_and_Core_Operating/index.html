Roadmap topic: Normalizing EBITDA & Core Operating Profit: Stripping exceptional items, one-off asset sale gains, forex fluctuations, and non-operating other income

Overview

Reported profit is a compliance number: it applies Ind AS faithfully, but it mixes the recurring operating business with treasury income, asset disposals, restructuring, currency moves, government incentives with an expiry date, and accounting effects such as Ind AS 116. Normalization strips these out to reach core EBITDA and core PAT — the earnings a buyer, lender or investor can expect the business to generate on a sustainable basis. Every valuation multiple, DCF terminal year, covenant test, management incentive target and quality-of-earnings report rests on such a figure, and the person who builds it controls the answer.

For an FP&A or financial-reporting professional moving from audit, this is where accounting knowledge pays off: knowing what Ind AS 20, 21, 23, 102, 105, 116 and 19 do to the P&L is what allows you to spot an item, judge whether it recurs, and quantify its tax effect. The output is not a single number but a documented bridge — reported PBT to core EBITDA to core PAT — that a reviewer can audit line by line, and that reconciles to cash flow (Topic 4).

The learning sequence: begin with the definition problem — Ind AS does not define EBITDA, so you write and apply your own policy (s01). Then work through the categories of adjustment: the exceptional items line and the Labour Codes charge of FY2026 (s02); other income — asset-sale gains, FVTPL fund gains, interest and dividends (s03); forex, separating operating from financing and applying Ind AS 23.6(e) (s04); other operating revenue — scrap, RoDTEP and PLI (s05); the Ind AS 116 basis choice for lease-heavy peers (s06); and boundary items — discontinued operations, associates/JVs and ESOP cost (s07). The concepts formalise the core EBITDA formula (c01), pre-116 EBITDA (c02), core PAT (c03), the Ind AS 1 rules on extraordinary and material items (c04), the s.115BAA rate (c05) and the normalized ETR (c06).

Related concepts to study

Non-GAAP regimes: SEC, IFRS 18 MPMs and SEBI ICDR

SEC Regulation G / Item 10(e) non-GAAP rules and IFRS 18 MPM disclosure vs India's weaker regime (SEBI ICDR KPI certification in offer documents)

In the US, Regulation G requires any public non-GAAP measure to be accompanied by the most directly comparable GAAP measure and a quantitative reconciliation; Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K adds, for SEC filings, that the GAAP measure be presented with equal or greater prominence and prohibits labelling items as non-recurring if similar items are reasonably likely within two years or occurred within the prior two years. IFRS 18 (from 2027) brings management-defined performance measures into an audited note with reconciliation and tax and NCI effects of each adjustment. India has no equivalent for listed-company investor communications: "adjusted EBITDA" in investor presentations is unregulated beyond general LODR disclosure duties. SEBI ICDR (amended 2022) does require KPIs in IPO offer documents to be defined, approved by the audit committee and certified by the statutory auditor or a peer-reviewed chartered accountant, with continued disclosure post-listing. Analysts must therefore impose their own discipline.

Ind AS 103 bargain purchase gain to capital reserve

Ind AS 103 bargain purchase gain goes to capital reserve (Ind AS carve-out) vs P&L under IFRS 3

Under IFRS 3.34, a gain on bargain purchase (fair value of net identifiable assets exceeding consideration, after reassessment) is recognised in profit or loss. Ind AS 103 carves this out: the gain is recognised in OCI and accumulated in equity as capital reserve if there is clear evidence of the underlying reasons for a bargain purchase; otherwise it is recognised directly in equity as capital reserve. For normalization this means Indian P&Ls do not contain bargain purchase gains — but IFRS-reporting peers (and Indian groups' foreign subsidiaries reporting under IFRS) may, so remove them when comparing. Common-control combinations under Ind AS 103 Appendix C also take any difference to capital reserve. Acquisition-related costs are expensed under both — a normalization candidate.

Quality of earnings report structure

Quality of earnings (QoE) report structure in transaction diligence → Week 22: M&A

A QoE report, prepared by financial due-diligence advisers for buyers or lenders, is the professional version of this topic. Typical structure: reported EBITDA → management adjustments → diligence adjustments → adjusted (normalized) EBITDA, often on an LTM basis; with sections on revenue quality (customer concentration, pricing, cut-off), cost normalization (owner compensation, related-party pricing, one-offs), run-rate and pro forma adjustments (acquisitions, cost savings), net working capital peg (normal level vs closing) and debt-like items (Topic 3). Each adjustment carries evidence and a confidence rating. The adjustments register in s01 is a QoE schedule in miniature. Week 22 builds on this in M&A pricing, locked-box vs completion accounts, and SPA definitions.

Normalized EBITDA in multiples and DCF

Normalized EBITDA as base for EV/EBITDA multiples and DCF terminal year → Week 15–16

EV/EBITDA is only meaningful if the EBITDA is sustainable and defined consistently with EV: other income excluded when cash is added back, lease basis matched with lease liabilities, associates excluded when their value is added separately. In a DCF, the terminal-year EBITDA margin anchors most of the value; if it contains PLI that expires, treasury income, or recurring "one-offs" added back, terminal value is overstated. Weeks 15–16 build the DCF and relative valuation; the normalized EBITDA and normalized ETR from this topic are their inputs, and the EV bridge in Week 16 is where the double-count risks of s03 and s07 are resolved.

Common pitfalls

Recurring "one-offs" added back

Adding back "one-offs" that recur every year (restructuring, inventory write-downs) and inflating sustainable EBITDA

Management labels restructuring, store closures, inventory obsolescence or legal settlements as exceptional, and analysts follow the label. When the same type of item appears in most years, it is a normal cost of running the business. The mistake is driven by reliance on the exceptional items line and by management guidance that quotes EBITDA "before exceptional items". Correct treatment: apply a recurrence test (frequency over 5 years, nature, link to strategy) and keep recurring items in core EBITDA, or include a multi-year average. Detection: list all adjustments over five years by type; if a type appears three or more times, challenge it; compare the sum of add-backs with cumulative reported profits — persistent large add-backs are a red flag.

Other income removed but cash kept, or vice versa

Removing all other income for cash-rich companies but keeping the cash in valuation, or vice versa (double count)

The correct pairing is: exclude treasury income from EBITDA and add treasury assets in the EV-to-equity bridge. Errors arise when an analyst uses a broker's EBITDA (often including other income) with their own net cash, or removes all other income — including operating elements — and ignores the cash. Correct treatment: classify each other income item (s03), remove only non-operating items, and add exactly the assets that generate them (cash, bank deposits, liquid funds, investments) in the bridge; if an asset is treated as operating (e.g., a float), keep its income in EBITDA and exclude it from surplus cash. Detection: compute an implied treasury yield (treasury income ÷ treasury assets) and check that every asset with income removed appears in the bridge.

Missing tax effect on adjustments

Forgetting the tax effect when moving from adjusted EBITDA to adjusted PAT/EPS

An analyst adds back a ₹100 crore exceptional loss to PAT, forgetting that the reported tax charge already reflected a ₹25 crore tax saving. Core PAT is then overstated by ₹25 crore. The opposite error applies to gains. It happens because EBITDA adjustments are pre-tax and analysts copy them into the PAT bridge. Correct treatment: tax-effect each adjustment at its applicable rate — 25.168% for s.115BAA business items, capital-gains rates for asset sales, zero for non-deductible items (goodwill impairment, penalties) — and remove non-recurring tax items separately (c03). Detection: check that the normalized ETR (c06) is plausible relative to the statutory rate and the tax reconciliation.

Mixed lease bases across retailers

Comparing post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA of one retailer with rent-adjusted EBITDA of another

Peer tables built from different sources mix bases: one company's investor presentation reports pre-Ind AS 116 EBITDA, another's broker report uses post-116, a third (a US GAAP peer) has rent inside operating costs. The resulting margins and multiples are not comparable, and the lease-heavy company looks either much better or much worse. Correct treatment: rebuild every company on the same basis from Ind AS statements — post-116 EBITDA with lease liabilities in net debt, or pre-116 EBITDA (deducting lease payments) with lease liabilities excluded (s06, c02). Detection: compute lease payments ÷ revenue for each peer; if EBITDA margins differ by roughly that amount, the bases are probably mixed.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full normalization of a listed consumer company [L3]

An NSE-listed consumer company (s.115BAA) reports for FY2026 (₹ crore): revenue from operations 18,400 (including PLI ₹160 crore under a scheme ending FY2028, RoDTEP ₹35 crore and scrap ₹42 crore); other income 520 (interest on deposits 190, FVTPL gains 230, profit on sale of a warehouse 60, sub-letting rent 40); EBITDA as reported on the face of the P&L (excluding other income) 2,940 including ESOP cost of 75; D&A 680 (including ROU depreciation 330); finance costs 260 (including lease interest 190 and Ind AS 23.6(e) forex 15); other expenses include a forex loss of 28 on an ECB; exceptional items: Labour Codes past service cost (110), VRS (45) (similar VRS in FY2024 and FY2025), impairment of goodwill (90); share of profit of a JV 85 (below exceptional items, before tax); tax expense 600 including a reversal of earlier-year provisions of 30; profit from discontinued operations (net of tax) (70). Lease payments in the CFS: principal 310, interest 190.

(a) Compute PBT and reported PAT. (b) Compute core EBITDA (post-116) and pre-116 core EBITDA under a stated policy. (c) Compute core PAT with tax effects (the warehouse gain is taxed at 14.30%). (d) Compute reported and normalized ETR. (e) Present the bridge as a documented table.

💡 Hint: Build the P&L downwards first, then the bridge upwards from PBT; treat the recurring VRS consistently.

Q2. Retail peer comparison with leases and other income [L3]

Retailer P (Ind AS) reports EBITDA ₹3,200 crore (post-116), other income ₹410 crore (mostly treasury), lease payments ₹1,050 crore, revenue ₹21,000 crore, lease liabilities ₹6,800 crore, borrowings ₹500 crore, cash and liquid funds ₹4,200 crore. Retailer Q (US GAAP filer, converted to ₹) reports EBITDA ₹2,450 crore on revenue ₹19,000 crore with operating lease cost of ₹980 crore in operating expenses; net debt excluding leases ₹1,200 crore; operating lease liabilities ₹5,900 crore. A broker table shows P at 22% EBITDA margin (EBITDA including other income ÷ revenue) and Q at 12.9%.

(a) Identify every inconsistency in the broker table. (b) Restate both on a pre-116 basis and on a post-116 basis. (c) Compute net debt/EBITDA for each on matching bases. (d) If the sector trades at 28x pre-116 EBITDA, compute equity values and explain how P's cash enters.

Q3. Industrial exporter: forex, incentives and exceptional items [L2]

An exporter reports FY2025 (₹ crore): revenue 7,300 (including RoDTEP 90 and PLI 140, of which 55 relates to FY2024), EBITDA 1,120 (company definition, including other income 130 of which FVTPL gains 70, interest 40, export-receivable forex gain 20); exceptional items: litigation settlement (35), insurance claim received for a FY2023 fire 48; finance costs 170 including Ind AS 23.6(e) exchange difference 22; separately, other expenses contain a forex loss on ECB principal of 31.

(a) Compute core EBITDA under a policy that includes operating forex and current-year incentives. (b) Compute margins with and without PLI. (c) Explain the treatment of the insurance claim and the litigation settlement. (d) Write the documentation lines for each adjustment for an adjustments register.

Q4. Interview case: management's adjusted EBITDA [L3]

A newly listed company's earnings call presents "Adjusted EBITDA" of ₹1,480 crore versus Ind AS-derived mechanical EBITDA of ₹1,120 crore. The reconciliation adds back ESOP cost ₹160 crore, "one-time" marketing for brand launch ₹90 crore (similar campaigns planned annually), store pre-opening costs ₹70 crore, and includes other income of ₹40 crore that is already in mechanical EBITDA. Treasury assets are ₹2,600 crore from IPO proceeds.

(a) Critique each adjustment, citing Ind AS 1 and practice. (b) Compute your core EBITDA. (c) Explain how SEC Item 10(e), IFRS 18 MPM rules and SEBI ICDR would each have constrained this presentation. (d) Explain how your core EBITDA and the IPO cash enter an EV/EBITDA valuation.
