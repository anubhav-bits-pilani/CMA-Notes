Subtopic note

Operating vs Financing Forex and Ind AS 23 Para 6(e)

Scope: Forex: operating (trade receivables/payables) vs financing (borrowings) gains/losses; Ind AS 23 para 6(e) exchange differences treated as borrowing cost

Why it matters

Indian exporters, importers and companies with foreign currency borrowings (ECBs, FCNR loans, buyer's credit) report foreign exchange gains and losses that can swing PBT by several percentage points in a year of rupee movement — the rupee moved from about ₹75 to ₹83 per USD between FY2022 and FY2024 and weakened further past ₹85 during FY2025. Where these differences are presented varies: other income, other expenses, finance costs, or exceptional items. Some forex is genuinely operating (it is part of the economics of selling in dollars), some is a financing cost, and some is pure noise. Normalized EBITDA requires sorting them consistently.

Core notes

Recognition under Ind AS 21

Ind AS 21.23 requires monetary items (receivables, payables, borrowings, cash) in foreign currency to be translated at the closing rate; Ind AS 21.28 requires exchange differences on settlement or translation to be recognised in profit or loss (except for net investment in a foreign operation and qualifying hedges). The P&L line is not prescribed. Schedule III Div II requires the net gain/loss on foreign currency transactions and translation (other than considered as finance cost) to be disclosed in the notes.

Classification for analysis

Source of exchange difference Nature Core EBITDA treatment
Export receivables realised/translated Operating — part of realised revenue in rupees Include (it reflects the rupee value of sales)
Import payables for raw materials Operating — part of material cost Include
Forward contracts hedging trade exposures (not hedge-accounted) Operating hedge, FVTPL Include, paired with underlying
Foreign currency borrowings (ECB, buyer's credit for capex, FCNR) Financing Exclude from EBITDA; treat with finance cost
Intercompany loans to foreign subsidiaries Financing/investing Exclude
Translation of foreign operations (Ind AS 21.39) OCI (FCTR) Not in P&L; not in EBITDA
Derivatives hedging borrowings Financing Exclude

Why include operating forex? An exporter invoicing in USD with 60-day credit earns revenue at the transaction-date rate; the difference to the realisation rate is a real component of its rupee economics. Excluding operating forex gains in a weak-rupee year would understate sustainable EBITDA if the company prices in dollars. But analysts often prefer to show operating forex separately because it is volatile — the core view can include a normalized (average) level.

Ind AS 23 para 6(e)

Ind AS 23.6(e) includes in borrowing costs "exchange differences arising from foreign currency borrowings to the extent that they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs". The rationale: a company that borrows in USD at 6% instead of in INR at 9% saves interest, but expects the rupee to depreciate. The portion of the exchange loss up to the interest saved is economically interest.

The ICAI and the Ind AS Transition Facilitation Group guidance indicate a method: the exchange loss treated as borrowing cost is limited to the difference between the interest cost on an equivalent rupee borrowing and the actual foreign currency interest cost. The remaining exchange difference is an ordinary Ind AS 21 forex item.

Consequences:

  • The 6(e) portion is presented within finance costs (Schedule III Div II note on finance costs includes "exchange differences regarded as an adjustment to borrowing costs").
  • If the borrowing funds a qualifying asset, the 6(e) portion may be capitalised into CWIP/PPE (Ind AS 23.8).
  • The excess over the 6(e) limit goes to other expenses/other income.

Illustration (₹ crore)

A company has a USD 100 million ECB at 6.5% (₹830 crore at the opening rate of ₹83). Equivalent INR borrowing rate is 9.0%. The rupee moves to ₹85.50 at year-end (average ₹84.20).

  • Interest in USD: 6.5% × USD 100m = USD 6.5m × ₹84.20 = ₹54.7 crore.
  • Equivalent INR interest: 9.0% × ₹830 crore = ₹74.7 crore.
  • 6(e) cap = ₹74.7 − ₹54.7 = ₹20.0 crore.
  • Total exchange loss on principal: USD 100m × (85.50 − 83.00) = ₹25.0 crore.
  • Presented in finance costs: ₹20.0 crore. Presented in other expenses (forex loss): ₹5.0 crore.

For core EBITDA, the ₹5.0 crore in other expenses is financing in nature and is added back. The ₹20.0 crore in finance costs is already outside EBITDA. Both belong in the analysis of total cost of debt: all-in cost = (54.7 + 25.0) ÷ 830 = 9.6%.

Locating forex in the statements

  • Other income / other expenses notes: "net gain/loss on foreign currency transactions and translation".
  • Finance costs note: exchange differences regarded as borrowing cost.
  • Ind AS 107 market risk note: unhedged foreign currency exposure by currency, sensitivity to a 1–5% move — use this to judge how much of the P&L forex relates to trade vs borrowings.
  • Cash flow statement: "effect of exchange rate changes on cash and cash equivalents" (Ind AS 7.28) and unrealised forex in the indirect-method adjustments.
  • Hedge reserve (OCI) for cash flow hedges; reclassification to P&L when the hedged item affects P&L.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mixed presentation: many companies report a single net forex line combining trade and borrowing differences. Allocate using exposure disclosures (Ind AS 107) or the ratio of trade to borrowing exposures, and disclose the assumption.
  • Hedge accounting: if export receivables are cash-flow hedged, the effective portion of hedge gains is recycled to revenue — the forex effect is already in revenue. Don't double-adjust.
  • Unrealised vs realised: core EBITDA should not depend on whether a receivable is collected before or after 31 March; use the total.
  • Volatile years: in a year with a 10% rupee move, consider normalizing operating forex to a multi-year average and showing the deviation as a separate line.
  • Para 6(e) on capitalised assets: forex capitalised into CWIP raises future depreciation — inspect the borrowing cost capitalisation note.
  • Legacy para 46A (Indian GAAP AS 11): companies that elected under Ind AS 101 to continue the AS 11 para 46A treatment for long-term foreign currency monetary items existing at transition (capitalising or amortising through FCMITDA) show forex in D&A or an amortisation line — these are financing in nature.

Key takeaways

  • Ind AS 21 forex on monetary items goes to P&L, but the line is not prescribed; locate it in the notes.
  • Operating forex (trade receivables/payables and their hedges) is part of rupee economics — include in EBITDA, ideally normalized.
  • Financing forex (borrowings, intercompany loans, borrowing hedges) is excluded from EBITDA.
  • Ind AS 23.6(e) treats exchange losses on foreign borrowings, capped at the interest saved versus an INR loan, as borrowing cost in finance costs; the excess is ordinary forex.
  • Use Ind AS 107 exposure disclosures to split a single net forex figure.

Practice Questions

Q1. Split the forex line [L1]

An exporter's other expenses include a net forex loss of ₹38 crore. The Ind AS 107 note shows unhedged exposures at year-end: trade receivables USD 60m, trade payables USD 15m, ECB USD 80m. During the year the rupee moved from ₹83.0 to ₹85.5. Estimate the operating and financing components of the forex line and state the core EBITDA adjustment.

Q2. Ind AS 23.6(e) computation [L2]

A company holds a EUR 40 million loan at 3.0% (opening rate ₹90.0, closing ₹95.0, average ₹92.4). A comparable INR loan would cost 9.25%. Compute the exchange difference regarded as borrowing cost, the residual forex loss, and their presentation in the P&L. If the loan funds a plant under construction for the full year, explain which amounts may be capitalised.

💡 Hint: Compute the cap as INR-equivalent interest less actual foreign currency interest, both in rupees.

Q3. Hedge accounting interaction [L2]

An IT services company designates forward contracts as cash flow hedges of forecast USD revenue. In FY2025 ₹120 crore of hedge gains were reclassified from the cash flow hedge reserve to revenue, and a further ₹15 crore of ineffective hedge loss sits in other expenses. Explain how each affects core EBITDA and why a peer without hedge accounting may show forex in other income instead.

Q4. All-in cost of debt [L3]

Using the following, compute the all-in rupee cost of a USD 50 million buyer's credit for FY2025 and compare it with a 9.0% INR loan: interest 5.8% p.a.; hedging premium paid on a forward cover of 2.1% p.a. (expensed in finance costs); opening rate ₹83.2, closing ₹85.4, average ₹84.3. Explain how your answer should influence the company's financing decision and how the P&L presentation might mislead an analyst who looks only at finance costs.
