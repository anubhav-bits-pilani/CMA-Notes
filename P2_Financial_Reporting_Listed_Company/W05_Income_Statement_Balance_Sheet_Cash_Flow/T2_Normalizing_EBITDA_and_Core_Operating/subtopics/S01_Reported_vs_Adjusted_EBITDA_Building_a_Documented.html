Subtopic note

Reported vs Adjusted EBITDA: Building a Documented Definition

Scope: Reported vs adjusted EBITDA: Ind AS does not define EBITDA; build a policy-consistent definition and document every adjustment

Why it matters

EBITDA is the most quoted performance number in Indian equity research, credit appraisal, M&A term sheets and covenant packages — yet no Ind AS defines it. Companies that present it (on the face of the P&L under Ind AS 1.85 or in investor presentations) choose their own definition: with or without other income, before or after exceptional items, post-Ind AS 116 or rent-adjusted, before or after ESOP cost. Two companies' "EBITDA" can differ by several percentage points of revenue with identical economics. An analyst who takes reported EBITDA at face value inherits those choices; one who builds a written, policy-consistent definition and applies it to every company and every year produces numbers that can drive valuation multiples, covenant headroom and FP&A targets. Lenders, private equity diligence teams and rating agencies all work this way.

Core notes

Why there is no defined EBITDA

Ind AS (like IFRS until IFRS 18) prescribes line items and permits additional subtotals (Ind AS 1.85–85B) but does not define operating profit or EBITDA. Schedule III Div II does not require EBITDA; the ICAI Guidance Note allows a company to present it as an additional line item provided the basis is disclosed and applied consistently. So "reported EBITDA" means whatever the company chose, and the starting point for analysis must be the Ind AS line items themselves.

Three layers of EBITDA

Layer Definition Typical use
Mechanical EBITDA PBT + finance costs + D&A (all Ind AS amounts as reported) Starting point; includes other income and exceptional items
Reported EBITDA Company's own subtotal or investor-presentation figure Read the footnote/definition; reconcile to mechanical
Adjusted (core/normalized) EBITDA Analyst's definition applied consistently: excludes other income, exceptional and non-recurring items; lease basis chosen explicitly Valuation multiples, peer comparison, forecasting base

The analyst's core definition is formalised in c01:

\[ \text{Core EBITDA} = \text{PBT} + \text{Finance cost} + \text{D\&A} - \text{Other income} \pm \text{Exceptional items} \pm \text{Non-recurring items in operating lines} \]

Writing the definition policy

Write a one-page EBITDA policy before touching the numbers. It should state, for every recurring judgement:

  1. Other income: excluded entirely, or split into operating (e.g., scrap sold if classified there, rental from sub-leasing stores) and treasury (interest, FVTPL gains, dividends, profit on sale of investments). Treasury always excluded.
  2. Exceptional items: excluded (added back if loss, deducted if gain), subject to a recurrence test (see s02).
  3. Non-recurring items within operating lines: inventory write-downs from a specific event, one-off legal costs, reversals of provisions — adjusted only with evidence from notes.
  4. Lease basis: post-Ind AS 116 (lease cost in D&A and interest) or pre-Ind AS 116 (lease payments deducted). Must match the net-debt definition (lease liabilities included or not).
  5. Forex: operating forex (trade receivables/payables) included; financing forex excluded (s04).
  6. Other operating revenue: recurring vs not (export incentives, PLI, scrap) — s05.
  7. ESOP cost (Ind AS 102): kept as an expense (recommended — it is a real economic cost) or added back (some companies do); be consistent.
  8. Share of associates/JVs: always excluded from EBITDA (below EBIT).
  9. Discontinued operations: excluded (already a single post-tax line).
  10. Basis: standalone or consolidated, and whether figures are owners' share or 100%.

Documenting every adjustment

Every adjustment needs a line in an adjustments register: year, amount, P&L line affected, direction, nature, source (note number and page), recurrence assessment and tax effect (for the PAT bridge, s03 of this topic and c03). The bridge from reported to adjusted EBITDA is then a transparent table.

The company's ₹2,150 crore exceeds the analyst's core figure by ₹250 crore post-Ind AS 116 (₹560 crore on the pre-Ind AS 116 basis) — that gap is the valuation risk of taking the headline number.

Excel/Power BI implementation

  • Keep an Adjustments table with columns: Company, Year, Category (OtherIncome, Exceptional, NonRecurring, Lease, Forex, ESOP), Amount, Sign, Note, TaxDeductible flag.
  • Core EBITDA = Mechanical EBITDA + SUMIFS(Adj[Amount],Adj[Company],c,Adj[Year],y,Adj[InEBITDA],TRUE).
  • In Power BI, a measure Core EBITDA := [Mechanical EBITDA] + CALCULATE(SUM(Adj[Amount]), Adj[InEBITDA] = TRUE()) allows slicing by adjustment category.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Company definition changes: if a company changes its EBITDA definition (e.g., starts including other income), the analyst's definition is unaffected — another reason to rebuild from line items.
  • "Adjusted EBITDA" in M&A and covenants: loan agreements define EBITDA contractually (often adding back non-cash items, pro forma acquisitions, synergies). Covenant EBITDA can be far above core EBITDA; never use it for valuation.
  • Capitalised costs: aggressive capitalisation of development costs (Ind AS 38) or borrowing costs raises EBITDA. Peer comparison may require expensing capitalised R&D.
  • Grants netted against expenses: Ind AS 20 allows grants related to income to be deducted from the related expense; a peer presenting them as other operating revenue has the same EBITDA but different revenue and margins.
  • Materiality: adjust only for items that are material and evidenced; dozens of small "adjustments" invite bias.
  • Asymmetry bias: management tends to call losses one-off and gains recurring. Apply the same test to gains (profit on sale, provision reversals) as to losses.

Key takeaways

  • Ind AS does not define EBITDA; reported figures embed company-specific choices.
  • Start from mechanical EBITDA (PBT + finance costs + D&A) and move to core EBITDA through documented adjustments.
  • Write a definition policy covering other income, exceptional items, leases, forex, incentives, ESOPs, associates and basis, and apply it to all companies and years.
  • Maintain an adjustments register with source, recurrence and tax effect for each item.
  • Lease basis of EBITDA must match the treatment of lease liabilities in net debt.

Practice Questions

Q1. Mechanical to core bridge [L1]

From the following FY2025 data (₹ crore), compute mechanical EBITDA and core EBITDA: PBT 860; finance costs 140; D&A 310; other income 190 (interest on FDs 90, FVTPL gain on liquid funds 70, rental from sub-let space 30); exceptional item: impairment of a brand ₹75 crore (loss); other expenses include ₹22 crore of IPO-related legal costs of a subsidiary that was later withdrawn.

Q2. Write an EBITDA policy [L2]

Draft a ten-point EBITDA definition policy for a sector coverage file on Indian quick-service restaurant chains, specifying treatment of other income, Ind AS 116, store pre-opening costs, ESOPs, franchise fee income and exceptional items, with a one-line rationale for each.

💡 Hint: Think about what drives comparability across chains with different lease and franchise models.

Q3. Reconcile a company's reported EBITDA [L2]

A company's investor presentation reports EBITDA of ₹1,240 crore, "excluding ESOP cost and exceptional items, including other income". The P&L shows PBT ₹780 crore, finance costs ₹95 crore, D&A ₹210 crore, other income ₹120 crore, exceptional loss ₹40 crore, ESOP cost in employee expense ₹55 crore. Reconcile the reported figure to mechanical EBITDA and to your core EBITDA (ESOP kept as an expense).

Q4. Covenant vs valuation EBITDA [L3]

A term loan agreement defines "Consolidated EBITDA" as including pro forma EBITDA of acquisitions for the full year, run-rate cost synergies up to 15% of EBITDA, and all non-cash charges. Explain how this differs from core EBITDA, why lenders accept it, and why you would not use it in an EV/EBITDA valuation.

Q5. Power BI adjustment slicer [L3]

Design the data model (tables, relationships) and DAX measures for a Power BI page showing reported, mechanical and core EBITDA for five companies over five years, with a slicer to switch individual adjustment categories on and off and a table listing each adjustment with its note reference.
