Roadmap topic: Segment Reporting Analysis (Ind AS 108): Evaluating revenue growth, EBIT margins, and ROCE across business verticals and geographies

Overview

Consolidated statements tell you how the group performed; segment disclosures tell you which parts performed. Ind AS 108 Operating Segments requires listed and other covered entities to report financial information for each reportable segment as the chief operating decision maker (CODM) sees it — revenue, result, assets, liabilities, capex, D&A — and to reconcile it to the entity's statements. It also requires entity-wide disclosures of revenue by product and geography and reliance on major customers. SEBI's Reg 33 results bring a condensed version of the segment table to the market every quarter, including segment assets and liabilities at each quarter-end.

For equity analysts, segment data drives SOTP valuation, mix analysis and the identification of value-creating and value-destroying businesses. For credit analysts, it reveals concentration and the stability of cash-generating segments. For FP&A and management reporting roles, Ind AS 108's "management approach" means the external segment note is a public window into the company's internal MIS — the same structure you will build in Week 20. For this week's exercise, Titan's segments (Jewellery, Watches & Wearables, Eyecare, Others) offer a ready dataset to measure growth, margins and ROCE by vertical and to see how one segment dominates group economics.

The learning sequence runs from identification to analysis. First understand how operating segments are identified through the CODM's reporting, aggregated and tested against quantitative thresholds (s01, c01, c02). Then learn what is disclosed for each segment and how it is measured (s02, c05), how segment totals reconcile to entity totals and what sits in unallocable items (s03, c04), and what entity-wide disclosures add (s04, c03). With clean data, compute segment ROCE, growth and margin by vertical (s05), and extend the analysis to quarterly Reg 33 data while checking consistency with annual figures (s06).

Related concepts to study

IFRS 8 and US GAAP ASU 2023-07

IFRS 8 is substantively the same; US GAAP ASU 2023-07 now requires significant segment expense disclosure

Ind AS 108 is substantively identical to IFRS 8, so segment data of Indian and IFRS reporters is broadly comparable in structure, although both depend on each CODM's measures. US GAAP ASC 280 follows the same management approach, but ASU 2023-07 (effective for public entities for fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2023) requires disclosure of significant segment expenses regularly provided to the CODM, other segment items, the CODM's title and position, and how the CODM uses the measures; it also extends annual disclosures to interim periods and allows more than one measure of profit. As a result, US filers now reveal cost structures (cost of sales, SG&A, R&D by segment) that Indian and IFRS reporters generally do not. When comparing a US peer's segment with an Indian one, expect richer cost detail on the US side; for Indian companies, look for cost detail in investor presentations instead.

Segment drivers in the operating model and SOTP

Segment-level revenue drivers feed the operating model → Week 13–14; SOTP → Week 16

In Weeks 13–14 the operating model's revenue build is often segment-based: jewellery revenue = stores × revenue per store (or gold volume × realisation), watches = units × average selling price, each with its own margin and capital intensity. Historical segment data from s02 and s05 calibrates these drivers, and the reconciliation (c04) ensures the sum of segments ties to consolidated revenue and EBIT, with unallocable costs modelled separately. In Week 16, SOTP valuation applies different multiples or DCFs to each segment — a high-ROCE jewellery business at a premium multiple, a loss-making eyecare business at a revenue multiple or option value — and deducts capitalised corporate costs and net debt. Errors in segment capital employed or cost allocation flow directly into valuation.

Titan segments as a practice dataset

Titan segments (Jewellery, Watches & Wearables, Eyecare, Others) as a practice dataset

Titan's consolidated segment note and Reg 33 tables provide multi-year data for Jewellery (dominant in revenue and result), Watches & Wearables, Eyecare and Others (which has included businesses such as fragrances, women's bags and ethnic wear at various times), plus geographical information split between India and outside India. Practice tasks: build a five-year segment table in ₹ crore; compute external revenue growth, result margin, capital employed and ROCE by segment; check the reconciliation to consolidated PBT and total assets; identify restated years where businesses moved between segments; and assess how gold metal loans and customer advances in segment liabilities affect jewellery ROCE. Verify segment definitions in each year's annual report rather than assuming continuity.

Segment profitability and management reporting

Segment profitability linkage to management reporting/MIS → Week 20

Because Ind AS 108 uses the management approach, the external segment note should be consistent with internal MIS: the same segments, profit measure and allocation conventions the CODM uses. In Week 20 you will design MIS packs — segment P&Ls, contribution margins, cost allocation methods (activity-based, revenue-based), transfer pricing for inter-segment transactions and segment balance sheets for ROCE. Design choices made internally (which costs are allocated, which assets are held centrally) directly determine what appears externally. FP&A professionals must ensure that changes to internal reporting are assessed for their Ind AS 108 consequences, including restated comparatives and disclosure of measurement changes.

Common pitfalls

Comparing segment EBIT across different allocation policies

Comparing segment EBIT across companies with different corporate cost allocation policies

Mistake: ranking segments of different companies by reported segment EBIT margin when one company allocates brand, IT and head-office costs to segments and another keeps them unallocable. Why it happens: segment tables look standardised, especially in the Reg 33 format, and the basis note is rarely read. Correct treatment: read each basis note (c05); restate to a common basis — either all costs allocated (fully loaded) using a disclosed or reasonable key, or all corporate costs excluded with unallocable costs compared separately as a % of revenue. Also align Ind AS 116 and exceptional item treatment. Detection: compare unallocable expenditure as a % of revenue across peers — large differences signal non-comparable segment results.

Missing segment re-definitions in trend series

Missing segment re-definitions, which break 3–5 year trend series unless prior years are restated

Mistake: stacking segment data from successive annual reports without noticing that a business moved between segments, a new segment was created, or the profit measure changed — producing spurious growth or margin jumps. Why it happens: data is pulled year by year and the restated comparatives in later reports are ignored. Correct treatment: always take the latest restated comparatives (Ind AS 108.29–30), rebuild earlier years on the new basis where possible using management disclosures, and flag breaks where restatement is impracticable. Detection: compare the prior-year column in the current report with the current-year column in the previous report; any difference indicates a re-definition or restatement.

Treating unallocable assets as segment capital

Treating unallocable assets (cash, investments) as part of segment capital employed

Mistake: spreading total capital employed (including cash, treasury investments and tax assets) across segments, or computing segment ROCE on total assets, which depresses ROCE for every segment and makes cash-rich companies look weaker. Why it happens: analysts start from consolidated capital employed and allocate it by revenue. Correct treatment: use segment assets minus segment liabilities as disclosed; separately classify unallocable assets into operating corporate assets (which can be allocated) and financial assets (excluded and valued separately), consistent with t3. Detection: the sum of segment capital employed should be well below consolidated capital employed for companies with large treasury balances; if it equals total capital employed, cash has been allocated.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated case: segment analysis for a diversified group [L3]

A listed group reports three segments for FY2024 and FY2025 (₹ crore). Consumer: revenue 6,800 → 7,700 (all external); result 980 → 1,050; assets 4,200 → 4,700; liabilities 1,500 → 1,650; capex 260; D&A 180. Chemicals: revenue 3,900 → 4,300 (including inter-segment 500 → 600); result 520 → 470; assets 5,100 → 6,000; liabilities 900 → 1,000; capex 950; D&A 260. Others: revenue 600 → 700; result (40) → (60); assets 400 → 450; liabilities 100 → 110. Unallocable expenses 220 → 290; unallocable income 180 → 210 (of which treasury 150 → 170); finance cost 140 → 160; unallocable assets 3,000 → 3,400 (cash and investments 2,500 → 2,800). (a) Reconcile segment results to PBT for FY2025. (b) Compute external revenue growth, result margin, average-capital ROCE and capex/D&A by segment. (c) Compute fully loaded margins allocating unallocable expenses by external revenue. (d) Recommend where capital should be allocated, with reasons.

Q2. Integrated case: identification and disclosure [L2]

An Indian company has six operating segments (₹ crore): A revenue 9,000 (external 8,400), profit 1,100, assets 7,000; B 1,000 (external 1,000), loss (420), assets 600; C 700, profit 60, assets 1,200; D 650, profit 50, assets 400; E 500, profit 30, assets 300; F 450, loss (10), assets 200 (all external unless stated). One customer accounts for ₹1,450 crore of A's revenue. (a) Apply the 10% tests. (b) Test the 75% coverage requirement. (c) Draft the major-customer disclosure. (d) Explain what an analyst gains from each disclosure.

💡 Hint: Use external revenue only for the 75% test and the entity's total revenue for the major-customer test.

Q3. Integrated case: Titan segment trend [L3]

Using five years of Titan Company's consolidated annual reports and Reg 33 filings: (a) build a segment table on a consistent basis, documenting restatements; (b) compute growth, margin and ROCE by segment, with jewellery ROCE both including and excluding gold metal loans and customer scheme advances in segment liabilities; (c) check that quarterly segment revenue sums to the annual figure and to the audited note; (d) write a 200-word assessment of whether non-jewellery segments create value.

Q4. Integrated case: peer comparability [L2]

Two Indian apparel retailers report a single fashion segment. Retailer A's segment result is EBITDA pre-Ind AS 116, before corporate costs: ₹1,250 crore on revenue ₹9,000 crore; owned-asset D&A ₹220 crore; rent ₹640 crore; ROU depreciation would be ₹500 crore; corporate costs ₹180 crore. Retailer B's segment result is EBIT post-Ind AS 116 after corporate costs: ₹760 crore on revenue ₹8,500 crore; segment capital employed ₹4,100 crore excluding lease liabilities of ₹2,600 crore. A's segment capital employed is ₹3,600 crore (no ROU assets). Restate A to B's basis and compare margins and ROCE on both a leases-as-debt and rent-adjusted basis, stating your assumptions about A's ROU assets and lease liabilities.
