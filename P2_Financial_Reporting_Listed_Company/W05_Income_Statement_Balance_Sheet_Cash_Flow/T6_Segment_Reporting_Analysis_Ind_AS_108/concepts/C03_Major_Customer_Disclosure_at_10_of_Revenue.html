Key concept

Major Customer Disclosure at 10% of Revenue

Concept: Ind AS 108 para 34: disclose reliance on any single customer contributing 10% or more of revenue

Statement

If revenues from transactions with a single external customer amount to 10% or more of an entity's revenues, the entity discloses (Ind AS 108.34):

  • that fact;
  • the total amount of revenues from each such customer; and
  • the identity of the segment or segments reporting the revenues.

\[ \text{Disclose if } \frac{\text{Revenue from customer } k}{\text{Entity revenue}} \geq 10\% \]

The entity need not disclose the customer's identity or the amount of revenue each segment reports from that customer.

Explanation

The rule addresses concentration risk: dependence on one buyer affects bargaining power, margin stability, credit risk and the probability of sharp revenue declines on contract loss. Rules for defining "single customer":

  • A group of entities known to be under common control is a single customer.
  • A government (national, state, local or foreign) and entities known to be under its control: judgement is applied, considering the extent of economic integration between those entities, to decide whether they are a single customer (Ind AS 108.34).

The disclosure applies to every entity in scope, including single-segment entities (entity-wide, s04).

Indian context: concentration is common among auto component suppliers (one or two OEMs), contract manufacturers (CDMO/EMS — one global brand owner), defence and railway suppliers (Government of India entities), IT services mid-caps (top client), and telecom infrastructure (a few telecom operators). Lenders and rating agencies apply explicit concentration haircuts.

Analytical use beyond the disclosure:

  • Combine with receivables ageing (Schedule III), ECL notes and related-party disclosures (customer may be a group entity).
  • Check contract terms (take-or-pay, cost pass-through) in the annual report or offer documents (DRHP risk factors usually name top-customer shares).
  • Model revenue by customer cohort if the concentration is high.

Illustration

An EMS company: revenue ₹9,000 crore. Customers: Global Brand X (three subsidiaries: ₹700, ₹450, ₹200 crore); Indian telecom operator ₹1,050 crore; State discoms (three state-owned utilities, each ₹400 crore).

Customer Revenue % Disclose?
Brand X group (common control) 1,350 15.0% Yes
Telecom operator 1,050 11.7% Yes
Each state discom 400 4.4% Only if treated as one customer (₹1,200 crore, 13.3%) — judgement on economic integration

If state-owned discoms of different states are not economically integrated, they are separate customers and not disclosed individually.

Connections

Part of the entity-wide disclosures in s04; relevant to segment risk assessment in s05 (ROCE and growth by vertical depend on a few customers) and to the credit side of t5 (concentrated revenue in a subsidiary affects upstreaming). Connects to revenue modelling in Weeks 13–14 and to risk-factor review in M&A diligence (Week 22).

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply the rule [L1]

Revenue ₹4,200 crore. Customer A ₹390 crore; Customer B ₹460 crore; Customers C1 and C2 (same parent) ₹250 crore and ₹220 crore. Identify which disclosures are required.

Q2. Government customers [L2]

A defence electronics company sells ₹2,600 crore to the Ministry of Defence, ₹900 crore to a defence PSU and ₹400 crore to Indian Railways, out of total revenue ₹5,000 crore. Discuss how the economic-integration judgement affects the disclosure.

💡 Hint: Consider whether these government entities operate under common direction and share economic risks.

Q3. Concentration risk analysis [L3]

An auto component supplier discloses that one customer contributes 42% of revenue (₹3,100 crore) through its four-wheeler segment. Outline how you would stress-test the company's EBITDA and FCF for a 20% volume decline at that customer, using segment margins and operating leverage assumptions.
