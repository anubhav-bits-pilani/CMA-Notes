SCQA Opening for the Executive Summary

Scope: SCQA opening (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer) for the executive summary

Why it matters

The pyramid (s01) tells you how to structure the argument. SCQA tells you how to open it so the reader immediately sees why the answer matters. Minto's introduction framework sets up a question the reader already cares about and answers it at once. It is the first paragraph of equity research initiations ("Sector re-rating is over; stock-picking now matters…"), consulting decks, credit notes and board papers. In Project 2 the SCQA paragraph is the first 60–100 words of the executive summary and the first 60 seconds of the Saturday presentation. If it fails, the rest is not read.

Core notes

The four elements

Element Purpose Test Project 2 example (jewellery)
Situation A stable, uncontroversial fact the reader already accepts Would the reader nod? "India's organised jewellery market is growing at 15–20% a year as hallmarking and trust shift share from family jewellers to national chains."
Complication What changed or creates tension in the situation Does it create a problem or a choice? "But record gold prices, the July 2024 customs-duty cut and rapid franchise-led expansion have made reported margins and returns hard to compare across chains."
Question The question the complication raises in the reader's mind (often implicit) Is it the question the reader actually has? "Which listed chain converts this growth into value most effectively?"
Answer The governing thought, which is the top of the pyramid Is it specific, quantified and falsifiable? "Titan still earns the highest returns (ROIC 24% vs WACC 12.5%), but Kalyan has improved fastest, so it offers the stronger improvement case. Senco's growth is not yet earning its cost of capital."

The answer is the governing thought from s01. The pillars follow immediately after the SCQA paragraph.

Why SCQA works

  • Situation establishes common ground and context in one sentence. It must not be news. If the reader disagrees with it, you have started an argument before giving your answer.
  • Complication creates the need to read on. In financial analysis it is usually a change (cost shock, regulation, competitive move, accounting change) or a puzzle (a peer growing fastest but with falling ROIC).
  • Question focuses the reader on the one issue the document resolves. It is often left implicit in writing but should be explicit in your planning.
  • Answer resolves the tension at once, rather than after ten pages of data.

Variants and ordering

Minto described alternative orderings for different audiences:

Order Effect Use when
S–C–Q–A (standard) Builds context, then answers The reader needs context (external audience, interviews)
A–S–C (answer first) Most direct A busy CFO or board who knows the context. This is often the preferred order for an executive summary
C–S–A (concern first) Urgency Risk or forensic findings drive the message ("A ₹600 crore inventory build is not explained by store additions…")

The Answer must appear within the first paragraph in every variant.

Writing rules for each element

  • Situation: one sentence, a fact or widely accepted trend, quantified if possible. Avoid history lessons.
  • Complication: one or two sentences, specific, including what makes comparison hard (accounting changes, business-model differences). For IT peers: "Demand has slowed to low single-digit CC growth, discretionary spending is weak and GenAI is deflating some service lines…"
  • Question: match the client. Investor: "Which is the better risk-adjusted investment?" Lender: "Which can best absorb a downturn?" CFO benchmarking: "Where is our company's capital efficiency gap and what drives it?"
  • Answer: governing thought, quantified, with the preview of three pillars.

Illustration: IT peer set

Situation: India's top three IT services firms together earn over ₹5 lakh crore of revenue, mostly from North America and Europe. Complication: Post-pandemic demand has normalised to low single-digit constant-currency growth, pricing pressure from GenAI-led productivity is rising, and the three firms differ sharply in their cost and capital discipline. Question (implicit): Which franchise is best positioned to compound value through a slower cycle? Answer: Peer A remains the highest-quality compounder, with a 43 pp ROIC–WACC spread and the most stable margins (CV 0.02). Peer B offers faster growth at lower returns, and Peer C needs a margin turnaround before its growth creates value.

(83 words. Then the three pillars follow.)

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Complication that is not a complication. "The companies reported results for FY25" creates no tension. Find the real issue: a divergence, change or puzzle.
  • Situation the reader disputes. "IT demand is strong" in a weak year loses credibility immediately. Situation statements must be checkable facts.
  • Multiple complications. Choose the one that leads to the question you answer. Others belong in pillars or risks.
  • Forensic-led openings. If Week 7 found a material red flag (for example related-party sales of 20% of revenue at one peer), the complication may be that flag, and the answer must address it.
  • Interview setting. In a case interview, SCQA lets you open confidently: restate the situation and complication from the case, state the question, and give your hypothesis answer, which you then test.

Key takeaways

  • SCQA is the opening of the pyramid: Situation (agreed fact), Complication (change or tension), Question (reader's question), Answer (governing thought).
  • The answer must appear in the first paragraph. For busy executives, lead with it (A–S–C).
  • The complication in financial analysis is usually a change, a divergence between peers, or a comparability problem.
  • Tailor the question to the reader (investor, lender, CFO), since the same data supports different questions.
  • Keep the SCQA paragraph to about 60–100 words, quantified, with the pillars immediately after.

Practice Questions

Q1. Label the elements [L1]

Label each sentence S, C, Q or A, and identify what is missing: "Senco has grown revenue at 26% a year since FY22. Its borrowings including gold metal loans have risen from ₹1,400 crore to ₹2,200 crore and ROIC has fallen below its 13% cost of capital. Senco is a leading jeweller in eastern India."

Q2. Write SCQA for three audiences [L2]

Using this data, write three SCQA openings of at most 90 words each for (a) an equity investor, (b) a bank considering a ₹500 crore working-capital line to Kalyan and (c) Kalyan's CFO benchmarking against Titan. Titan ROIC 24%, Kalyan 14%, Senco 11%; WACC 12–13%; Kalyan net debt/EBITDA fell from 2.4x to 1.5x; Kalyan FOCO stores rose from 30% to 55% of the network; CCC Titan 150 days, Kalyan 205 days, Senco 195 days.

💡 Hint: Keep the data constant and change the question. The answer and its emphasis follow from the question.

Q3. Fix a weak complication [L2]

A draft reads: "Situation: Indian IT companies are large exporters. Complication: They published FY25 results in April 2025. Answer: TCS is the best." Rewrite it using IT peer data of your choice (CAGR, margin, ROIC, CFO/EBITDA), making the complication genuine and the answer quantified.

Q4. Answer-first rewrite [L2]

Convert your answer to Q2(a) into A–S–C order. Explain in two sentences when you would choose this order in the Project 2 PDF report versus the recorded presentation.

Q5. Forensic-led opening [L3]

Your Week 7 forensic review found that one IT peer's unbilled revenue rose from 14% to 22% of revenue in two years while billed DSO was flat. Write a C–S–A opening for a peer report where this finding changes the ranking, and explain how the rest of the pyramid must change.
