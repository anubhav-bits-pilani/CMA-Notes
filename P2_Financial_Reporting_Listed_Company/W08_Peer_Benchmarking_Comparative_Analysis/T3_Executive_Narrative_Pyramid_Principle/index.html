Roadmap topic: Executive Narrative Writing (Pyramid Principle): Leading with the analytical conclusion, backed by 3 quantitative pillars and key downside risks

Overview

Topics t1 and t2 produce a comparable peer set and a ranked, stress-tested capital efficiency matrix. This topic turns that analysis into a decision-ready argument. The governing idea, from Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle, is that readers absorb ideas best when they get the conclusion first and then the grouped reasons that support it, with detail available underneath. For a comparative financial analysis report, that means opening with one quantified conclusion ("Titan remains the most capital-efficient jeweller; Kalyan is closing the gap fastest"). Then come three MECE, quantified pillars. Then the downside risks and what would change the view. Finally an appendix that makes every number verifiable.

This skill separates an analyst from a spreadsheet operator in the roles this curriculum targets. Equity research associates write initiation summaries and quarterly result notes in exactly this structure. Credit analysts write rating rationales with key rating drivers and sensitivities. FP&A analysts write CFO and board commentary that leads with the "so what" of variances. In interviews, structured answers (SCQA opening, three reasons, risks) are what panels score. Project 2's Saturday assessment, a 15-minute recorded walkthrough defending ratio conclusions, tests this topic directly.

The subtopics form a sequence. First build the logic: the pyramid with governing thought, MECE pillars and evidence (s01), tested by vertical and horizontal logic (c02). Then build the opening: SCQA (s02). Then the headlines: action titles and the ghost deck (s03), with the "so what" test on every exhibit (c03). Then the evidence: quantified pillars with metric, trend, peer gap and driver (s04). Then the honesty layer: risks, monitorables and what would change the conclusion (s05). Finally assemble the document: the six-part report flow (s06) and the overall structure (c01), compressed into a one-page, two-minute executive summary (c04).

Related concepts to study

Equity research and credit rating report structures

Equity research and credit rating report structures as templates

Sell-side initiation reports open with a rating, target price and a short investment thesis of three or four bullets, then "key risks", then sections on industry, company, financials and valuation, with financial tables at the back. That is the pyramid with a valuation conclusion. Indian credit rating rationales from CRISIL, ICRA, CARE and India Ratings follow a standard format: rating action, "key rating drivers" split into strengths and weaknesses, liquidity position, outlook, "rating sensitivities" (factors that could lead to upgrade or downgrade), and an analytical approach note (consolidated or standalone). Both are useful templates for Project 2. Use the equity format for an investor question and the rating format for a lender question. The rating sensitivities section is the model for s05's "what would change the conclusion". Download one recent rationale for a peer and map its sections to the six-part flow.

Board/management MIS commentary style (Week 20)

Board/management MIS commentary style → Week 20

Monthly MIS and board-pack commentary apply the same principles to internal performance. Lead with the headline ("Q2 EBITDA ₹42 crore below budget, 70% from gold-price-driven inventory loss"). Group variances in MECE drivers (price, volume, mix, cost, one-offs). Quantify each, and close with actions and outlook. Board readers have even less time than external readers, so the one-page summary (c04) and action titles (s03) are mandatory. Commentary also needs a "so what" for the business: what management will do, by when. Week 20 builds this with variance bridges and rolling forecasts. The narrative discipline you build this week transfers directly. The difference is the audience: internal readers want accountability and actions, while external readers want comparative judgement.

Case presentation and storytelling in interviews (Week 24)

Case presentation and storytelling in interviews → Week 24

FP&A, equity research and transaction-advisory interviews often include a case: a set of financials or a peer table, preparation time, and a presentation to a panel. Panels score structure (did you lead with the answer?), quantification (did you cite numbers?), judgement (did you see the trade-off and the risks?) and composure under challenge. The pyramid gives a repeatable script. Open with an SCQA in 20 seconds, state three reasons with one number each, name the top risk, then invite questions. When challenged, go down a level ("the evidence for that is…") rather than abandoning the conclusion. Week 24 rehearses this with Project 2 as the portfolio piece, so this week's 15-minute recorded walkthrough is the first rehearsal.

Common pitfalls

Narrating data chronologically instead of leading with the answer

Narrating data chronologically instead of leading with the answer

The mistake. "In FY23, Titan's revenue was…; in FY24…; in FY25…; turning to Kalyan…", with the conclusion on the last page. Why it happens. Analysts write in the order they did the work, and a chronological account feels thorough and safe. Correct treatment. Reverse the order of discovery. Put the governing thought in the first paragraph (SCQA, s02) and organise by pillar (reason), not by year or company (s01). Years and companies become dimensions within each pillar's exhibit. Detection in review. Read only the first paragraph and the section headings. If you cannot state the conclusion and the three reasons, the narrative is chronological. Also look for headings such as "FY24 performance" or "Company overview".

Overlapping pillars

Overlapping pillars (e.g., margin and EBITDA growth as separate arguments)

The mistake. Presenting "margins expanded" and "EBITDA grew fastest" as two separate reasons. EBITDA growth is revenue growth plus margin change, so the same fact is counted twice and a key dimension (capital, risk) is missing. Why it happens. Pillars are written from the metric list rather than from a MECE frame. Correct treatment. Use a frame that is MECE by construction (growth / returns / risk, or margin / capital turnover / balance sheet) and test every pillar with the vertical and horizontal logic tests (c02). Move overlapping facts into one pillar as supporting evidence. Detection in review. For each fact cited, check whether it could appear in two pillars. Check also that each matrix dimension from topic t2 is covered by some pillar or is explicitly excluded as non-discriminating.

Omitting risks or making untraceable claims

Omitting risks or making claims not traceable to the workbook

The mistake. A report with no risk section, or one that lists only generic risks. Or claims ("superior cash conversion", "best-in-class working capital") that have no exhibit and no workbook cell behind them. Why it happens. Fear that risks weaken the conclusion, time pressure, and narrative written from memory after the analysis. Correct treatment. Include driver-linked, quantified risks with monitorables and explicit "what would change our view" thresholds (s05). Maintain a Storyline tab mapping every number in the narrative to a workbook cell and source (s04, topic t5). Apply the "so what" test (c03) in both directions: every exhibit supports a claim, and every claim has an exhibit or cell. Detection in review. Highlight every number and evaluative adjective in the summary, and trace each to the workbook. Any that cannot be traced within a minute fail.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full executive summary from a peer matrix [L3]

Jewellery peer data (FY25, consolidated, ₹ crore, illustrative):

Metric Titan (jewellery segment) Kalyan Senco
Revenue 50,000 25,000 6,300
3-yr revenue CAGR 21% 30% 26%
EBITDA margin (post-116) 10.5% 7.4% 7.0%
ROIC (FY22 → FY25) 19% → 24% 10% → 14% 12% → 11%
WACC 12.0% 12.5% 13.0%
CCC (days) 150 205 195
Net debt incl. GML/EBITDA 0.5x 1.5x (from 2.4x) 2.1x (from 1.4x)
Unhedged inventory share 15% 25% 40%
FOCO share of stores 20% 55% 35%

(a) Write a governing thought and SCQA paragraph. (b) Write three MECE quantified pillars (metric, trend, peer gap, driver). (c) Write three risks with monitorables and thresholds and one "what would change our view" statement. (d) Keep the whole summary within 350 words and describe the single exhibit.

Q2. Restructure a chronological draft [L2]

A draft report on Infosys, TCS and Wipro has these sections: 1. Company histories; 2. FY23 results; 3. FY24 results; 4. FY25 results; 5. Ratio tables; 6. Conclusion: "TCS appears strongest overall." Using the data CC CAGR (TCS 8.0%, Infosys 9.5%, Wipro 3.4%), EBITDA margin (27.3%, 24.8%, 20.3%), ROIC (55%, 38%, 21%) and CFO/EBITDA (76%, 81%, 79%): (a) write a ghost deck of 7 action titles, (b) map each old section's content to the new structure or the appendix, and (c) identify the "so what" of the ratio tables.

Q3. Logic tests and pillar repair [L2]

Governing thought: "Kalyan is the most improved capital allocator among listed jewellers." Draft pillars: (1) EBITDA margin up 100 bps; (2) EBITDA CAGR of 35%; (3) store count doubled to 280; (4) brand campaigns with national celebrities. Apply vertical and horizontal logic tests, rebuild a three-pillar MECE key line from the data in Q1, and explain where each discarded draft pillar goes.

Q4. Rating-rationale style rewrite [L3]

Rewrite your Q1 conclusion for a lender audience in the structure of an Indian rating rationale: key rating drivers (strengths and weaknesses), liquidity, outlook and rating sensitivities (upward and downward factors, quantified). Explain how pillar order and emphasis change compared with the investor version, and which additional data (cash, undrawn lines, GML maturity, covenants) you would need from the workbook.

Q5. Defending the narrative in a recorded walkthrough [L3]

Plan a 15-minute recorded walkthrough of your Q1 report. Give a minute-by-minute outline (slides with action titles). Then write prepared answers to three challenges: (i) "Titan's ROIC lead is just a segment-allocation artefact"; (ii) "Kalyan's improvement is only because franchisees fund its inventory"; (iii) "Your conclusion ignores gold-price risk". For each answer, cite the exhibit or workbook evidence you would show.
