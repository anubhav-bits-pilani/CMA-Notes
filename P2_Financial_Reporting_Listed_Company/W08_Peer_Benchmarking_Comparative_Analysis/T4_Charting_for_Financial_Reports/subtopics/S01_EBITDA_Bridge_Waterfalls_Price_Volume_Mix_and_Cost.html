Subtopic note

EBITDA Bridge Waterfalls: Price, Volume, Mix and Cost Drivers

Scope: EBITDA bridge waterfalls: revenue/price, volume, mix, raw material, employee cost, other opex

Why it matters

The EBITDA bridge is the single most-used chart in Indian sell-side results notes, board packs and FP&A variance decks. It answers the question a CFO or investor asks first: why did EBITDA move from last year to this year? A table of line items shows what changed; the bridge shows how much of the change each economic driver explains, in the order a reader thinks about it — top line first, then cost lines.

In Project 2 the bridge does two jobs. First, for each peer it decomposes the year-on-year EBITDA change so the report can say, for example, "Peer A's margin expansion came from pricing, not from cost control". Second, it is an integrity device: a bridge that starts at one period's EBITDA and ends at the next must reconcile exactly (see the bridge-integrity concept), so it catches mapping errors in the standardized statements. In interviews for FP&A roles, building a price-volume-mix bridge from raw data is a standard case exercise.

Core notes

Anatomy of a bridge

A bridge has three kinds of bars:

  • Start total — prior-period EBITDA (reported or normalized; never mixed).
  • Driver bars — positive (increase) or negative (decrease) effects, floating from the running cumulative.
  • End total — current-period EBITDA, which must equal start plus the sum of drivers.

The standard driver sequence for a manufacturing or consumer business is: price → volume → mix → raw material → employee cost → other operating expenses. Revenue-side drivers come first because they are the ones management controls least directly in the short run and investors weight most heavily; cost-side drivers follow in income-statement order (Schedule III Division II face order: cost of materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade, changes in inventories, employee benefits expense, other expenses).

Revenue-side drivers

Let \(P\) be average realised price per unit, \(V\) units sold, and \(c\) raw-material cost per unit. The revenue change is split as:

\[ \Delta \text{Revenue} = \underbrace{(P_t - P_{t-1}) \times V_t}_{\text{price}} + \underbrace{(V_t - V_{t-1}) \times P_{t-1}}_{\text{volume}} \]

This convention (price at current volume, volume at prior price) leaves no residual. In an EBITDA bridge the price effect drops through 100% (a price increase costs nothing to produce), whereas the volume effect should be valued at the prior-period unit contribution \((P_{t-1} - c_{t-1})\), because extra units also consume raw material. If you value volume at price and then show the full raw-material increase separately, the raw-material bar mixes genuine inflation with the cost of extra units — a common source of misleading bridges.

Mix arises when there are several products with different prices or margins. With products \(i\):

  • Price effect \(= \sum_i (P_{t,i} - P_{t-1,i}) \times V_{t,i}\)
  • Pure volume effect \(= (V_t^{\text{total}} - V_{t-1}^{\text{total}}) \times \bar{P}_{t-1}\), where \(\bar{P}_{t-1}\) is the prior weighted-average price
  • Mix effect \(= \sum_i V_{t,i} \times P_{t-1,i} - V_t^{\text{total}} \times \bar{P}_{t-1}\)

The mix effect is the revenue gained (or lost) purely because the portfolio shifted toward higher- (or lower-) priced products at prior prices. For an EBITDA bridge, replace prices with unit contributions.

Cost-side drivers

  • Raw material — the per-unit inflation effect \(-(c_t - c_{t-1}) \times V_t\). When unit data are not available (the usual case for listed peers, which disclose only rupee totals), use the gross margin effect \(\Delta \text{GM\%} \times \text{Revenue}_t\) and value revenue growth at prior gross margin.
  • Employee cost — \(-(\text{Employee}_t - \text{Employee}_{t-1})\); for IT services peers this is the dominant cost driver (wage hikes, utilisation, subcontracting).
  • Other opex — \(-(\text{Other}_t - \text{Other}_{t-1})\): power and fuel, freight, advertising, rent not capitalised under Ind AS 116 (short-term and low-value leases), royalty, franchise commission.

A sign convention is critical: drivers are shown as their effect on EBITDA, so a cost increase is a negative bar.

The story the chart tells: pricing (+21.6) did not fully pass through raw-material inflation (−27.0); EBITDA was flat only because volume grew 8%. EBITDA margin fell from 14.0% to 12.6%.

Mix illustration (revenue, ₹ lakh)

Product A: 60,000 units at ₹100 → 50,000 units at ₹105. Product B: 40,000 units at ₹200 → 60,000 units at ₹200. Revenue moves from ₹140 lakh to ₹172.5 lakh. Price = ₹2.5 lakh; prior average price = ₹140, so pure volume = 10,000 × ₹140 = ₹14 lakh; mix = (₹50 lakh + ₹120 lakh) − 1,10,000 × ₹140 = ₹16 lakh. Total ₹32.5 lakh reconciles. Most of the growth came from shifting toward Product B, not from selling more units.

Building it in Excel

Lay out a calculation block with one row per driver, columns for the formula, the value and a source reference. Feed the chart from this block, never from typed numbers. A typical block:

Start        = EBITDA_FY24
Volume       = (Vol_FY25 - Vol_FY24) * (Price_FY24 - RMc_FY24)
Price        = (Price_FY25 - Price_FY24) * Vol_FY25
RM           = -(RMc_FY25 - RMc_FY24) * Vol_FY25
Employee     = -(Emp_FY25 - Emp_FY24)
OtherOpex    = -(Opex_FY25 - Opex_FY24)
End          = EBITDA_FY25
Check        = ROUND(Start + SUM(Volume:OtherOpex) - End, 2)

Then plot with the native waterfall chart or the stacked-column workaround (covered in a separate subtopic).

Peer bridges with public data

Listed Indian peers rarely disclose units and prices. Practical substitutes:

  • Jewellery (Titan, Kalyan, Senco): gold price change versus volume (grams) proxies from investor presentations; studded-ratio shifts as mix; gold-on-lease or gold metal loan hedging effects as part of raw material.
  • IT services (Infosys, TCS, Wipro): constant-currency growth (volume/mix) versus currency effect (price-like); wage hikes, utilisation and subcontracting as employee-cost drivers.
  • Cement/FMCG: volume in tonnes is usually disclosed; realisation per tonne and cost per tonne allow a full unit-level bridge.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Ordering affects attribution only when an interaction term exists; with the price-at-current-volume convention there is none, but if you value volume at current price the split changes. State the convention in the chart footnote.
  • Normalized vs reported endpoints: if FY24 had a one-off (e.g., a ₹12 crore inventory write-down), either bridge normalized-to-normalized or add an explicit "one-offs" bar. Never start reported and end normalized.
  • Acquisitions: separate an "inorganic" bar so organic drivers are not overstated.
  • Ind AS 116 transition moves rent out of other opex; a pre/post-116 bridge shows a spurious "other opex" gain. Bridge on a consistent basis or add a lease-accounting bar.
  • Other operating revenue and other income: decide whether EBITDA includes other operating revenue (export incentives, scrap sales) and whether other income is excluded; apply identically across peers.
  • Too many bars: beyond 7–9 driver bars, group the minor ones into "others".

Key takeaways

  • A bridge decomposes the EBITDA change by economic driver; every bar must come from a formula block.
  • Price at current volume plus volume at prior price reconciles exactly with no residual.
  • In an EBITDA bridge, value volume at prior unit contribution, not at price, so raw material shows only inflation.
  • Mix captures portfolio shift at prior prices; it needs product-level data.
  • Without unit data, use revenue growth at prior gross margin plus the gross-margin-% effect.
  • Endpoints must tie to the reported or normalized EBITDA used elsewhere in the report.

Practice Questions

Q1. Unit-level EBITDA bridge [L2]

A cement company sold 12.0 million tonnes in FY24 at ₹5,200 per tonne with variable cost ₹3,400 per tonne; in FY25 it sold 13.2 million tonnes at ₹5,050 per tonne with variable cost ₹3,250 per tonne. Fixed employee cost rose from ₹620 crore to ₹680 crore and other fixed opex from ₹900 crore to ₹940 crore. Build the EBITDA bridge (volume at prior unit contribution, price, variable cost, employee, other opex) and show the zero-check.

💡 Hint: Compute FY24 and FY25 EBITDA independently first; the bridge must close to that difference.

Q2. Three-product price-volume-mix [L2]

A paints company sells three pack sizes. FY24: 1 L — 40 lakh units at ₹300; 4 L — 25 lakh units at ₹1,100; 20 L — 5 lakh units at ₹4,800. FY25: 1 L — 38 lakh units at ₹315; 4 L — 30 lakh units at ₹1,140; 20 L — 6 lakh units at ₹4,900. Decompose the revenue change into price, pure volume and mix effects and comment on which driver dominates.

Q3. Choosing drivers without unit data [L3]

You are building FY24→FY25 EBITDA bridges for Titan, Kalyan Jewellers and Senco Gold using only annual reports and investor presentations. Propose the driver set and the data proxy for each bar, explain how you would treat gold price movements and hedging gains/losses, and state what you would disclose in the footnote.

Q4. Spotting a broken bridge [L1]

A colleague's bridge shows FY24 EBITDA of ₹1,250 crore (normalized), five drivers summing to +₹185 crore and FY25 EBITDA of ₹1,410 crore (reported, including a ₹25 crore one-time insurance claim). Explain what is wrong and two ways to fix it.

Q5. Interview case: margin narrative [L3]

An IT services peer's EBITDA margin fell 180 bps in FY25 despite 6% constant-currency growth and 1.5% rupee depreciation benefit. List the bridge bars you would build, the order, and the one-sentence conclusion format you would put in the chart's action title.
