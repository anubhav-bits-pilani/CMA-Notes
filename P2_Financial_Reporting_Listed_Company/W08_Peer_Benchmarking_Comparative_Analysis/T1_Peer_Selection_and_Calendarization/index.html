Roadmap topic: Peer Group Selection & Calendarization: Aligning accounting policies (FIFO vs Weighted Average, depreciation methods) across competitors for true apples-to-apples comparison

Overview

Every conclusion in a comparative financial analysis report is relative. "Kalyan's ROIC is improving" means little. "Kalyan's ROIC has closed 400 bps of the gap to Titan while Senco's has widened" is a finding. But that finding is only as reliable as two foundations: who the peers are and whether their numbers measure the same thing. This topic builds both foundations before any ratio is computed in topic t2 or any narrative is written in t3. It is the step most reviewers (equity research supervisors, rating committee members, CFOs reading an FP&A benchmark) probe first.

The topic has two halves. Peer selection starts from public taxonomies: NSE's four-level industry classification and AMFI's market-cap buckets give a reproducible long list (s02, c04). Economic judgement then reduces it to a short list matched on revenue mix, scale, geography, business model and customer segment (s01). Comparability alignment then puts every peer on the same footing in four layers:

  • the same 12-month period, through calendarization and LTM (s03, c01, c02);
  • the same reporting basis: consolidated, continuing operations, ₹ crore, latest restated comparatives (s06);
  • the same accounting policies: inventory cost formula, depreciation, borrowing costs, gross vs net revenue (s04, c03);
  • the same framework: Ind AS vs IFRS 20-F, US GAAP ASC 842 and LIFO (s05).

The recommended learning sequence follows the order in which you would actually build Project 2. Screen the universe (s02, c04), choose and document peers (s01), fix the basis (s06), align periods (s03, c01, c02), align policies (s04, c03), and finally align frameworks if non-Ind AS peers are included (s05). The output is a peer-selection memo, a policy alignment table, an adjustments log and a quarterly data tab. Topics t2–t5 consume these directly, and they get reused in Week 10 (Power Query extraction), Week 13 (model historicals) and Week 16 (trading comparables).

Related concepts to study

Trading comparables and multiple selection (Week 16)

Trading comparables and multiple selection → Week 16

Trading comparables value a company by applying peer multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Sales, P/B) to its metrics. The peer set built here becomes the comps universe in Week 16, so the same selection logic applies, with two additions. First, the peers' growth and ROIC should be similar, because multiples are driven by growth, return on capital and risk. A peer with twice the growth deserves a higher multiple, not a "discount". Second, the period alignment becomes forward-looking: calendarized LTM, FY26E and FY27E consensus. The policy alignment you do now (Ind AS 116 leases in EBITDA, net debt definitions, LIFO and ASC 842 for US peers) also determines whether EV/EBITDA multiples are comparable. Mismatched lease treatment alone can move EV/EBITDA by several turns for retailers.

Automated multi-company extraction via Power Query (Week 10)

Automated multi-company extraction via Power Query → Week 10

Manual copy-paste from PDFs is where unit, sign and basis errors creep in (s06). In Week 10 you will automate extraction. Power Query can import quarterly results and annual financial data from Excel/CSV downloads (NSE/BSE XBRL-based files, data-provider exports). It can unpivot them into a long "fact" table (company, period-end date, line item, value, unit, basis, source) and merge NSE classification and AMFI lists on ISIN. Calendarization (s03) and LTM (c01) then become filters on period-end dates instead of hand-built formulas, and adding a fourth peer is a refresh rather than a rebuild. Design this week's raw-extracts tab in that long format so the Week 10 migration is direct.

Week 5 normalization rules applied identically to all peers

Week 5 normalization rules (EBITDA, net debt, invested capital) applied identically to all peers

Week 5 defined normalised EBITDA (excluding exceptional items, other income, one-off inventory gains or losses), net debt (borrowings plus lease liabilities, less cash, bank balances and liquid investments, with a stated rule for gold metal loans and earmarked balances) and invested capital (operating approach: net working capital plus net fixed assets plus ROU assets plus goodwill, or financing approach: equity plus net debt). The rule for this topic is uniformity. Every definition is written once on a definitions sheet and applied by the same formula to every peer and every year. A normalisation applied to one peer only (say, removing Titan's inventory gain but not Kalyan's) is a selective adjustment and biases the ranking. The adjustments log records each item per peer-year with its source.

Common pitfalls

Picking peers by sector label rather than business economics

Picking peers by sector label rather than business economics (e.g., Titan vs pure-play regional jewellers)

The mistake. Selecting every company in "Gems, Jewellery and Watches", or comparing Titan's consolidated numbers directly with a regional pure-play jeweller, as though the label made them comparable. Why it happens. Labels are quick, defensible-sounding and available on every data terminal. Correct treatment. Use the label only as the starting filter (s02). Then test revenue mix (Titan's watches, eyecare and emerging businesses; studded vs plain gold share), store model (owned vs franchise), geography (pan-India vs regional) and scale (s01). Use Titan's jewellery segment data for margins and capital employed where comparing jewellers. Detection in review. Ask for the peer-selection matrix. If there is no written reason per peer, or segment mix differs by more than about 20 percentage points without a caveat, the set fails.

Mixing fiscal periods or standalone/consolidated bases across peers

Mixing fiscal periods or standalone/consolidated bases across peers

The mistake. Comparing Castrol's January–December year with a March-year peer's FY, or one peer's standalone figures with another's consolidated. Why it happens. Data aggregators label every company's year "FY2024" regardless of its end date. Quarterly press releases often headline standalone numbers. Correct treatment. Calendarize to a common window by summing Reg 33 quarters (s03, c02), use LTM for the latest data (c01), and use consolidated statements for all peers unless the question is entity-specific (s06). Detection in review. Every table header should state the period end and basis. Add a workbook check that counts four quarters per peer-window and a basis flag column that must be identical across peers.

Not disclosing residual policy differences that cannot be quantitatively adjusted

Not disclosing residual policy differences that cannot be quantitatively adjusted

The mistake. Adjusting what is easy (lease add-backs, unit conversions) and silently ignoring what is not (cost formula effect with no disclosure, useful-life judgements, lease-term estimates, principal-vs-agent calls). Why it happens. Analysts fear that caveats weaken the report, or they simply never tabulated policies. Correct treatment. Complete the policy alignment table for every peer (s04, s05). Mark each row "adjusted", "disclosed – not quantifiable" or "immaterial". Add a short comparability note in the report body when a residual difference could plausibly affect a ranking. Detection in review. Ask, "What would change if Peer B used Peer A's policy?" If the analyst cannot answer from the alignment table, or the report has no comparability note, the disclosure is missing.

Practice Questions

Q1. End-to-end peer set for a jewellery report [L3]

You are preparing a comparative report on Kalyan Jewellers. Candidate peers are Titan Company, Senco Gold, PN Gadgil Jewellers, Thangamayil Jewellery, a B2B diamond exporter and a gold-bullion trader. Use this data (FY25, ₹ crore, illustrative): Titan consolidated revenue 57,800, of which jewellery segment 50,000; Kalyan 25,000; Senco 6,300; PN Gadgil 7,700; Thangamayil 4,900; diamond exporter 3,200 (90% exports); bullion trader 9,000 (gross margin 1.1%). (a) Apply a two-step screen (taxonomy, then economics) and select a final set with written reasons. (b) Identify the Titan figures you would use for margin and ROIC. (c) Draft the comparability note for the report covering franchise models, studded mix and gold-metal-loan presentation.

Q2. Calendarization and LTM for a lubricant peer set [L2]

Castrol India (December year-end) quarterly EBITDA (₹ crore): Jan–Mar 24: 330; Apr–Jun 24: 310; Jul–Sep 24: 285; Oct–Dec 24: 320; Jan–Mar 25: 345; Apr–Jun 25: 325; Jul–Sep 25: 300. A March-year peer reports FY25 EBITDA ₹1,450 crore, H1 FY26 EBITDA ₹760 crore and H1 FY25 EBITDA ₹700 crore. (a) Compute Castrol's EBITDA for the 12 months to 31 March 2025 and LTM to 30 September 2025. (b) Compute the peer's LTM to 30 September 2025. (c) Compute Castrol's CY2024 EBITDA from its own reported quarters, and explain why using it against the peer's FY25 would mislead. (d) State the balance-sheet date you would use for each company's net debt in an LTM leverage comparison.

Q3. Policy and framework alignment table [L3]

Peer A (Ind AS) uses weighted average inventory, SLM depreciation and capitalises ₹45 crore of borrowing costs in FY25. Peer B (Ind AS) uses FIFO and WDV and capitalised nothing. Peer C (US GAAP, USD) uses LIFO with a LIFO reserve rising from USD 120 million to USD 150 million and reports operating lease cost of USD 180 million inside SG&A. FY25 figures: Peer A EBITDA ₹2,800 crore, finance cost ₹210 crore; Peer B EBITDA ₹2,500 crore, D&A ₹480 crore on opening gross block ₹3,000 crore (Peer A D&A ₹260 crore on ₹3,100 crore); Peer C EBITDA USD 620 million. The average rate is ₹84/USD. (a) Build the policy alignment table. (b) Quantify each adjustment you can make to EBITDA, EBIT and interest coverage. (c) List the residual differences you would disclose and why.

Q4. Basis reconciliation and restated comparatives [L2]

For a three-year IT peer series (FY23–FY25), you have: FY25 annual report with FY25 and FY24 (restated for a common-control merger adding ₹3,400 crore to FY24 revenue); FY24 annual report with FY24 (original) and FY23; FY23 revenue of the merged entity ₹3,050 crore (from the scheme document). Original FY24 revenue is ₹1,21,000 crore, FY23 is ₹1,12,500 crore and FY25 is ₹1,31,200 crore. Wipro-style data arrives in ₹ million. (a) Build both the reported and like-for-like revenue series and the respective two-year CAGRs. (b) Specify the source-log entries. (c) Explain which series the peer matrix should use and the footnote required.

Q5. Interview case: defending comparability [L3]

In a final-round FP&A interview you present a benchmarking slide showing that Infosys has the highest EBITDA margin among Infosys, TCS, Wipro and Accenture. The interviewer challenges: (i) Infosys numbers came from a US terminal in USD under IFRS; (ii) Accenture reports under US GAAP with an August year-end and adjusted metrics excluding business-optimisation costs; (iii) Wipro data was standalone. Prepare a structured response explaining how each challenge could change the ranking, how you would fix it (with the specific source documents and adjustments), and what residual caveat would remain on the corrected slide.
