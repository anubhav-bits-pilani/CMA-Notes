Key concept

Definitions Sheet: One Formula per Metric Across All Peers

Concept: Definitions sheet: one formula definition per metric used across all peers

Statement

Every metric used anywhere in the workbook or report has exactly one entry on the definitions sheet, and every calculation of that metric — for every peer, every year, every exhibit — implements that single definition. Each entry specifies:

Field Content
Metric ID and name e.g., M07 Return on invested capital (ROIC)
Formula in words NOPAT ÷ average invested capital
Numerator EBIT (excluding other income) × (1 − effective tax rate)
Denominator Average of opening and closing (total equity + total borrowings + lease liabilities − cash and current investments)
Standard line codes used EBIT, TAX, EQ, BORR-NC, BORR-C, LEASE-L, CASH, CURINV
Balance basis Average (opening + closing) ÷ 2
Treatment notes Post-Ind AS 116; goodwill included; gold metal loans treated as borrowings
Unit and format %, 0.0%
Direction Higher is better
Source of non-financial inputs None
Version / change date v1.1, 25-Nov-2026

Explanation

Inconsistent definitions are the most dangerous error in peer analysis because they do not show up as arithmetic failures — they silently change rankings. One peer's EBITDA includes other income and another's does not; one CCC uses closing receivables and another average; one ROIC includes lease liabilities and another excludes them. A definitions sheet prevents this in three ways:

  1. Single source of truth: the ratio engine's formulas are written once per metric row and copied across all company-year columns; no company-specific formula variants. Where a peer genuinely needs different data (e.g., no inventory for an IT company), the definition handles it explicitly (DIO = 0 or "n.m.") rather than through an ad hoc edit.
  2. Transparency: the report appendix prints the definitions sheet, so readers know what "ROIC" means and can compare with their own.
  3. Change control: a definition change (e.g., switching EBITDA to pre-Ind AS 116) is made once, logged with a version, and flows through every peer.

Good practice details:

  • Link the ratio engine's metric labels to the definitions sheet (=Definitions!C9) so labels cannot diverge.
  • Store parameters that definitions depend on (day-count basis 365, tax rate method) as named cells on the definitions sheet.
  • Reconcile to company-reported metrics: if a company reports its own "EBITDA" or "ROCE", note the differences rather than switching to the company's definition for that peer.
  • Record sector-specific KPIs (constant-currency growth, gold volume, same-store sales growth) with source type and whether audited.
  • Keep it MECE: no two metrics with near-identical definitions under different names.

Illustration

A draft matrix ranked Peer A first on CCC. The definitions sheet said: "CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO; DIO on average inventory ÷ COGS × 365". Review found Peer A's DIO used revenue as the denominator (a copied formula from a different template), giving 92 days instead of 121. Because the engine had a single row formula for every other peer, the anomaly stood out as the only formula that differed in the row (Excel's inconsistent formula error indicator also flagged it). After correction, Peer A dropped to second on CCC, and the composite ranking changed — a definitional inconsistency, not an arithmetic error, had driven the ranking.

Practice Questions

Q1. Write three definitions [L2]

Write complete definitions-sheet entries (all fields in the table) for net debt/EBITDA, CFO/EBITDA and cash conversion cycle for a jewellery peer set where gold metal loans are significant and some peers operate franchise stores.

Q3. Detecting definitional drift [L2]

Describe three Excel techniques to confirm that every company-year cell in a ratio engine row implements the same definition, and what you would do if one peer genuinely lacks an input (e.g., no inventory).
