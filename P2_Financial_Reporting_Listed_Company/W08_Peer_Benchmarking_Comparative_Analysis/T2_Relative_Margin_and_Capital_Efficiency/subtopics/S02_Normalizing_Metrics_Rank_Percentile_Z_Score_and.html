Normalizing Metrics: Rank, Percentile, Z-Score and Direction

Scope: Normalization: rank, percentile, z-score; direction-adjusting metrics where lower is better (CCC, leverage)

Why it matters

The six metrics in s01 are in different units: percent, days and multiples. They also point in different directions, since a higher CAGR is good and a higher CCC is bad. You cannot add 10% CAGR to 82 days of CCC. Normalisation converts each metric to a common, unit-free scale where "higher = better" before weighting (s03). The method you choose changes the ranking, because rank throws away magnitude and z-score keeps it. Reviewers expect you to name the method, justify it and show that the conclusion survives an alternative. This is also a classic interview question: "How would you combine metrics with different units into one score?"

Core notes

Step 0: direction-adjust

Classify every metric on the definitions sheet:

Metric Direction Multiplier
3-yr revenue CAGR Higher is better +1
EBITDA margin level Higher is better +1
EBITDA margin CV Lower is better −1
ROIC Higher is better +1
CCC Lower is better −1
Net debt/EBITDA Lower is better −1 (after net-cash treatment, s04)
CFO/EBITDA Higher is better (capped near 100%) +1

Direction is applied inside the normalisation. For ranks, use ascending order for lower-is-better metrics. For z-scores, multiply by −1 (concept c03). For percentiles, use \(1 - \text{percentile}\).

Method 1: Rank

Rank 1 is best. For \(n\) peers, rank runs 1 to \(n\). Convert to a score where higher is better, for example \(\text{score} = n + 1 - \text{rank}\), or scale to 0–1 as \((n - \text{rank})/(n-1)\).

=RANK.EQ(B5, $B5:$F5, 0)          ' higher is better: descending
=RANK.EQ(B9, $B9:$F9, 1)          ' lower is better (CCC): ascending
  • Pros: robust to outliers, easy to explain, works with any distribution.
  • Cons: discards magnitude. A 0.1-percentage-point margin lead and a 10-point lead both earn "one rank". Ties need a rule: RANK.EQ gives equal ranks, and RANK.AVG averages them.

Method 2: Percentile

Percentile rank places each value within the peer distribution on a 0–1 scale.

=PERCENTRANK.INC($B5:$F5, B5)          ' higher is better
=1 - PERCENTRANK.INC($B9:$F9, B9)      ' lower is better

PERCENTRANK.INC returns \((\text{number of values below } x) / (n - 1)\), with interpolation for values between data points. With 3 peers the only possible scores are 0, 0.5 and 1, so a percentile is just a rescaled rank. With 15–20 peers (a broad comps universe) percentiles become informative. PERCENTRANK.EXC excludes the end points (scores lie strictly between 0 and 1), which avoids awarding a perfect 1.0 to the top company.

  • Pros: bounded, comparable across metrics, intuitive ("top quartile").
  • Cons: still mostly ordinal, and coarse for small peer sets.

Method 3: Z-score

\[ z_{i,m} = d_m \times \frac{x_{i,m} - \bar{x}_m}{s_m} \]

where \(x_{i,m}\) is peer \(i\)'s value on metric \(m\), \(\bar{x}_m\) is the peer mean, \(s_m\) is the peer standard deviation, and \(d_m\) is +1 or −1 (direction).

=LET(x, B5, rng, $B5:$F5, d, $H5,
     d * (x - AVERAGE(rng)) / STDEV.S(rng))
  • Pros: keeps magnitude, so a peer far ahead gets credit proportionate to the gap. Scores are comparable across metrics in standard-deviation units.
  • Cons: sensitive to outliers (one extreme value inflates \(s_m\) and compresses everyone else). It also assumes something like a symmetric distribution, and it is unstable for small \(n\). With 3 peers the z-scores on any metric always sum to zero. Their magnitudes are bounded too: with STDEV.S the largest possible absolute z is \((n-1)/\sqrt{n} \approx 1.15\). One outlier then makes the other two look near-identical.

STDEV.S vs STDEV.P. STDEV.S (sample, divides by \(n-1\)) is conventional because peers are treated as a sample of possible competitors. STDEV.P (population, divides by \(n\)) is defensible if the peer set is the whole universe of interest. The choice scales all z-scores by the same factor for a given \(n\), so it does not change rankings within a metric. But state it on the definitions sheet.

Illustration: five-peer CCC (days, lower is better)

Peer CCC Rank (asc) Rank score \((n+1-r)\) 1 − PERCENTRANK.INC z-score × (−1)
P1 60 1 5 1.00 +0.79
P2 70 2 4 0.75 +0.53
P3 75 3 3 0.50 +0.40
P4 90 4 2 0.25 +0.00
P5 155 5 1 0.00 −1.71

Mean = 90, STDEV.S ≈ 37.9. Rank and percentile treat P4 → P5 as one step, and z-score treats it as a 1.71-sigma gap (P5 alone sits 1.71 standard deviations below the mean, while P1–P4 are bunched within 0.8). If P5's 155 days reflects a genuine business-model difference (for example a jeweller with a heavy studded inventory), z-score penalises it heavily. If it is a one-off inventory build, z-score over-penalises. P5's outlier also inflates the standard deviation, which compresses the gaps among P1–P4: a 15-day difference between P1 and P4 earns less than 0.8 sigma. Fix the cause first (normalise the inventory build, or caveat the model difference). Then either use rank, or winsorise the raw value or cap the z-score (s04). In larger peer sets, caps of ±2 or ±2.5 are common.

Choosing the method

Situation Preferred method
3–5 peers (typical Project 2) Rank or 0–1 rank score as primary, z-score as a robustness check
10+ peers (comps universe) Percentile or capped z-score
Outliers present Rank, percentile, or winsorised/capped z-score
Magnitude matters for the story Z-score, with underlying values shown

Whatever the method, always show the raw values beside the normalised scores (pitfall 2 in topic.md).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Zero standard deviation. If all peers have the same value, \(s_m = 0\) and the z-score is undefined (#DIV/0!). Wrap it as IF(STDEV.S(rng)=0, 0, …). The metric carries no information.
  • Non-monotonic "good". CFO/EBITDA above 100% is not necessarily better (working-capital release). CCC that is very negative may signal supplier stretch. Consider capping the metric at a sensible band before normalising.
  • Ties. Decide RANK.EQ vs RANK.AVG and apply it consistently. RANK.AVG keeps total points constant.
  • Mixing methods across metrics. Don't. A composite that adds ranks for some metrics and z-scores for others weights them inconsistently.
  • Peer set changes. Normalised scores are relative. Adding a fourth peer changes every other peer's score. Freeze the peer set before ranking, and report sensitivity if a peer is added.

Key takeaways

  • Normalise to make metrics unit-free, and direction-adjust so higher always means better before weighting.
  • Rank and percentile are robust but discard magnitude. Z-score keeps magnitude but is outlier-sensitive and unstable for 3–5 peers.
  • Use RANK.EQ (or RANK.AVG) with ascending order for lower-is-better metrics, 1 − PERCENTRANK.INC, or multiply z-scores by −1.
  • State STDEV.S vs STDEV.P, the tie rule and any caps on the definitions sheet.
  • Show raw values next to normalised scores, and test the conclusion under at least two methods.

Practice Questions

Q1. Three methods on one metric [L1]

ROIC for five peers is 18%, 22%, 25%, 31% and 64%. Compute the rank score \((n+1-\text{rank})\), PERCENTRANK.INC and z-score (STDEV.S) for each. Comment on how the 64% outlier affects each method.

Q2. Direction-adjusting a lower-is-better metric [L2]

Net debt/EBITDA for four jewellers is 0.4x, 1.1x, 1.8x and 2.6x. Write the Excel formulas (RANK.EQ, PERCENTRANK.INC, z-score) that produce higher-is-better scores, compute the scores, and explain why the direction multiplier is applied after centring rather than to the raw ratio before averaging.

💡 Hint: Multiplying raw values by −1 and then z-scoring gives the same answer. Check it and explain why.

Q3. Small-sample z-score behaviour [L2]

For a three-peer set, prove numerically that the z-scores on any metric sum to zero. Show also that with STDEV.S the maximum absolute z-score is about 1.15, using values 10, 10 and 40. Explain the implication for a composite score built on three peers.

Q4. Method choice for a broad comps universe [L3]

You are expanding the IT peer set to 14 companies, including mid-tier Indian IT firms and US-listed peers. Two of them have ROIC above 80% (asset-light, small IC) and one has negative EBITDA growth. Recommend a normalisation method and any caps or winsorisation rules for each of the six metrics. Write the definitions-sheet entries you would add.

Q5. LET-based normalisation function [L3]

Write a single LET formula (optionally wrapped in a LAMBDA named NormZ) that takes a metric value, the peer range, a direction flag and a cap, and returns a capped, direction-adjusted z-score. It should return 0 when the standard deviation is zero. Describe the test cases you would use to check it.
