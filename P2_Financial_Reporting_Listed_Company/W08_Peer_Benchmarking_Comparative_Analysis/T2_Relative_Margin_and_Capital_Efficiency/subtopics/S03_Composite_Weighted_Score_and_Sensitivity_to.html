Composite Weighted Score and Sensitivity to Weights

Scope: Composite weighted score and sensitivity of rankings to weights

Why it matters

Once metrics are normalised (s02), the matrix has to produce an answer: which peer is the strongest capital allocator, and by how much? A composite weighted score aggregates the normalised metrics into one number per peer. It is the quantitative backbone of the report's governing conclusion (topic t3). But the weights are a judgement. A reader who disagrees with them should be able to see whether the conclusion survives. Sensitivity analysis is what separates an institutional-grade matrix from a scorecard that can be gamed. The Week 8 checklist item explicitly asks for "a ranked capital efficiency matrix with sensitivity to weights".

Core notes

The composite formula

\[ S_i = \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m \times z_{i,m}, \qquad \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m = 1, \quad w_m \ge 0 \]

where \(S_i\) is peer \(i\)'s composite score, \(w_m\) is the weight on metric \(m\), and \(z_{i,m}\) is the direction-adjusted normalised score (rank score, percentile or z-score, the same method for every metric). See concept c04.

=SUMPRODUCT(Weights, INDEX(NormScores, , MATCH(B$3, Peers, 0)))

Setting the base-case weights

Anchor weights in what drives value in the sector, and document the reasoning:

Metric IT services weight Jewellery weight Reasoning
3-yr revenue CAGR 25% 20% Growth drives value in both. In jewellery, part of growth is gold inflation
EBITDA margin level 20% 15% IT margin reflects pricing power and mix. Jewellery margin is structurally thin
EBITDA margin CV 10% 10% Predictability
ROIC 25% 25% Core value-creation metric (pair with WACC, c05)
CCC 10% 15% Critical for inventory-heavy jewellers
Net debt/EBITDA 0% 10% IT peers are all net-cash, so little information
CFO/EBITDA 10% 5% Earnings quality
Total 100% 100%

Equal weights (1/M each) are a neutral reference case and should always be shown. Theory-based weights use the value driver formula. Value depends on growth, ROIC and risk:

\[ \text{Value} = \frac{\text{NOPAT} \times (1 - g / \text{ROIC})}{\text{WACC} - g} \]

So growth and ROIC deserve the largest weights, with the other metrics acting as supporting or risk indicators.

Sensitivity of rankings to weights

Run at least three kinds of sensitivity:

  1. Scenario weights. Equal weights, "growth-tilted" (CAGR 40%), "quality-tilted" (ROIC 35%, CV 15%, CFO/EBITDA 15%) and "balance-sheet-tilted" (leverage and CCC 40%). Show the rank of each peer under each scenario in one table.
  2. One-at-a-time perturbation. Move each weight by ±10 percentage points, rescaling the others proportionally so the sum stays 1, and record rank changes:

\[ w'_k = w_k + \Delta, \qquad w'_m = w_m \times \frac{1 - w_k - \Delta}{1 - w_k} \quad (m \ne k) \]

  1. Break-even weight. For two closely ranked peers A and B, find the weight on metric \(k\) at which their scores tie. This tells the reader how much they would have to believe in metric \(k\) to flip the conclusion.

Excel tools: a two-variable Data Table (What-If Analysis) varying two weights, a scenario selector with CHOOSE/INDEX over weight sets, or a LAMBDA that takes a weights vector and returns the rank order.

Base composite: \(S_A = 0.25(0.55) + 0.20(1.05) + 0.10(1.00) + 0.25(1.08) + 0.10(0.98) + 0.10(-1.09) = 0.7065\). Similarly \(S_B = 0.176\) and \(S_C = -0.8825\). The ranking is A > B > C.

Sensitivity: under a "cash-quality tilt" (CFO/EBITDA 40%, other weights scaled down proportionally), B's score rises. The break-even CFO/EBITDA weight at which B overtakes A tells you how robust "A is best" is. If the break-even weight is implausibly high (say 60%), the conclusion is robust. If it is 25%, the report must caveat it.

Presenting sensitivity

  • A rank-stability table: rows are peers, columns are weight scenarios, cells are ranks. Colour unchanged ranks neutral and changed ranks amber.
  • A sentence for the narrative: "Peer A ranks first in 11 of 12 weight scenarios. It is displaced only when cash conversion carries more than 35% weight."
  • A tornado chart of the score gap between first and second place for ±10 pp moves in each weight.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Weights as a lever for bias. Choosing weights after seeing results to produce a desired ranking is a classic analytical error. Fix weights and their rationale before computing scores, and record them in the change log.
  • Correlated metrics. If two metrics are highly correlated (margin and ROIC in asset-light IT), their combined weight effectively double-counts. Reduce one or combine them.
  • Zero-information metrics. When every peer is net-cash, leverage scores are noise. Set its weight to 0 and flag it, rather than letting tiny differences in cash pile drive ranks.
  • Score magnitude vs rank. Report composite scores, not just the final rank. A 0.02 gap is a tie in practice.
  • Peer set dependency. Composite scores are relative to the peer set. A peer added later can reorder others. State the set in the table title.
  • Negative weights. Never use them. Direction adjustment belongs in normalisation, not in the weights.

Key takeaways

  • Composite score = Σ weight × direction-adjusted normalised score, with weights summing to 1 and a check cell.
  • Ground base weights in value drivers (growth, ROIC, risk) and sector economics, and always show an equal-weight case.
  • Test sensitivity with scenario weights, one-at-a-time ±10 pp perturbations and break-even weights.
  • Report composite magnitudes and rank stability, not only the final order.
  • Set weights before seeing results, and document the rationale in the change log.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute composites under two weight sets [L2]

Using the z-scores in the illustration table above, compute composite scores for Peers A, B and C under (a) equal weights across the six metrics with non-zero information (exclude leverage) and (b) a growth tilt where revenue CAGR is 45% and the remaining 55% is spread proportionally to the base weights. Report the ranking in each case.

Q2. Break-even weight [L3]

Two jewellers have direction-adjusted scores: Peer X: CAGR +0.9, margin +0.2, CV −0.3, ROIC +0.4, CCC −0.8, leverage −0.6, CFO/EBITDA +0.1. Peer Y: CAGR −0.2, margin +0.5, CV +0.6, ROIC +0.3, CCC +0.7, leverage +0.5, CFO/EBITDA +0.3. Base weights are 20/15/10/25/15/10/5. Compute both composites. Then find the weight on revenue CAGR, with other weights rescaled proportionally, at which the two tie.

💡 Hint: Write each composite as a linear function of the CAGR weight after proportional rescaling, then solve for equality.

Q3. Build a sensitivity engine [L3]

Specify an Excel sheet that holds five named weight scenarios, a selector cell, a composite score row and a rank row for four peers. It must also hold a rank-stability table across all scenarios, built either with a one-variable Data Table over the scenario index or with a LAMBDA, plus a tornado-chart data block for ±10 pp perturbations. List the check cells you would include.

Q4. Critique a weighting scheme [L2]

A draft report uses weights of 40% EBITDA margin, 30% EBIT margin, 20% PAT margin and 10% ROIC for an IT peer set, and concludes that Peer A is the best capital allocator. Critique the scheme on redundancy, the missing dimensions and its link to the stated conclusion. Propose replacement weights with reasons.

Q5. Interview: "Your ranking depends on your weights" [L2]

An interviewer says, "Your weights are arbitrary, so your ranking is meaningless." Prepare a 90-second answer explaining how you set the weights, how you tested them and how you would present the result so the reader can apply their own view.
