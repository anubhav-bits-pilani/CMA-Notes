Roadmap topic: Relative Margin & Capital Efficiency Matrix: Ranking peers across 3-year Revenue CAGR, EBITDA margin stability, ROIC, CCC, and Net Debt/EBITDA

Overview

The capital efficiency matrix is the quantitative core of Project 2. It takes the standardized, policy-aligned three-year statements from topic t1 and answers one question with evidence: which peer converts growth and capital into value most effectively, and by how much? It condenses six dimensions into a table, a set of normalised scores, a weighted composite and two quadrant views: growth, margin level, margin stability, return on capital, working-capital intensity, leverage and cash conversion. Equity analysts use the same structure in sector initiation reports. Rating agencies use it to position a borrower against peers. FP&A teams use it to benchmark their company in board strategy reviews.

The method has three layers. Measurement (s01, c01, c02, c05) defines each metric precisely and identically for every peer: 3-year CAGR, margin level and coefficient of variation, ROIC (and its spread over WACC), CCC, net debt/EBITDA and CFO/EBITDA. Scoring (s02, s03, c03, c04) turns metrics with different units and directions into comparable scores (rank, percentile or z-score, direction-adjusted), then into a weighted composite whose weights are justified and stress-tested. Robustness and presentation (s04, s05, s06) handle the cases that break naive scoring: net-cash peers, outliers and Ind AS 116 definitional choices. Quadrant charts and heat maps then show why the ranking comes out as it does.

Work through the topic in build order. Define metrics (s01, c01, c02, c05), normalise (s02, c03), treat net-cash peers and outliers (s04), composite and sensitivity (s03, c04), test Ind AS 116 robustness (s06), and finally visualise (s05). The outputs feed the executive narrative (topic t3), where each quantitative pillar cites matrix metrics, and the charting topic (t4). The same engine is rebuilt with LET/LAMBDA in Week 9 and as a Power BI dashboard with RANKX in Week 12.

Related concepts to study

Excel ranking and statistics functions (Week 9)

Excel: RANK.EQ, PERCENTRANK.INC, STDEV.S, LET/LAMBDA, conditional formatting → Week 9

The matrix uses a small set of functions. Week 9 covers them in model-audit depth. RANK.EQ (with order 0 for descending and 1 for ascending) and RANK.AVG for ties. PERCENTRANK.INC and .EXC for bounded scores. STDEV.S versus STDEV.P for z-scores. LET keeps intermediate values (range, mean, standard deviation, direction) named and computed once. LAMBDA wraps a normalisation rule into a reusable function, for example a NormZ(value, range, direction, cap), so the same logic applies to every metric. Formula-based conditional formatting drives heat maps from a parallel score block. Week 9 also adds FAST-standard layout and audit checks. Build this week's matrix so its formulas can be converted to named LAMBDA functions without restructuring.

Peer dashboard with slicers and RANKX (Week 12)

Peer dashboard with slicers and DAX ranking (RANKX) → Week 12

In Week 12 the matrix becomes an interactive Power BI dashboard. A peer dimension and a metric fact table feed measures such as RANKX(ALLSELECTED(Peer[Company]), [ROIC]), which re-rank dynamically when a user slices by year, sector or peer subset. A disconnected weights table with what-if parameters lets users change weights and watch the composite re-rank, which is the dashboard version of the s03 sensitivity. Z-scores use AVERAGEX and STDEVX.S over ALLSELECTED peers. The design lesson carries back to this week: keep data in a long, tidy format (company, year, metric, value, definition version) so it can move to Power BI unchanged, and keep definitions in one place.

ROIC, CCC and leverage definitions (Week 6)

ROIC, CCC, leverage definitions → Week 6

Week 6's ratio dictionary fixed the definitions this topic depends on. ROIC is NOPAT over average operating invested capital, with explicit choices on goodwill, surplus cash and ROU assets. CCC is DIO + DSO − DPO, with DIO and DPO on COGS, averages of opening and closing balances, and unbilled revenue included in IT DSO. Leverage is net debt including lease liabilities over EBITDA, plus interest coverage and net gearing as alternatives. The matrix must not redefine any of these. It references the dictionary through the definitions sheet. If this week's analysis reveals a need to change a definition (for example the treatment of gold metal loans in net debt), change it in the dictionary, apply it to all peers and all years, and record the change in the change log.

Common pitfalls

Ranking net debt/EBITDA naively when some peers have negative net debt

Ranking net debt/EBITDA naively when some peers have negative net debt

The mistake. Ranking raw net debt/EBITDA ascending, so the most negative value wins. This rewards cash hoards and, worse, can rank a loss-making levered company (positive net debt, negative EBITDA, negative ratio) as the safest. Why it happens. The ratio is "lower is better" in normal ranges, and analysts apply that rule mechanically. Correct treatment. Floor net-cash peers at 0x, cap stressed values (for example 5x), assign "worst" to negative EBITDA with net debt, and show a separate net-cash flag and net cash as % of market cap (s04). Reduce the leverage weight when all peers are net-cash (s03). Detection in review. Scan for negative ratios in the leverage row and check the sign of EBITDA for each. Any negative raw ratio entering the scoring without a floor is an error.

Showing only ranks without underlying values, hiding small real gaps

Showing only ranks without underlying values, hiding small real gaps

The mistake. Presenting a table of 1s, 2s and 3s, or a coloured heat map with no numbers, so a 10 bps margin difference looks the same as a 1,000 bps difference. Why it happens. Rank tables look clean and decisive, and three-peer percentiles produce perfect green/amber/red rows. Correct treatment. Always display raw values beside scores. Use economic-significance thresholds (for example ±100 bps margin, ±10 days CCC treated as "in line"). Report composite magnitudes and gaps, not only order, and state when a gap is within noise (s02, s05, c04). Detection in review. Ask, for each "first place", how big the lead is in the metric's own units. If the analyst must look it up, the presentation is hiding the gap.

Inconsistent definitions across peers silently driving the ranking

Inconsistent definitions across peers silently driving the ranking

The mistake. One peer's EBITDA includes other income, another's excludes it. One peer's DSO includes unbilled revenue, another's doesn't. One peer's net debt includes lease liabilities or gold metal loans and another's doesn't. These differences, not performance, then drive the ranking. Why it happens. Figures are copied from company presentations or data terminals that apply each company's own definitions. Correct treatment. Compute every metric from standardized statements using one definitions sheet (Week 6 dictionary). Log every adjustment, and run the Ind AS 116 robustness test (s06) to expose definition sensitivity. Detection in review. Pick one metric and recompute it for each peer from the standardized statements. Any difference from the matrix value signals a definition leak. A definitions-version column in the data table makes leaks visible.

Q3. Weight sensitivity and break-even [L2]
