Subtopic note

Bargain Purchase to Capital Reserve and Common-Control Mergers

Scope: Bargain purchase: Indian carve-out to capital reserve; common-control combinations at book value (Appendix C)

Why it matters

Two features of Ind AS 103 differ from IFRS 3 or are especially important in India. First, when the fair value of net assets exceeds the consideration (a bargain purchase), IFRS 3 recognises a gain in profit or loss, but Ind AS 103 sends it to capital reserve, keeping it out of profit, EPS and dividends. This is common in IBC acquisitions and distressed sales. Second, Indian groups restructure constantly through schemes of arrangement: mergers of subsidiaries, demergers and transfers between fellow subsidiaries. Those are common-control combinations, accounted at book values under Appendix C, with no new goodwill or fair-value step-ups. Using the wrong method changes the balance sheet and post-deal EPS materially.

Core notes

Bargain purchase (Ind AS 103.34–36)

A bargain purchase arises when

\[ \text{FV of net identifiable assets} > \text{Consideration transferred} + \text{NCI} + \text{FV of previously held interest} \]

Step 1: reassess (103.36). Before recognising a gain, the acquirer must review whether it has correctly identified all assets acquired and all liabilities assumed, and review the procedures used to measure the identifiable net assets, NCI, previously held interest and consideration. Bargain purchases are expected to be rare, so the reassessment is a real test: missed liabilities (contingent liabilities, environmental obligations), over-valued intangibles or understated DTL are common causes.

Step 2: recognise (Indian carve-out, 103.34):

  • If there exists clear evidence of the underlying reasons for classifying the combination as a bargain purchase (for example a forced sale, an IBC resolution plan, a distressed seller, a regulatory requirement to divest), the gain is recognised in OCI on the acquisition date and accumulated in equity as capital reserve.
  • If there is no such clear evidence, the excess is recognised directly in equity as capital reserve (not through OCI).

In both cases, the gain never passes through profit or loss, and it does not affect EPS (c06). Capital reserve is not available for dividend distribution.

Why the carve-out: Indian regulators were concerned that recognising gains in P&L could be used to inflate profits and distributable reserves, and Indian GAAP (AS 14) already took such differences to capital reserve.

Illustration: an IBC resolution applicant pays ₹1,200 crore for 100% of a steel company. Fair value of identifiable net assets after DTL: ₹1,540 crore. After reassessment, the PPA is confirmed. The forced sale under IBC is clear evidence.

Common-control combinations (Ind AS 103 Appendix C)

Scope (Appendix C para 2–4): a business combination in which all combining entities or businesses are ultimately controlled by the same party or parties both before and after the combination, and that control is not transitory. Examples: merger of a wholly owned subsidiary into its parent; merger of fellow subsidiaries; transfer of a business from one group company to another; a promoter group merging two companies it controls (where control by a group of individuals under a contractual arrangement can qualify).

Pooling of interests method (Appendix C para 9):

  1. Assets and liabilities of the combining entities are reflected at their carrying amounts. Adjustments are made only to harmonise accounting policies.
  2. No new assets or liabilities are recognised: no goodwill, no new intangibles, no fair-value step-ups. The only goodwill shown is goodwill already in the transferor's books (if any).
  3. The financial information in the financial statements for prior periods is restated as if the combination had occurred from the beginning of the preceding period, irrespective of the actual date. If common control began later, restate from that date.
  4. Reserves: the identity of the transferor's reserves is preserved; they appear in the transferee's financial statements in the same form.
  5. The difference between the amount recorded as share capital issued plus any additional consideration (cash or other assets) and the amount of share capital of the transferor is transferred to capital reserve and presented separately from other capital reserves.

Illustration: parent P merges its 100% subsidiary S. P's investment in S is ₹500 crore. S's share capital ₹100 crore, other equity ₹650 crore. No new shares are issued (cross-holding cancelled).

  • S's assets and liabilities are added at carrying amounts (net ₹750 crore).
  • P's investment of ₹500 crore is cancelled against S's share capital of ₹100 crore; the difference of ₹400 crore is debited to capital reserve (a negative capital reserve, sometimes called amalgamation adjustment deficit).
  • S's other equity of ₹650 crore is carried over in its existing form.
  • Comparatives are restated.

Consolidated vs standalone: in P's consolidated statements, S was already consolidated, so the merger has no effect except reclassifications. The main effect is in P's standalone accounts. Under ICAI ITFG guidance, the standalone accounts after the merger reflect carrying amounts as they appeared in the consolidated financial statements of the parent (including any goodwill and PPA amounts from the original acquisition of S), not S's own standalone book values. This is a common error in practice.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Transitory control: a group that acquires a company and merges it with another subsidiary shortly after may be using common control to avoid fair-value accounting. Assess substance.
  • Promoter groups: control by a group of individuals (a family) qualifies only if they act together under a contractual arrangement. Family relationships alone may not be enough.
  • NCI in the transferor: in a merger of a 90% subsidiary into the parent, the 10% minority receives shares. Appendix C still applies, but share issuance to minority holders is part of the consideration.
  • Bargain purchase with weak evidence: if the reasons are not clear, the gain goes directly to capital reserve without OCI. Auditors will look for a fully reassessed PPA in either case.
  • Tax: neither a bargain gain nor a capital reserve under Appendix C is taxable income in itself, but waiver of debt in IBC cases and Sec 56 deemed income on share acquisitions below fair value need separate analysis.

Key takeaways

  • Before recognising a bargain purchase, reassess the identification and measurement of every item.
  • India carve-out: a bargain gain goes to OCI and capital reserve if there is clear evidence of the reasons, otherwise directly to capital reserve. It never hits P&L or EPS.
  • Appendix C applies when the same party controls the combining entities before and after, and control is not transitory.
  • Pooling: carrying amounts, no new goodwill or intangibles, restated comparatives, and the share capital difference to capital reserve.
  • In standalone accounts after merging a subsidiary, use the carrying amounts in the parent's consolidated accounts.

Practice Questions

Q1. Bargain purchase entries [L1]

Consideration ₹650 crore for 100%. FV of identifiable net assets ₹710 crore after DTL. The seller was a foreign parent exiting India under a global restructuring with a 3-month deadline. Pass the entry and state the presentation.

Q2. Reassessment [L2]

A provisional PPA shows a bargain purchase of ₹90 crore. On review: a contingent liability of ₹40 crore for a pending environmental claim was omitted; customer relationships of ₹120 crore were valued with a TAB that is not available in India (TAB portion ₹18 crore); and the DTL on a ₹200 crore land step-up was omitted (rate 14.95% for capital gains). Recompute the result at a 25.17% rate for other items.

💡 Hint: Each correction reduces net assets, and some may tip the result into goodwill.

Q3. Common-control merger [L2]

Holding H merges fellow subsidiary F (both 100% owned by ultimate parent U) on 1 January 2027 by issuing shares with a face value of ₹50 crore to U. F's share capital ₹80 crore, securities premium ₹120 crore, retained earnings ₹300 crore. Show the merged balance sheet treatment, the capital reserve and the restatement of FY26 comparatives.

Q4. Which method applies? [L3]

A listed company buys 100% of a company owned by its promoter family (the family holds 52% of the listed company and 100% of the target), paying ₹900 crore in cash. Fair value of the target's net assets is ₹600 crore; book value is ₹350 crore. Discuss whether Appendix C applies, the accounting under each possible answer, and the corporate governance and related-party approvals needed.
