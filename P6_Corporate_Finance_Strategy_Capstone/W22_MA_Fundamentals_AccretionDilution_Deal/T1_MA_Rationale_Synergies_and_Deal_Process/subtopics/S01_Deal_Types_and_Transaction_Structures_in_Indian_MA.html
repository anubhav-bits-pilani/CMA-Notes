Subtopic note

Deal Types and Transaction Structures in Indian M&A

Scope: Deal types: horizontal, vertical (backward/forward), concentric, conglomerate, acqui-hire; share purchase vs slump sale vs asset deal

Why it matters

The deal type tells you where the synergies should come from, which regulator will look hardest at the deal, and how the model should be built. A horizontal cement deal is a cost-synergy and CCI story. A vertical deal into a captive supplier is a margin and working-capital story. A conglomerate deal has almost no operating synergy, so it has to be justified on price alone. Separately, the legal structure (buying shares, buying a business as a going concern, or buying selected assets) decides stamp duty, capital gains for the seller, whether the buyer inherits tax losses and contingent liabilities, and whether the buyer gets a tax-deductible step-up. In due diligence and in the Sources & Uses table, the structure changes the numbers more than most analysts expect.

Core notes

Strategic deal types

Horizontal acquisition. Acquirer and target sell the same products in the same market, for example one cement maker buying another, or a listed paint company buying a regional peer. The rationale is scale: procurement leverage, plant and depot consolidation, removal of duplicate SG&A, and pricing discipline in an oligopolistic market. Horizontal deals raise the highest competition risk because market shares add up directly. CCI looks at overlaps in relevant product and geographic markets (for cement, typically regional markets).

Vertical acquisition. Acquirer and target sit at different stages of the same value chain.

  • Backward integration: buying a supplier, for example a steel producer buying an iron-ore or coking-coal asset, or an FMCG company buying a packaging unit. The benefits are input security, capture of the supplier's margin and lower working capital through tighter scheduling.
  • Forward integration: buying a distributor or customer-facing business, for example a manufacturer buying a distribution or retail chain. The benefits are control over the channel, pricing data and capture of the distributor margin.

The competition concern in vertical deals is foreclosure: the merged firm may deny inputs or channels to rivals. Modelling concern: intra-group sales after closing must be eliminated on consolidation (Ind AS 110), so combined revenue is lower than the simple sum. Unrealised profit in inventory must also be eliminated.

Concentric (congeneric) acquisition. The target is in a related but different business that shares technology, customers or distribution. An example is a two-wheeler maker buying an electric-battery company, or a bank buying an asset-management company. Synergies are mostly revenue synergies from cross-selling into a shared customer base, plus some shared R&D or distribution cost.

Conglomerate acquisition. The businesses are unrelated, as in a diversified Indian group buying into a new sector. Operating synergies are minimal. The usual arguments are diversification of cash flows, use of group cash or tax capacity, and access to group capital. Markets often apply a conglomerate discount, because shareholders can diversify themselves. An FP&A reviewer should push back on synergy claims in such deals.

Acqui-hire. The acquirer buys a small (often start-up) company mainly to get its team and know-how, not its revenue. Typical in technology and fintech. Accounting consequences are important:

  • An assembled workforce is not an identifiable intangible under Ind AS 103, so its value is included in goodwill.
  • If the "acquired" set does not meet the definition of a business (inputs plus substantive processes), the transaction is an asset acquisition, not a business combination. The optional concentration test in Ind AS 103 can lead to that conclusion.
  • Payments to founders that are forfeited if they leave are post-combination remuneration, not purchase consideration (Ind AS 103 B55). They hit the P&L over the service period, not goodwill.

A merger or amalgamation under Sec 230–232 via NCLT is a fourth route. It can be tax-neutral under Sec 47 and can carry forward losses under Sec 72A. That is covered in s06 and s07.

How the structure flows into the model

  1. Purchase price: in a share deal you buy equity value and then deal with target debt (refinance or assume). In a slump sale you usually buy a cash-free, debt-free business, so the price is close to enterprise value.
  2. PPA and DTL: in a share deal the tax base does not change, so fair-value uplifts create a DTL. In an asset deal or slump sale, tax base and book value move together for depreciable assets, so the DTL is small. The tax-amortisation benefit then has value.
  3. Inherited exposures: in a share deal, tax demands, litigation and labour liabilities sit inside the company. DD and indemnities (escrow, holdback, W&I insurance) are the protection.

Illustration

A listed acquirer is evaluating a ₹600 crore purchase of a packaging unit, a backward integration. The unit has net worth of ₹350 crore and tax WDV of plant of ₹300 crore. Identified fair value of plant is ₹420 crore and customer contracts are worth ₹60 crore. The tax rate is 25.17%.

  • Share deal: plant tax base stays at ₹300 crore. Temporary differences are ₹120 crore on plant plus ₹60 crore on contracts, giving a DTL of about ₹45.3 crore that reduces net assets and increases goodwill. No extra tax depreciation.
  • Slump sale: the buyer allocates ₹600 crore across assets. Plant at ₹420 crore attracts tax depreciation on the full amount. Goodwill gets no tax depreciation. The seller pays Sec 50B tax on ₹600 crore − ₹350 crore.

The seller will usually prefer the share deal (often lower tax at shareholder level and no stamp duty on property), while the buyer values the step-up. The price negotiation shares out that difference.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Business vs asset acquisition: a "business" in a slump sale for tax purposes is not the same test as a "business" under Ind AS 103 Appendix B. A slump sale of a single licence plus land may be an asset acquisition in the books.
  • Vertical deals with inter-company trade: double-counting revenue is the classic model error. Model eliminations explicitly.
  • Acqui-hire with retention payments: split founder consideration between purchase price and remuneration. Get it wrong and goodwill and post-deal EPS are both misstated.
  • Hybrid structures: buying shares from promoters and then merging the target into the acquirer combines a SAST open offer with an NCLT scheme. Timelines and tax analysis must cover both legs.
  • Sec 79 trap: a share purchase of an unlisted, closely held loss-making target can wipe out its carried-forward business losses if more than 49% of voting power changes hands. IBC cases and some start-up cases are exceptions.

Key takeaways

  • Strategic type (horizontal, vertical, concentric, conglomerate, acqui-hire) tells you which synergies are credible and which regulator matters most.
  • Horizontal deals mean cost synergies and the highest CCI scrutiny. Vertical deals raise foreclosure risk and require elimination of inter-company trade.
  • An acqui-hire puts workforce value into goodwill, and retention-linked payments to founders are remuneration.
  • Share purchase: no tax step-up, liabilities inherited, low stamp duty, losses subject to Sec 79.
  • Slump sale: Sec 50B for the seller, step-up for the buyer, high stamp duty, GST exemption for a going concern.
  • Structure drives DTL, PPA amortisation and the Sources & Uses, so fix it before building the model.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classifying deal types [L1]

Classify each of the following and name the dominant synergy type: (a) a listed tyre maker buys a carbon-black producer; (b) a private bank buys an insurance broker; (c) a cement company buys a competitor's two grinding units in the same state; (d) an infrastructure group buys a hospital chain; (e) a SaaS company buys a 12-person AI start-up with negligible revenue.

Q2. Share deal vs slump sale tax comparison [L2]

Target undertaking: net worth ₹240 crore, FMV under Rule 11UAE ₹500 crore, held for 8 years. Tax WDV of plant ₹180 crore; fair value of plant ₹310 crore; brand fair value ₹70 crore. Shareholders' cost of acquisition of the shares ₹90 crore; agreed equity price ₹500 crore. Assume an LTCG rate of 12.5% (ignore surcharge and cess) and a corporate rate of 25.17% for the buyer. Compute (a) the seller-side tax in a share sale vs a slump sale and (b) the DTL the buyer recognises in each structure. Comment on which side gains from each structure.

💡 Hint: In the slump sale, the brand's tax treatment depends on whether it is a depreciable intangible or goodwill.

Q3. Acqui-hire accounting [L2]

An acquirer pays ₹45 crore for a start-up: ₹30 crore at closing and ₹15 crore to the two founders after two years, forfeited if either leaves. The start-up's identifiable net assets have a fair value of ₹4 crore. Explain the Ind AS 103 treatment of each payment and compute goodwill at acquisition.

Q4. Vertical deal consolidation in the model [L2]

The acquirer (revenue ₹2,000 crore) buys its main supplier (revenue ₹600 crore, of which ₹250 crore is sold to the acquirer). At year-end the acquirer holds ₹40 crore of inventory bought from the supplier at a 20% margin on cost. Show how combined revenue and the unrealised profit adjustment should appear in the pro-forma P&L.

Q5. Advising on structure [L3]

A promoter wants to sell a profitable division (not a separate company) of an unlisted company to a listed acquirer. Write a one-page note comparing slump sale and demerger-plus-share-sale on tax for seller and buyer, stamp duty, GST, contracts and employees, and timeline. Recommend a structure and state the assumptions.
