Roadmap topic: M&A Strategic Rationale & Deal Process: Horizontal vs vertical acquisitions, synergy identification (revenue vs cost synergies), and due diligence focus areas

Overview

Every M&A model starts from a strategic hypothesis: why is this combination worth more than the two companies apart? The deal type (horizontal, vertical, concentric, conglomerate or acqui-hire) tells you where the extra value should come from. Synergy analysis turns that hypothesis into rupees: cost synergies from procurement, plant consolidation and SG&A; revenue synergies from cross-selling, pricing and distribution; and financial synergies from tax losses and cheaper capital. These are then phased, risked, and reduced by costs to achieve and dis-synergies. The net synergy value sets the ceiling on what the acquirer should pay.

The deal process (teaser, NDA, CIM, non-binding offer, VDR, confirmatory DD, binding offer, SPA, conditions precedent and closing) decides when information becomes available and when numbers become binding. Due diligence turns reported numbers into price inputs: normalised EBITDA, net debt including debt-like items, and the NWC peg. In India, the process also runs through a regulatory map: CCI merger control (including the ₹2,000 crore deal value threshold since September 2024), SEBI SAST open offers for listed targets, NCLT schemes under Sec 230–232, FEMA pricing for cross-border deals, and tax structuring under Sec 47, 72A and 50B. Distressed deals through IBC resolution plans and intra-group fast-track mergers under Sec 233 are alternative routes.

For FP&A and valuation roles, this topic is what makes the later modelling credible. A Sources & Uses table (t2) needs to know whether a 26% open offer must be funded. A PPA (t3) depends on whether the deal is a share purchase or a slump sale. An accretion/dilution model (t4) and break-even analysis (t5) are only as good as the synergy schedule behind them. A good sequence for study is: deal types and structures (s01), synergy taxonomy (s02) and phasing (s03), then value logic (c01, c02); next the process (s04) and DD (s05, c06); and finally the Indian regulatory and tax layers (s06, s07, s08 with c03, c04, c05).

Related concepts to study

Forensic analysis and peer benchmarking in DD

→ Week 5–8: forensic analysis and peer benchmarking feed commercial and financial DD

The ratio analysis, cash-flow quality tests (CFO vs EBITDA conversion, accrual ratios), related-party scrutiny and Beneish-style red flags from Weeks 5–8 are the toolkit of financial DD. Peer benchmarking gives the commercial DD baseline: if the target's gross margin is 6 points above peers, the QoE work should explain why (pricing power, product mix or aggressive capitalisation). Benchmarking also sizes synergies: the gap between the target's cost ratios and the acquirer's best-in-class ratios is the starting point for procurement and SG&A synergy estimates. Use the same screens on the acquirer's own numbers to check that the standalone base is clean.

DCF and comps for target valuation

→ Week 16: DCF and trading/transaction comps for target valuation

The standalone value \(V_B\) in the maximum-price test (c02) comes from the Week 16 toolkit: an unlevered DCF on the target's standalone plan (without synergies), trading comps on consistent EBITDA definitions, and precedent transaction multiples that already include control premiums. Synergies are valued separately (c01) so that the board can see how much of the price depends on them. Precedent transaction multiples should not be added to synergy value, because those multiples already reflect synergies paid away in past deals. A football field chart that shows standalone DCF, comps, precedents, and standalone plus synergies is the standard presentation.

Delisting and minimum public shareholding after open offers

SEBI Delisting Regulations 2021 and minimum public shareholding (SCRR Rule 19A, 25%) after open offers

If an open offer takes the acquirer above 75%, public shareholding falls below the 25% minimum required by SCRR Rule 19A. The company must restore it within 12 months through an offer for sale, QIP or other permitted methods. Alternatively, the acquirer can delist under the SEBI (Delisting of Equity Shares) Regulations 2021, which require the promoter holding to reach 90% after the offer and use a reverse book-building or (after the 2024 amendments) a fixed-price route, with a floor price and counter-offer mechanism. SAST Reg 5A lets the acquirer announce a delisting intention with the open offer. These choices change the funding requirement and the NCI assumption in the model.

Common-control combinations (Ind AS 103 Appendix C)

Ind AS 103 Appendix C common-control combinations (group restructurings via schemes)

Many Indian schemes are group restructurings: merging a subsidiary into its parent, or moving businesses between fellow subsidiaries. If the combining entities are controlled by the same party before and after the transaction, and that control is not transitory, Appendix C applies. Assets and liabilities are carried at their existing carrying amounts, no new goodwill or intangibles are recognised, and the difference between consideration and the transferor's share capital goes to capital reserve. Prior periods are restated as if the combination happened at the start of the earliest period presented (or the date common control began, if later). So a scheme's accounting treatment, which the auditor must certify (c05), depends on whether common control exists.

Post-merger integration and synergy tracking

Post-merger integration (PMI) and synergy tracking in MIS (→ Week 20)

Deal value is realised after closing. An integration management office runs workstreams (procurement, manufacturing footprint, sales, IT, HR) with initiative-level synergy targets that come from the s03 schedule. FP&A builds a synergy tracker in the MIS (Week 20): planned vs actual synergies by initiative and quarter, costs to achieve against budget, dis-synergies, and the bridge from synergies to reported EBITDA. Power BI dashboards with drill-down by initiative owner are typical. The tracker also supports goodwill impairment testing (Ind AS 36), because recoverable amounts for the CGUs carrying acquisition goodwill depend on synergy delivery.

Common pitfalls

Counting revenue synergies at full value

What happens: the deal case includes revenue synergies at their full run-rate from Year 1 or 2, without probability weights or realistic ramp-up. Why: revenue synergies make the strategic story attractive, and management decks present them as certain. Correct treatment: convert revenue to EBITDA at contribution margin, net of selling costs and NWC, phase over 3–5 years, and weight by probability (often 25–50%). Many acquirers keep them out of the base case entirely. Detection: check the share of total synergies coming from revenue, the Year 1 ramp, and whether customer-level evidence exists.

Ignoring integration costs and dis-synergies

What happens: run-rate synergies are valued, but costs to achieve, retention bonuses, customer attrition and lost incentives are omitted. Why: CTA is classified as "one-off" and excluded from adjusted EPS, so it gets forgotten in the value test as well. Correct treatment: include CTA (typically 1.0–1.5x run-rate cost synergies) and dis-synergies in the net synergy schedule (s03) and in the NPV (c01). Detection: look for a negative or low Year 1 net synergy line. If Year 1 is strongly positive, CTA is probably missing.

Missing the SAST open-offer obligation

What happens: the model funds only the SPA stake in a listed target, ignoring the mandatory 26% open offer and the escrow. Why: analysts used to private deals forget the listed-company rules, or assume low acceptance. Correct treatment: test the Reg 3 and Reg 4 triggers, compute the Reg 8 price, fund the full 26% (with acceptance scenarios for ownership and NCI), and fund the Reg 17 escrow before the detailed public statement. Detection: if the target is listed and the stake crosses 25% or gives control, Sources & Uses must show an open-offer line.

Assuming Sec 72A carry-forward applies

What happens: the synergy case includes the full tax value of the target's losses after an amalgamation without checking eligibility. Why: Sec 72A is seen as automatic for any merger. Correct treatment: check that the target owns an eligible undertaking (industrial undertaking, ship, hotel, bank or other specified cases), meets the 3-year business and 3/4 asset-holding tests, and that the integration plan allows 5-year continuity, 3/4 asset retention and 50% capacity utilisation. In a share deal, test Sec 79 instead. Detection: ask for the tax DD memo on Sec 72A. If plant closures are planned, check them against the asset-retention condition.

Practice Questions

Q1. Horizontal cement acquisition: synergy case and value test [L3]

A listed cement company (acquirer) plans to buy 100% of an unlisted regional peer. Target standalone DCF equity value ₹3,200 crore, net debt ₹800 crore. Proposed EV ₹4,600 crore. Synergies: procurement run-rate ₹70 crore (phasing 50% / 100%), freight and depot ₹60 crore (30% / 70% / 100%), SG&A ₹40 crore (60% / 100%), revenue synergy EBITDA ₹50 crore (0% / 30% / 60% / 100%, probability 40%). Dis-synergy: ₹10 crore a year of lost volume. CTA: ₹120 crore in Year 1, ₹60 crore in Year 2. Tax 25.17%, discount rate 11%, perpetual growth 2% after Year 4. Transaction costs ₹45 crore. (a) Build the net synergy schedule. (b) Compute synergy NPV. (c) Compute the premium over standalone value and the acquirer NPV. (d) State which synergies you would challenge and why.

Q2. Listed target: process, regulation and funding [L3]

An NSE-listed acquirer signs an SPA on 1 July 2026 to buy the promoter's 40% of a listed peer (15 crore shares) at ₹410 per share. The acquirer bought 3% of the target in the market at ₹395 four months earlier. The 60-day VWAP is ₹402. Combined Indian turnover exceeds ₹30,000 crore and the target's Indian turnover is ₹2,100 crore. (a) Identify all regulatory approvals and their sequence. (b) Compute the open-offer price, size, maximum consideration and escrow. (c) Compute the acquirer's holding at 0%, 50% and 100% acceptance and state the MPS or delisting consequence. (d) List the CPs for the SPA.

Q3. DD findings to price [L2]

Target headline EV is ₹1,500 crore at 10x reported EBITDA of ₹150 crore (post-Ind AS 116). DD finds: non-recurring gain ₹9 crore in EBITDA; promoter rent normalisation ₹4 crore; channel stuffing margin ₹5 crore; lease liabilities ₹60 crore not in the CIM net debt; unfunded gratuity ₹12 crore; financial net debt ₹300 crore; NWC peg ₹180 crore vs expected closing NWC ₹165 crore; a GST demand of ₹40 crore under appeal. (a) Compute adjusted EBITDA and the revised EV at the same multiple. (b) Compute the equity price. (c) Recommend the SPA treatment of the GST demand.

Q4. Merger structuring with tax losses [L3]

A profitable listed specialty-chemicals company is considering merging a group company (90% owned, unlisted, manufacturing) with ₹280 crore of business losses and ₹150 crore of unabsorbed depreciation. The integration plan would sell 30% of the target's fixed assets (by book value) in Year 2. (a) Choose between Sec 230–232 and Sec 233 and explain the approvals. (b) Assess Sec 47 and Sec 72A for the merger, including the effect of the planned asset sale. (c) Explain whether Ind AS 103 Appendix C applies and the effect on goodwill. (d) Quantify the tax-loss synergy at 25.17% if the losses can be absorbed over 3 years, with and without Sec 72A.
