Subtopic note

Acquisition Finance in India: RBI Rules, NBFCs, NCDs and ECBs

Scope: Acquisition finance in India: historical RBI curbs on bank funding of share acquisitions, 2025 RBI enabling framework, NBFC/NCD/ECB alternatives

Why it matters

In the US or Europe, leveraged acquisition loans from banks are routine. In India, for decades, banks were largely barred from financing the purchase of shares of Indian companies. Indian acquirers therefore used internal cash, NBFC loans, privately placed NCDs, structured holding-company debt or overseas borrowing for overseas targets. In 2025 the RBI moved to allow banks to finance acquisitions within a prudential framework. The source of debt affects its cost, tenor, security, covenants and documentation, and the cost of debt in the model drives EPS accretion and break-even synergies.

Core notes

Historical RBI restrictions

RBI's directions on loans and advances (statutory and other restrictions) and its capital-market exposure norms generally did not allow banks to grant advances to companies for buying shares of other companies, including for acquisitions. The concerns were speculative leverage, concentration risk and connected lending. Exceptions and workarounds:

  • Overseas acquisitions: banks could fund Indian companies' equity contributions to overseas JVs and wholly owned subsidiaries, and Indian banks' overseas branches could lend to overseas SPVs (subject to limits).
  • Capital-market exposure: limited lending against shares, IPO financing and promoter funding within capital market exposure caps, with margins.
  • Refinancing: banks could refinance acquisition debt in some cases (for example after a merger into the operating company) where the loan became an ordinary corporate loan.
  • Infrastructure and PSU disinvestment: specific relaxations for strategic disinvestment or infrastructure takeovers at times.

As a result, Indian acquisition debt was often raised as NCDs or NBFC loans by a holding company, secured by share pledges and promoter guarantees, at higher rates than bank loans.

The 2025 enabling framework

In its October 2025 policy, the RBI announced that banks would be allowed to finance acquisitions by Indian corporates, and issued draft directions (amending the credit-facility and capital-market exposure directions) for consultation. Key features as proposed (verify the final notified directions and their effective date before relying on any number):

  • Purpose: strategic acquisitions that give the acquirer control of the target, not financial (portfolio) investments.
  • Eligible acquirer: a listed Indian company (or its SPV) with satisfactory net worth and a record of profitability. The target can be listed or unlisted.
  • Bank share of funding: bank finance capped at around 70% of the acquisition value, with the acquirer funding at least 30% from its own equity.
  • Leverage cap: post-acquisition consolidated debt-to-equity of the acquirer not to exceed about 3:1.
  • Bank-level limit: acquisition finance exposure capped at around 10% of the bank's Tier 1 capital, within overall capital-market exposure limits.
  • Security and valuation: shares of the target pledged; valuation by independent valuers; board-approved credit policies.

For modelling, this means bank debt at lower spreads can now be a source, but it comes with an equity contribution requirement (cash or shares) and leverage tests that interact with covenants (Week 21 t5).

NBFC funding

NBFCs (including those in the upper layer under scale-based regulation) can lend for acquisitions. Features:

  • Higher rates than banks (often 150–400 bps higher), shorter tenors (2–5 years), bullet repayments.
  • Share pledge security with margin calls tied to the target's share price (a risk for listed targets).
  • Covenants on loan-to-value, promoter guarantees and corporate guarantees.
  • Often refinanced through bonds or bank loans once the deal closes.

NCDs

  • Listed NCDs under the SEBI NCS Regulations 2021: need a credit rating, debenture trustee, security cover and continuous disclosure. Large listed entities are required to raise a portion of incremental borrowings through debt securities.
  • Privately placed NCDs to mutual funds, insurers, AIFs and private credit funds: common for HoldCo acquisition finance. Structured with coupon plus redemption premium (to give an IRR to the investor), share pledges and cash sweeps.
  • Mutual fund limits and sectoral exposure limits restrict demand for lower-rated paper.
  • Accounting: issue costs and redemption premium are amortised through the EIR (Ind AS 109).

ECBs

  • Under the ECB framework (Master Direction on External Commercial Borrowings), eligible borrowers can raise foreign-currency or INR-denominated loans and bonds from recognised lenders within all-in-cost ceilings and minimum average maturities.
  • The end-use negative list has historically included equity investment in domestic companies and investment in capital markets. So ECBs could not fund the purchase of shares of Indian targets, except where specifically permitted (for example certain cases involving acquisition of shares of PSUs in disinvestment).
  • ECBs are commonly used for overseas acquisitions through overseas direct investment.
  • The RBI released draft amendments to the ECB framework in October 2025 to liberalise borrowing limits, cost ceilings and end-uses. Check the final rules for whether acquisitions of Indian companies are now permitted.
  • Model the hedging cost (forward premium, often 2–4% a year for USD/INR) and withholding tax on interest (which may be grossed up by the borrower). An unhedged foreign-currency loan adds FX risk to the debt schedule.

Structuring patterns

Pattern How it works Pros Cons
On-balance-sheet debt at acquirer Acquirer borrows and pays Simple, cheapest if rated Uses acquirer's covenant headroom
HoldCo / SPV debt SPV borrows (NCDs, NBFC), buys target shares Ring-fenced, non-recourse to acquirer Structural subordination, higher cost
Debt pushdown via merger SPV merges into target after closing Interest offset against target profits Needs scheme approval; tax scrutiny
Bridge-to-bond Short bridge loan refinanced by NCDs Speed and certainty Refinancing risk

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Share-price-linked margin calls: NBFC loans against listed target shares can trigger top-ups or forced sales if the share price falls, which is a risk in the downside case.
  • Interest deductibility: interest on money borrowed to buy shares is deductible mainly against income from those shares. Since dividends became taxable in shareholders' hands from FY 2020-21, Sec 57 caps the interest deduction against dividend income at 20% of the dividend (check later amendments). An acquirer holding company with little dividend income therefore gets little tax shield. Debt pushdown aims to move the interest to where operating profits are.
  • Thin capitalisation: Sec 94B limits interest deductions on debt from non-resident associated enterprises to 30% of EBITDA.
  • Regulatory uncertainty: the 2025 bank framework is new. Credit policies and final parameters may differ from the draft. State the source and date of each assumption in the model.
  • Covenant interaction: SAST open-offer funding is needed before the acquirer knows the acceptance level, so committed facilities may be larger than drawn debt. Commitment fees apply on undrawn amounts.

Key takeaways

  • Banks historically could not lend for buying shares of Indian companies, so acquisition debt came from NBFCs, NCDs and holding-company structures.
  • The 2025 RBI framework (draft October 2025) allows bank acquisition finance for control deals, with an equity contribution, leverage limits and bank exposure caps. Verify final parameters.
  • NBFC loans are faster but more expensive, with share-pledge margin risk.
  • NCDs (listed or private) are the main bond route. ECBs have end-use restrictions for domestic acquisitions and carry hedging costs.
  • Model each tranche's rate, fees, amortisation and tax deductibility separately.

Practice Questions

Q1. Funding mix under the bank framework [L2]

Acquisition value ₹5,000 crore. Assume the framework allows bank funding of up to 70% and post-deal consolidated debt/equity of up to 3:1. The acquirer's consolidated equity is ₹3,200 crore and existing debt ₹4,100 crore. Compute the maximum bank loan and check the leverage cap. If the cap binds, what is the maximum bank funding?

💡 Hint: Test both constraints and take the lower; equity is unchanged by a debt-funded deal except for fees expensed.

Q2. Cost of debt by source [L2]

Compare the all-in annual cost (pre-tax and after-tax at 25.17%) of: (a) a bank term loan at 8.6% with a 1% upfront fee, 5-year tenor; (b) an NBFC loan at 11.25% with a 1.5% fee, 3-year bullet; (c) a USD ECB at SOFR + 200 bps (SOFR 4.3%) with a hedging cost of 3% and 5% withholding tax grossed up, 5-year tenor. Use straight-line fee amortisation for approximation.

Q3. Margin call risk [L2]

An SPV borrows ₹1,200 crore from an NBFC against a pledge of 6 crore listed target shares at ₹500 (LTV 40%). The LTV covenant requires top-up if LTV exceeds 50%. At what share price is a margin call triggered? How many additional shares or how much cash is needed at a price of ₹350?

Q4. Structuring memo [L3]

An unlisted Indian PE-backed company wants to buy a listed company (including a SAST open offer) for ₹3,500 crore. Bank finance under the 2025 framework requires a listed acquirer. Propose two alternative debt structures, with the security package, expected cost, regulatory constraints and the exit or refinancing plan.
