Key concept

Minimum Cash and Excess Cash Available to Fund a Deal

Concept: Minimum cash = operating cash needed post-close; excess cash used = acquirer cash − minimum cash

Statement

\[ C_{\text{excess}} = \max\left(0, \ C_{\text{acq}} + C_{\text{tgt, accessible}} - C_{\min}\right) \]

\[ C_{\text{used}} \leq C_{\text{excess}} \]

where \(C_{\text{acq}}\) is the acquirer's cash and liquid investments at closing, \(C_{\text{tgt, accessible}}\) is target cash that the acquirer can actually use (zero in a cash-free, debt-free deal, and excluding trapped cash), and \(C_{\min}\) is the minimum operating cash for the combined group. \(C_{\min}\) is often set as:

\[ C_{\min} = \text{Days of operating outflows} \times \frac{\text{Annual cash opex}}{365} + \text{Regulatory or covenant balances} \]

or as a percentage of revenue, or as a fixed amount set by treasury policy.

Explanation

A company cannot spend all its cash on an acquisition. It needs cash to pay suppliers and salaries through the working-capital cycle, to meet seasonal peaks, to service debt on due dates and to hold balances that lenders or regulators require (DSRA, margin money, unpaid dividend accounts). Treasury policies often express this as "X days of cash costs" or as a revolving facility headroom.

Why it matters in the model:

  • Sources: only excess cash is a source. Overstating it understates the debt plug, interest and leverage.
  • EPS: cash used costs foregone interest after tax (t4 c03). Keeping a higher minimum increases debt and interest. The trade-off is small in EPS terms but matters for liquidity.
  • Net debt: using cash increases net debt by the same amount as borrowing. For leverage covenants, funding with cash and funding with debt have the same effect on Net Debt/EBITDA.
  • Excluded cash: margin money, escrow (including the SAST Reg 17 escrow until release), unclaimed dividend accounts and cash in overseas subsidiaries with repatriation costs or restrictions.
  • Target cash: in a share deal, target cash belongs to the acquired company. It can reduce net debt acquired or be used after closing, but the minimum for the target's own operations must stay.

Illustration

Acquirer cash and liquid funds ₹2,600 crore, of which ₹150 crore is margin money and ₹50 crore is in a DSRA. Combined annual cash opex ₹14,600 crore; policy is 30 days of cash opex. Target cash ₹300 crore, of which the target needs ₹120 crore for operations; the deal is a share purchase.

  • Usable acquirer cash = 2,600 − 150 − 50 = ₹2,400 crore.
  • \(C_{\min}\) = 30 × 14,600 / 365 = ₹1,200 crore for the combined group.
  • Target cash is accessible after closing (share purchase, no trapped cash). Because \(C_{\min}\) is set for the combined group, it already includes the ₹120 crore the target needs, so all ₹300 crore counts towards the group position.
  • Excess cash = 2,400 + 300 − 1,200 = ₹1,500 crore.

The deal team's draft used all ₹2,600 crore of acquirer cash. The ₹1,100 crore difference must be funded with debt or equity. Note also that target cash can be used only after closing (for example through an inter-company loan), so it cannot pay the seller on the closing date unless the funds-flow mechanics allow it.

Practice Questions

Q1. Excess cash [L1]

Acquirer cash ₹950 crore (including ₹60 crore unclaimed dividend account); policy minimum 25 days of cash opex of ₹7,300 crore a year. No target cash is accessible. Compute excess cash available for the deal.

Q2. Cash vs debt funding of the same deal [L2]

A ₹1,000 crore deal can be funded with excess cash yielding 6.5% or with debt at 8.75%. Tax 25.17%. Compute the after-tax EPS cost of each (acquirer has 40 crore shares) and the effect on Net Debt/EBITDA (EBITDA ₹1,400 crore, existing net debt ₹800 crore).

💡 Hint: Net debt rises by the same amount whichever source is used.

Q3. Setting a minimum cash policy [L3]

The combined group is seasonal: monthly cash outflows range from ₹600 crore to ₹1,100 crore, with an undrawn working-capital facility of ₹500 crore. Propose a minimum cash level for the deal model, with reasons, and explain how the SAST escrow and debt service dates should affect it.
