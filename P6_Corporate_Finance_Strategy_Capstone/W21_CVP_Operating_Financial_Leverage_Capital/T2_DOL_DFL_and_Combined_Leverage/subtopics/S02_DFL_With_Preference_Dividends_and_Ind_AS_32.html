DFL With Preference Dividends and Ind AS 32 Classification

Scope: DFL with preference dividends grossed up for tax; Ind AS 32 classification of redeemable preference shares as debt

Why it matters

The degree of financial leverage (DFL) measures how fixed financing charges magnify EBIT changes into EPS changes. Indian companies, especially promoter-funded mid-caps, NBFCs and project SPVs, often use preference shares: cumulative redeemable preference shares (CRPS), optionally convertible preference shares (OCPS) or compulsorily convertible preference shares (CCPS). The way these instruments enter DFL depends on two things. Preference dividend is paid out of post-tax profit, so it must be grossed up to a pre-tax equivalent. And under Ind AS 32, a redeemable preference share is usually a financial liability, so its "dividend" is already in finance costs. Getting either wrong misstates DFL, EPS sensitivity and the financial break-even.

Core notes

DFL definition

\[ \text{DFL} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{EPS}}{\%\Delta \text{EBIT}} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{EBIT} - I - \dfrac{D_p}{1 - t}} \]

where \(I\) is interest (finance cost) on debt, \(D_p\) is the preference dividend paid out of profit (that is, on preference shares classified as equity), and \(t\) is the tax rate. EPS \(= [(\text{EBIT} - I)(1 - t) - D_p] \div N\), where \(N\) is the number of equity shares.

Why gross up the preference dividend

Interest is tax-deductible; equity-classified preference dividend is not. To put both on the same pre-tax footing, convert the preference dividend into the pre-tax profit needed to pay it: \(D_p \div (1 - t)\). Example: ₹7.5 crore of preference dividend at \(t = 25.168\%\) needs ₹10.02 crore of PBT. Forgetting the gross-up understates the fixed financial charge and so understates DFL (topic pitfall 2).

The derivation: EPS \(= [(\text{EBIT} - I)(1 - t) - D_p] \div N = (1 - t)[\text{EBIT} - I - D_p \div (1 - t)] \div N\). A change in EBIT changes EPS by \((1 - t)\Delta\text{EBIT} \div N\). Dividing proportional changes gives the DFL formula.

Ind AS 32 classification

Ind AS 32 classifies an instrument by substance, not legal form. A financial liability exists if the issuer has a contractual obligation to deliver cash that it cannot avoid.

Instrument Typical classification Effect on DFL computation
Redeemable preference shares with mandatory redemption on a fixed date, or redeemable at holder's option Financial liability (debt) "Dividend" is recognised as interest expense under the effective interest method (Ind AS 109); include it in \(I\), with no gross-up
Non-redeemable, non-cumulative, dividends discretionary Equity Dividend is a distribution; include as \(D_p \div (1 - t)\)
Cumulative but redeemable only at issuer's option, dividends discretionary Generally equity (judgement on substance) Gross up
CCPS converting into a fixed number of equity shares, dividends discretionary Equity (fixed-for-fixed) Gross up any dividend; dilution in diluted EPS
Convertible with fixed coupon plus fixed-for-fixed conversion Compound: liability component (PV of coupons/redemption) plus equity component Liability unwinding in \(I\); equity component outside DFL
Preference shares convertible into a variable number of shares Financial liability Include in \(I\)

Classification is judgement-heavy and depends on terms such as dividend-pusher clauses, step-ups and the holder's put options. Read the instrument terms and the company's accounting-policy note. For tax purposes, the treatment can differ. Dividends on preference shares are not deductible under the Income-tax Act even if Ind AS presents them as interest. The Ind AS finance cost therefore includes a non-deductible element, and the tax shield applies only to genuine interest.

Practical consequence for analysts

When computing DFL from Ind AS financial statements:

  1. Take finance costs from the P&L (they already include the liability-classified preference "dividend" and the unwinding of lease liabilities under Ind AS 116).
  2. Identify equity-classified preference dividends from the statement of changes in equity or the dividend note, and gross them up.
  3. Adjust the effective tax rate if a significant part of finance costs is non-deductible. A strictly correct pre-tax equivalent then treats the non-deductible part like a grossed-up dividend.

Illustration

A Mumbai specialty-chemicals company: EBIT ₹120 crore, bank interest ₹24 crore, 10 lakh 9% redeemable preference shares of ₹100 each (₹10 crore, mandatorily redeemable in 2029, classified as liability; dividend ₹0.9 crore in finance cost), and ₹50 crore of 8% non-redeemable non-cumulative preference shares (equity; dividend ₹4 crore). Tax rate 25.168%. Equity shares: 4 crore.

Item ₹ crore
EBIT 120.00
Finance cost (24 + 0.9) 24.90
Grossed-up equity preference dividend: 4 ÷ 0.74832 5.35
Denominator: \(\text{EBIT} - I - D_p \div (1 - t)\) 89.75
DFL 1.337

Without the gross-up, the denominator would be ₹91.10 crore and DFL 1.317. Treating the redeemable preference shares as equity (grossing up the ₹0.9 crore) would also be wrong, though small here.

EPS (simplified, ignoring the non-deductibility of the ₹0.9 crore for tax) \(= [(120 - 24.9) \times 0.74832 - 4] \div 4 = \text{₹}16.79\).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Non-deductible "interest": when liability-classified preference dividend is non-deductible for tax, the tax charge is higher than \(t \times\) PBT. Use the actual tax computation for EPS, and in DFL treat that element as grossed up for precision.
  • Cumulative arrears: for cumulative equity-classified preference shares, Ind AS 33 deducts the dividend for the period whether or not declared. Include it in \(D_p\).
  • Capitalised interest (Ind AS 23) is not in finance cost during construction, so DFL looks lower than the economic financial burden. Note it when the company is mid-capex.
  • Ind AS 116 lease interest sits in finance costs and raises DFL relative to pre-2019 figures (s06).
  • Losses: if \(t\) is effectively nil (losses carried forward), the gross-up factor is 1 in the short run.

Key takeaways

  • DFL \(= \text{EBIT} \div [\text{EBIT} - I - D_p \div (1 - t)]\); the gross-up converts a post-tax charge to pre-tax.
  • Under Ind AS 32, mandatorily redeemable preference shares are usually debt, so their dividend is interest in \(I\) and is not grossed up.
  • Equity-classified preference dividends (non-redeemable, discretionary) are grossed up.
  • Read the instrument terms: classification turns on unavoidable cash obligations and fixed-for-fixed conversion.
  • Accounting presentation and tax deductibility can differ, so check both.

Practice Questions

Q1. DFL with preference dividend [L1]

A company has EBIT of ₹80 crore, interest of ₹18 crore and equity-classified preference dividend of ₹6 crore, with a tax rate of 25.168%. Compute DFL with and without the gross-up, and the percentage understatement from omitting it.

Q2. Classification judgement [L2]

A company issues 10% cumulative preference shares redeemable at the holder's option after five years, with dividends payable only if declared. Classify the instrument under Ind AS 32, explain the effect on the P&L and on DFL, and state how the treatment changes if redemption is solely at the issuer's option.

💡 Hint: Ask whether the issuer can avoid delivering cash on redemption.

Q3. Recomputing DFL from an annual report [L2]

An annual report shows EBIT of ₹340 crore and finance costs of ₹92 crore, which include ₹14 crore of lease interest and ₹8 crore of dividend on redeemable preference shares. The statement of changes in equity shows ₹10 crore of dividend on CCPS. Tax rate is 25.168%. Compute DFL and list the adjustments you made.

Q4. EPS with non-deductible finance cost [L3]

Continuing Q3, the ₹8 crore preference dividend is not deductible under the Income-tax Act. Equity shares are 20 crore. Compute PBT, tax, PAT and basic EPS, and explain how you would treat the ₹8 crore in a precise DFL calculation.

Q5. Interview question [L1]

"Why do we gross up preference dividend but not interest in the DFL formula?" Answer in three sentences.
