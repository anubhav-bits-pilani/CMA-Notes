Subtopic note

Leverage Profiles: Capital-Intensive vs Asset-Light Businesses

Scope: Capital-intensive (cement, steel, telecom, power) vs asset-light (IT services, FMCG with contract manufacturing) leverage profiles

Why it matters

The roadmap topic asks you to quantify EPS volatility in capital-intensive versus asset-light businesses. The difference is structural. Capital-intensive businesses carry large fixed operating costs (depreciation, plant manpower, fixed power charges) and usually fund their assets with debt. They therefore stack high DOL on top of high DFL. Asset-light businesses have variable cost structures and little debt, so earnings track revenue closely. These profiles drive sector P/E multiples, rating outcomes, covenant design and the debt capacity of projects. They also inform how you build sensitivity cases in valuation and FP&A models for each sector.

Core notes

What drives each leverage profile

Dimension Capital-intensive Asset-light
Fixed-asset intensity (gross block ÷ revenue) High: often 1.0–3.0x (power, telecom), 0.8–1.5x (cement, steel) Low: 0.1–0.3x (IT services, contract-manufactured FMCG)
Main fixed operating costs Depreciation, plant salaries, fixed power and maintenance, spectrum amortisation Salaries (semi-fixed in IT), brand spend, rent (now ROU)
Main variable costs Fuel, raw materials, freight Contract manufacturing fees, materials, subcontractors, variable pay
Price behaviour Commodity pricing; realisation swings Contracted or brand-supported pricing
Typical DOL at mid-cycle 2.5–5x 1.2–2x
Funding Term loans, NCDs, ECBs; project finance Equity, internal accruals; often net cash
Typical DFL 1.3–2.5x (much higher in stressed cases) ≈1.0x
EPS volatility High; losses in down-cycles Low; tracks revenue

The ranges are indicative and move with the cycle. Always compute from the company's own cost structure.

Sector notes (Indian context)

  • Cement: high fixed costs (depreciation on kilns and grinding units, fixed manpower), with variable power, fuel (petcoke, coal) and freight. Regional price discipline matters. A ₹200 per tonne realisation change on a base EBITDA of ₹1,000 per tonne moves EBITDA by 20%. Leverage rises during capacity-addition phases.
  • Steel: integrated plants have very high fixed costs and commodity-linked realisation and input costs (iron ore, coking coal), so EBITDA per tonne can swing several-fold over the cycle. DFL is high for groups that expanded with debt.
  • Telecom: network and spectrum costs are almost entirely fixed. Incremental subscribers or ARPU drop to EBITDA at very high margins. Debt includes deferred spectrum liabilities and lease liabilities (towers). DOL and DFL are both very high.
  • Power (thermal or renewable IPPs): under regulated tariffs (cost-plus), fixed costs are recovered through a capacity charge, which dampens operating leverage for regulated assets. Merchant and renewable plants with fixed PPA tariffs have revenue that varies with generation (PLF or CUF), so DOL is high and project finance gearing of 70–75% gives high DFL.
  • IT services: largely people costs, with utilisation and bench management making them semi-variable. DOL is modest, and most large Indian IT companies are net cash, so DFL is about 1. Currency movements (USD/INR) behave like price changes and are the main source of margin swings.
  • FMCG with contract manufacturing: brand owners outsource production. Fixed costs are mainly advertising (a discretionary fixed cost), distribution overhead and staff. DOL is moderate and there is little debt.

Quantifying EPS volatility: a comparison

Two companies, each with revenue of ₹1,000 crore, under a ±10% volume shock (tax rate 25.168%, 10 crore shares).

Item (₹ crore) Cement Co (capital-intensive) FMCG Co (asset-light)
Contribution (P/V) 450 (45%) 250 (25%)
Fixed costs 300 100
EBIT 150 150
Interest 60 5
PBT 90 145
DOL 3.00 1.67
DFL 1.67 1.03
DCL 5.00 1.72
EPS (₹) 6.73 10.85
EPS after −10% volume (₹) 3.37 (−50%) 8.98 (−17.2%)

Same revenue, same EBIT, yet a 10% volume fall halves the cement company's EPS and trims the FMCG company's by about 17%. The market's response is a lower P/E for the cement company at mid-cycle and a higher cost of equity.

Implications for practice

  1. Debt capacity: lenders size debt to downside cash flows. High DOL means downside EBITDA is low, so debt capacity is lower per rupee of mid-cycle EBITDA (t5). Many capital-intensive companies nonetheless carry high leverage because they need to fund assets, so covenants and DSRAs are tighter.
  2. Valuation: use normalised (mid-cycle) earnings for cyclicals, and EV/EBITDA rather than P/E. Asset-light businesses are often valued on P/E or DCF with stable margins.
  3. FP&A scenarios: cyclicals need price and volume scenarios with realistic troughs; asset-light businesses need scenarios on wage inflation, utilisation, currency and channel mix.
  4. Strategy: firms can lower DOL by outsourcing (contract manufacturing, tolling), leasing on variable terms, or converting fixed staff to variable pay. That transfers some margin to suppliers in exchange for lower risk.

Excel implementation

Build a side-by-side comparison block with inputs for P/V, fixed costs, interest, tax and shares. Compute DOL, DFL and DCL, and use a one-way data table on the volume shock (−30% to +30%) with EPS for both companies as outputs. Chart the two EPS lines to show the fan-out.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Regulated utilities can be capital-intensive yet have low DOL, because tariffs pass fixed costs through. Do not assume capital intensity always means high DOL.
  • IT services fixed costs: bench costs and fixed-price contracts make costs stickier in downturns than they look. DOL rises when utilisation falls.
  • Ind AS 116: asset-light retailers and QSR chains with large store leases now show high depreciation and interest. Their reported DFL looks high even though economically the rent was always a fixed cost (s06).
  • Holding structures: group companies with promoter-level debt (pledged shares) carry financial risk outside the listed entity's balance sheet.
  • Cycle timing: comparing a cyclical at peak with an asset-light company understates the cyclical's risk.

Key takeaways

  • Capital-intensive businesses usually combine high DOL with high DFL, so DCL and EPS volatility are high.
  • Asset-light businesses have variable cost structures and little debt, so EPS tracks revenue.
  • Same revenue and EBIT can hide very different EPS sensitivities; always compute DOL, DFL and DCL.
  • Regulated tariffs and contract structures can break the link between capital intensity and DOL.
  • Leverage profiles drive valuation multiples, rating outcomes, covenant design and scenario design.

Practice Questions

Q1. Sector comparison [L1]

An integrated steel company has contribution of ₹9,000 crore, fixed costs of ₹6,000 crore and interest of ₹1,500 crore. An IT services company has contribution of ₹4,200 crore, fixed costs of ₹1,200 crore and interest of ₹60 crore. Compute DOL, DFL and DCL for each and the expected percentage EPS change for a 15% fall in volume.

Q2. Outsourcing to reduce DOL [L2]

An FMCG company manufactures in-house with fixed costs of ₹180 crore and a P/V ratio of 42% on revenue of ₹900 crore. A contract manufacturer offers to take over production, cutting fixed costs to ₹60 crore but reducing the P/V ratio to 30%. Compute EBIT and DOL under both structures at current revenue and at −20% revenue, and comment on the trade-off.

💡 Hint: Find the cost-structure indifference revenue as well; it frames the trade-off.

Q3. Regulated vs merchant power [L2]

Explain why a thermal power plant under a cost-plus CERC/SERC tariff has lower operating leverage than a merchant plant with the same capacity and cost base, and what this implies for the debt each can carry.

Q4. Valuation implication [L3]

You are asked to compare a cement company and an FMCG company for a fund that targets low earnings volatility. Both trade at an EV/EBITDA of 14x. Using the leverage concepts in these notes, set out the analysis you would perform, the adjustments to earnings you would make for the cement company, and the risks you would highlight.
