Roadmap topic: Capital Budgeting Evaluation Models: Project NPV, IRR, MIRR, Profitability Index, Discounted Payback, and mutually exclusive projects with unequal lives (EAA)

Overview

Capital budgeting decides where long-term capital goes: new plants, capacity expansions, replacements, technology platforms, new stores and hospitals. For an FP&A or corporate-finance professional in India, it means building a project model that a board, a lender's TEV consultant and the statutory auditor (for impairment and Ind AS 16/23 treatment) can all rely on. It also means interpreting the outputs correctly when they disagree. The evaluation metrics (NPV, IRR, MIRR, PI, discounted payback, EAA) are easy to compute in Excel. The hard parts are getting the incremental cash flows right, applying Indian tax depreciation and regime rules, and choosing the right metric for the decision at hand.

The sequence starts with the cash flows. You define incremental unlevered cash flows: capex, working-capital build-up, operating cash flow with the tax shield, and terminal flows including WC release (s01, c06). Relevance rules filter out sunk costs, allocated overheads and financing flows, and bring in opportunity costs, cannibalisation and after-tax salvage (s02, c05). The Indian tax layer follows: Sec 32 WDV depreciation on blocks of assets with the half-rate and additional-depreciation rules (s03), and the company-level choice between Sec 115BAA and the old regime, now with the Income-tax Act 2025 renumbering (s04).

You then evaluate. NPV is the primary rule (c01). IRR and MIRR give rate-based views, with MIRR fixing the reinvestment and multiple-root problems (c02). Conflicts between NPV and IRR arise from scale and timing and are resolved with the Fisher rate (s05). PI ranks projects under capital rationing, and combination search handles indivisible projects (s06, c03). Unequal-life mutually exclusive projects need replacement chains or EAA (s07, c04). Discounted payback adds a liquidity lens (c07). Finally, risk analysis (sensitivity, scenarios, Monte Carlo and certainty equivalents) and nominal/real consistency make the single-point NPV credible (s08).

This topic is the engine of the Week 21 exercise (a capital budgeting and expansion feasibility model) and of the Saturday case test comparing two expansion proposals. It draws on t1 (break-even utilisation) and t2 (operating leverage of the two proposals), and feeds t4 (hurdle rates and capital allocation) and t5 (debt capacity).

Related concepts to study

WACC as the hurdle rate (Week 16)

→ Week 16: WACC as hurdle rate; project vs firm-level discount rates

The discount rate for FCFF is WACC: \(E/V \times K_e + D/V \times K_d(1 - t)\), with Ke from CAPM (Indian risk-free rate from G-sec yields, ERP and beta). Week 16 built the company-level WACC. For a project, the relevant rate reflects the project's risk, not the company's average. A new-segment project (for example a cement company entering renewables) needs a pure-play beta, unlevered and relevered to the project's target gearing (t4). Using the corporate WACC for all projects systematically over-invests in risky projects and under-invests in safe ones. Keep the WACC nominal if the cash flows are nominal, and use the tax rate consistent with s04.

Reusing capex, depreciation and WC schedules (Weeks 13/14)

→ Week 13/14: capex and depreciation schedules, WC schedules reused in project models

The 3-statement model builds capex schedules (opening gross block, additions, disposals), book depreciation, and working-capital schedules from days ratios. A project model reuses these, with two changes. First, add a separate tax depreciation schedule on the Income-tax WDV basis (s03) alongside the Schedule II book schedule, because the tax shield must come from the tax schedule. Second, compute WC on incremental project revenue (WC as % of incremental revenue or days) and release it at the end. Linking both schedules to the same capex timeline avoids inconsistencies between the project NPV and the projected financial statements.

Ind AS 16 components and Ind AS 23 borrowing costs

Ind AS 16 component accounting and Ind AS 23 borrowing cost capitalisation during construction

Ind AS 16 requires significant components with different useful lives (for example kiln refractory lining, turbine overhaul) to be depreciated separately. Major inspections and overhauls are capitalised as components. For the project model, these appear as periodic capex and inform the maintenance capex line. Ind AS 23 requires borrowing costs directly attributable to a qualifying asset to be capitalised during construction. That affects reported profit and book depreciation, but not FCFF, which excludes interest entirely. For tax, interest capitalised up to the asset being put to use also forms part of actual cost (Sec 36(1)(iii) proviso, Explanation 8 to Sec 43(1)), slightly increasing the depreciation shield.

PLI incentives and state subsidies as project inflows

PLI scheme incentives and state industrial-policy subsidies (capital subsidy, SGST reimbursement) as project cash inflows

Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes (electronics, auto components, specialty steel, solar modules, pharma and others) pay a percentage of incremental sales over a base year, subject to minimum investment and sales thresholds, for a defined number of years. State policies offer capital subsidies, interest subvention, SGST reimbursement (often capped at a percentage of eligible fixed capital investment), stamp duty and power-tariff concessions. In a project model, include them as separate inflow lines with their eligibility conditions, timing (often delayed 12–24 months) and tax treatment (capital subsidies may reduce actual cost under Explanation 10 to Sec 43(1); revenue grants are taxable income). Run NPV with and without incentives to show dependence on them.

Real options that NPV misses

Real options: expansion, abandonment and deferral options that NPV misses

Static NPV assumes a fixed plan. Managers can expand if demand is strong (a second line on land already acquired), abandon if it is weak (sell the plant or switch it to another product), or defer investment until uncertainty resolves (wait for a tariff or policy decision). Each flexibility has value that a single-path DCF ignores, and the value is highest when uncertainty is high and the decision can be delayed or reversed. Simple valuation uses decision trees with probabilities and NPV at each node. More formal approaches use binomial lattices or Black–Scholes analogies. In practice, identify the options qualitatively in the board paper and quantify the largest one.

Common pitfalls

Interest in cash flows and WACC discounting

Including financing costs (interest) in project cash flows and also discounting at WACC (double-counting)

The mistake is to deduct interest (or loan repayments) in project cash flows and then discount at WACC, which already reflects the after-tax cost of debt. It happens because business plans are prepared on a PAT basis and copied into the DCF. The correct approach is either FCFF (no financing flows) at WACC, or FCFE (after interest, net borrowing) at Ke, consistently. Detect it in review by searching the cash-flow rows for "interest", "finance cost" or "loan" and checking the discount rate used against the cash-flow definition.

Book depreciation for the tax shield

Using book depreciation (Schedule II) instead of Income-tax WDV depreciation for the tax shield

Models built from projected P&Ls use Schedule II straight-line depreciation, which understates early-year tax deductions relative to Sec 32 WDV (15% on P&M, plus additional depreciation where available). The error delays the shield and understates NPV. The correct treatment is a separate tax depreciation schedule driving the tax line, with book depreciation used only for reported profit and deferred tax. Review by checking whether the tax computation references the tax WDV schedule and whether the half-rate rule and regime flag are applied.

Missing WC recovery or salvage tax

Forgetting WC recovery at end of life or the tax on salvage value

Analysts often build WC in early years and never release it, or include salvage at gross value. The first understates NPV; the second overstates it. Both happen because the terminal year is treated like any other year. The correct approach is a terminal-flows block with WC release (cumulative NWC at the final year), salvage, the tax WDV, and the tax treatment under the block rule (c05). In review, check that the sum of ΔNWC over the life is zero and that the salvage row has an accompanying tax row.

Ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR

Ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR instead of NPV/EAA

IRR ignores scale and assumes reinvestment at the IRR. For mutually exclusive projects with different sizes, timing or lives, it can pick the project that adds less value. Promoters like IRR because it is a single percentage. The correct approach is NPV for equal lives and EAA (or replacement chains) for unequal lives, with the Fisher-rate analysis to explain any disagreement with IRR. In review, check whether the recommendation table ranks by IRR and whether projects differ in size or life.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated project model: specialty-chemicals plant [L3]

A Dahej specialty-chemicals company evaluates a new plant.

  • Capex: ₹240 crore of plant and machinery, paid ₹140 crore on 1 April 2026 and ₹100 crore on 1 October 2026; put to use on 1 January 2027 (tax year 2026-27). Land already owned: book value ₹4 crore, market value ₹30 crore (tax on sale foregone ₹3 crore).
  • A feasibility study costing ₹2 crore was paid in FY26.
  • Operations FY28 to FY35 (8 years). Revenue ₹180 crore in FY28, ₹260 crore in FY29, ₹300 crore a year thereafter. Cash costs 68% of revenue. Incremental fixed cash costs ₹12 crore a year. HO allocation 2% of revenue.
  • Cannibalisation: lost contribution of ₹6 crore a year on an existing product.
  • NWC: 18% of revenue, released at the end of FY35.
  • Salvage of plant at end of FY35: ₹45 crore; the company's P&M block continues.
  • Tax: Sec 115BAA. WDV 15%. WACC 12% (nominal).

(a) List which items are relevant and how each enters the model. (b) Prepare the tax depreciation schedule for FY27–FY35. (c) Prepare the FCFF schedule and compute NPV, IRR, MIRR (finance and reinvestment at 12%), PI and discounted payback. (d) Compute the after-tax value of salvage under the continuing-block treatment and compare with the standalone formula.

Q2. Mutually exclusive projects with unequal lives and a regime question [L3]

A Pune auto-parts company must choose between two machining lines for a continuing product.

Line X Line Y
Cost (Year 0, ₹ crore) 50 85
Annual pre-tax cash savings (₹ crore) 22 26
Life (years) 4 8
Salvage (₹ crore) 5 8

Tax rate 25.168% (115BAA), WDV 15% (full rate in Year 1), block ceases at the end of each line's life (standalone salvage formula), WACC 11%.

(a) Compute NPV, IRR and EAA for each. (b) Explain any ranking conflict and recommend. (c) Under the old regime (34.944%), Line Y would qualify for 20% additional depreciation. Explain qualitatively how this would change the comparison and why the regime decision cannot be taken on this project alone.

Q3. Capital rationing across a group [L2]

A group has ₹150 crore for FY27. Independent projects (outlay, NPV in ₹ crore): A (60, 18), B (50, 17), C (40, 8), D (30, 10.5), E (20, 3). C and D are mutually exclusive.

(a) Assuming divisibility, rank by PI and find the optimal allocation. (b) Assuming indivisibility, find the NPV-maximising set. (c) Describe the Excel Solver set-up including the mutual-exclusivity constraint.

Q4. Risk and consistency review [L3]

You are reviewing a ₹900 crore solar-glass project model. You find: cash flows at constant FY26 prices with a nominal WACC of 12.5%; interest deducted in cash flows; book depreciation used for tax; a single-point NPV of ₹140 crore; and an IRR of 19% presented as the key metric. Expected inflation is 4.5%.

(a) Explain the direction of the effect of each error on NPV. (b) Set out the corrected structure of the model. (c) Design the risk analysis you would add: a tornado chart, three scenarios with a CHOOSE switch, break-even utilisation for NPV = 0, and a two-way data table of capex overrun × WACC. (d) State how you would present PLI incentives if the project's NPV is negative without them.

Q5. Interview case: defending NPV to a promoter [L2]

A promoter says: "IRR is 26% on Project S and 18% on Project T, so S is obviously better." Project S costs ₹80 crore with NPV of ₹30 crore over 5 years; Project T costs ₹400 crore with NPV of ₹95 crore over 12 years; WACC is 12%; the projects are mutually exclusive and repeatable. Prepare a structured response covering scale, timing, the Fisher rate, reinvestment, EAA and discounted payback, with the calculations you would show.
