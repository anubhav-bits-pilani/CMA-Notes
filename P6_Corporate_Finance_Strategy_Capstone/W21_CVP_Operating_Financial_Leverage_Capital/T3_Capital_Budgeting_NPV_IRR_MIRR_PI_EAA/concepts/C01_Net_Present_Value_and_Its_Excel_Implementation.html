Key concept

Net Present Value and Its Excel Implementation

Concept: NPV = Σ CF_t ÷ (1 + r)^t − I0; Excel =NPV(r, CF1:CFn) + CF0 (CF0 is outside the NPV function); =XNPV for dated flows

Statement

\[ \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t} - I_0 \]

where \(\text{CF}_t\) is the incremental after-tax free cash flow in period \(t\), \(r\) is the discount rate per period appropriate to the cash flows' risk and definition (WACC for FCFF, Ke for FCFE), \(n\) is the number of periods, and \(I_0\) is the initial outlay at \(t = 0\). Accept independent projects with NPV \(> 0\); among mutually exclusive projects of equal life, choose the highest NPV.

For dated flows:

\[ \text{XNPV} = \sum_{j} \frac{\text{CF}_j}{(1 + r)^{(d_j - d_0)/365}} \]

where \(d_j\) is the date of cash flow \(j\) and \(d_0\) the date of the first cash flow.

Explanation

NPV is the increase in shareholder wealth (on an enterprise basis for FCFF) from undertaking the project. It is additive across projects, uses the opportunity cost of capital as the reinvestment rate, and accounts for scale. These properties make it the primary decision rule.

Excel mechanics and traps:

  • =NPV(rate, value1, …) discounts the first value by one full period. It assumes the first cash flow occurs at the end of period 1. So the Year 0 outlay must be added outside: =NPV(r, C5:L5) + B5, where B5 is the (negative) Year 0 flow. Putting Year 0 inside the function discounts it by a year and understates the outlay's weight, overstating NPV for projects with a negative Year 0 flow.
  • =XNPV(rate, values, dates) uses actual dates with a 365-day year and discounts to the first date in the range. Use it for construction-phase flows on irregular dates, or for mid-year conventions. The rate is an annual effective rate.
  • Mid-year convention without XNPV: multiply the NPV result by \((1 + r)^{0.5}\), or discount each flow at \(t - 0.5\) explicitly.
  • NPV ignores blank cells but treats zeros as values. A blank in the middle of the range shifts the timing of later flows; use zeros, not blanks.
  • Per-period rate: for quarterly flows, use the quarterly rate \((1 + r)^{0.25} - 1\), not \(r \div 4\), if \(r\) is an annual effective rate.

Illustration

A Kolkata FMCG company's new packaging line (₹ crore; WACC 11.5%):

Year 0 1 2 3 4 5
FCFF −75 15 22 25 25 38

Correct: =NPV(11.5%, C2:G2) + B2 gives \(15 \div 1.115 + 22 \div 1.115^2 + 25 \div 1.115^3 + 25 \div 1.115^4 + 38 \div 1.115^5 - 75\). The discount factors are 0.8969, 0.8044, 0.7214, 0.6470 and 0.5803, giving PVs of 13.45, 17.70, 18.03, 16.17 and 22.05, which total ₹87.41 crore. NPV \(= \text{₹}12.41\) crore.

Wrong: =NPV(11.5%, B2:G2) gives \((87.41 - 75) \div 1.115 = \text{₹}11.13\) crore, because it treats every flow as one year later.

If the outlay actually occurs on 1 October 2026 and inflows on each 31 March from 2028, XNPV with those dates gives the precise answer.

Connections

NPV is the anchor of this topic: IRR is the rate at which NPV \(= 0\) (c02), PI is NPV scaled by outlay (c03), EAA is NPV annualised (c04), and discounted payback uses the same discounting (c07). The discount rate comes from Week 16 (WACC) and t4 (project-specific hurdle rates). The cash flows come from s01–s04. Practice Questions

Q1. Compute NPV correctly [L1]

A project has flows (₹ crore) of −120 in Year 0 and 30, 35, 40, 40, 45 in Years 1–5. WACC is 12%. Compute NPV, then compute the result an analyst would get by including Year 0 inside Excel's NPV function, and explain the difference.

Q2. XNPV for construction flows [L2]

Capex of ₹200 crore is paid on 1 April 2026 and ₹150 crore on 1 January 2027. Operating inflows of ₹90 crore arrive on 31 March of each year from 2028 to 2033. WACC is 12%. Write the Excel formula you would use, explain the date convention, and estimate the NPV.

💡 Hint: XNPV discounts to the first date in the range, so order the dates and include all flows.

Q3. Mid-year convention [L2]

Explain how switching from a year-end to a mid-year convention changes the NPV of a project with mostly positive operating flows, and show the adjustment factor for a 12% rate.
