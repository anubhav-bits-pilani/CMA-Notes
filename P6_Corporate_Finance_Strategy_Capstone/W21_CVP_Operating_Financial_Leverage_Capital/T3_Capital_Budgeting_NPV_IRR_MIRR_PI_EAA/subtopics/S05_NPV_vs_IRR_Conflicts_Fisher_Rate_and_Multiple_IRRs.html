NPV vs IRR Conflicts, Fisher Rate and Multiple IRRs

Scope: NPV vs IRR conflicts: scale, timing and the crossover (Fisher) rate; multiple IRRs for non-conventional flows

Why it matters

Boards and promoters in India like IRR: it is a single percentage that can be compared with a hurdle rate or a loan rate. For independent, conventional projects, IRR and NPV give the same accept/reject decision. For mutually exclusive projects, they can rank differently, and for projects with sign changes in later years (mine closure, major overhauls, staged capex) IRR can have several values or none. Knowing why and when the conflict arises, and resolving it in favour of NPV, is a core competency for capex review and a frequent interview question. Ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR is one of this topic's listed pitfalls.

Core notes

Why NPV and IRR agree for independent conventional projects

A conventional project has one sign change (outflows followed by inflows). Its NPV profile, NPV as a function of the discount rate \(r\), slopes down and crosses zero once, at the IRR. So NPV \(> 0\) if and only if \(r <\) IRR. The accept/reject signals are identical.

Why they conflict for mutually exclusive projects

With two projects A and B, each NPV profile slopes down, but at different rates. If the profiles cross at a positive rate (the crossover or Fisher rate), then below that rate one project has the higher NPV and above it the other does. IRR ranks the projects by where each profile meets the axis, which is independent of the actual cost of capital. NPV ranks them at the actual cost of capital.

Two sources of conflict:

  1. Scale differences: a large project with a lower IRR can add more value than a small project with a higher IRR. IRR is a rate; NPV is an amount of wealth.
  2. Timing differences: a project with cash flows concentrated early has a flatter NPV profile (less sensitive to \(r\)) than one with cash flows late. At low discount rates, the back-loaded project wins on NPV; at high rates, the front-loaded project wins.

Underlying both is the reinvestment assumption. IRR implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself, while NPV assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital. The cost of capital is the more defensible assumption. MIRR (c02) fixes this by making the reinvestment rate explicit.

Finding the Fisher rate

The crossover rate is the IRR of the incremental cash flows (B − A):

\[ \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_{B,t} - \text{CF}_{A,t}}{(1 + r_F)^t} = 0 \]

If the cost of capital is below \(r_F\), choose the project with the higher NPV at that rate (the one whose profile is steeper, usually the larger or later one). Equivalently, accept the incremental project if its IRR exceeds the cost of capital.

Illustration: timing conflict

Two mutually exclusive projects for a Tamil Nadu textile mill (₹ crore), with a WACC of 12%:

Year 0 1 2 3 4
A: process upgrade (front-loaded) −100 100 20 10 5
B: new product line (back-loaded) −100 0 20 60 100
B − A 0 −100 0 50 95

Discount factors at 12% are 0.8929, 0.7972, 0.7118 and 0.6355.

  • NPV(A) \(= 100 \times 0.8929 + 20 \times 0.7972 + 10 \times 0.7118 + 5 \times 0.6355 - 100 = \text{₹}15.5\) crore; IRR(A) \(\approx 25.0\%\).
  • NPV(B) \(= 20 \times 0.7972 + 60 \times 0.7118 + 100 \times 0.6355 - 100 = \text{₹}22.2\) crore; IRR(B) \(\approx 18.9\%\).
  • Fisher rate \(=\) IRR of (B − A) \(\approx 15.1\%\).

IRR ranks A first. NPV at 12% ranks B first. Because the WACC (12%) is below the Fisher rate (15.1%), B adds more value. At a WACC of 22%, the ranking would flip: NPV(A) \(\approx \text{₹}3.2\) crore and NPV(B) \(\approx -\text{₹}8.4\) crore. B's profile is steeper because its cash flows arrive later, so it is more sensitive to the discount rate.

In Excel, compute =IRR(B2:F2) for each project row and =IRR(B4:F4) for the incremental row, and confirm with an NPV data table over discount rates.

Illustration: scale conflict

Project Investment Annual inflow (5 years) IRR NPV at 12%
Small (S) 10 3.5 22.1% 2.62
Large (L) 50 15.0 15.2% 4.07

S has the higher IRR, but L adds more value (₹4.07 crore against ₹2.62 crore). If the two are mutually exclusive and capital is not rationed, choose L. The incremental project (L − S: invest ₹40 crore for ₹11.5 crore a year) has an IRR of about 13.5%, above 12%, which confirms the choice.

Multiple IRRs and no IRR

With non-conventional flows (more than one sign change), the NPV polynomial can have several roots:

  • Example: a mining project with flows \(-100, +230, -132\) (₹ crore), where the final outflow is mine-closure cost. NPV \(= 0\) at \(r = 10\%\) and \(r = 20\%\). NPV is positive between them and negative outside.
  • Descartes' rule: the number of positive IRRs is at most the number of sign changes.
  • Some flows have no real IRR (for example \(+100, -300, +250\), where NPV is positive at every rate).
  • Excel IRR returns only the root nearest the guess. Change the guess to find others, and always plot the NPV profile.

Resolution: use NPV at the cost of capital, or MIRR (single-valued for any flows), and present the NPV profile.

Excel implementation

=NPV(WACC, CF1:CFn) + CF0               ' NPV at WACC
=IRR(CF0:CFn, 0.1)                      ' IRR with guess
=IRR(Incremental_CF0:CFn)               ' Fisher (crossover) rate
=MIRR(CF0:CFn, Finance_Rate, Reinvest_Rate)

Build a one-way data table of NPV for each project over \(r\) from 0% to 30%, and chart the profiles to show the crossover.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Independent projects without rationing: NPV and IRR agree on accept/reject; ranking is irrelevant.
  • Capital rationing: ranking by PI is the relevant tool, not IRR (s06).
  • Borrowing-type flows (inflow first, then outflows, such as customer advances): IRR is the cost of borrowing, and the decision rule reverses (accept if IRR < cost of capital).
  • Unequal lives: NPV comparisons need replacement chains or EAA (s07).
  • Promoter preference for IRR: present IRR alongside NPV, but state that the ranking follows NPV, and show the Fisher-rate analysis.

Key takeaways

  • For independent conventional projects, NPV and IRR give the same accept/reject decision.
  • For mutually exclusive projects, scale and timing differences can make IRR and NPV rank differently.
  • The Fisher (crossover) rate is the IRR of the incremental cash flows; below it, the steeper-profile project wins.
  • Non-conventional flows can have multiple IRRs or none; use NPV or MIRR and plot the NPV profile.
  • NPV at the appropriate cost of capital is the decision rule; IRR is supplementary.

Practice Questions

Q1. Scale conflict [L1]

Project X costs ₹20 crore and returns ₹6 crore a year for 6 years. Project Y costs ₹60 crore and returns ₹15.5 crore a year for 6 years. They are mutually exclusive, and WACC is 11%. Compute NPV for each, estimate the IRRs (Excel allowed), and state your choice with reasons.

Q2. Fisher rate [L2]

Projects P and Q have flows (₹ crore): P: −80, 50, 30, 20; Q: −80, 10, 30, 75. Compute NPV for each at 8% and 18%, find the Fisher rate, and explain which project you would choose at a WACC of 12%.

💡 Hint: The crossover rate is the IRR of Q − P.

Q3. Multiple IRRs [L2]

A coal mine has flows (₹ crore) of −200 in Year 0, +500 in Year 1 and −309 in Year 2 (closure and rehabilitation). Find both IRRs, plot the NPV profile conceptually, and state whether the project should be accepted at a 12% cost of capital. Then compute MIRR with a 12% finance and reinvestment rate.

Q4. Board presentation [L3]

A promoter insists on choosing a hotel project with an IRR of 24% over a mixed-use development with an IRR of 18%. The hotel needs ₹150 crore; the development needs ₹600 crore. At the group's 13% WACC, NPVs are ₹48 crore and ₹130 crore respectively. Prepare a one-page argument for the board, including the incremental analysis and the reinvestment-rate point.

Q5. Interview question [L1]

"Why does IRR assume reinvestment at the IRR, and why does it matter?" Answer in four sentences.
