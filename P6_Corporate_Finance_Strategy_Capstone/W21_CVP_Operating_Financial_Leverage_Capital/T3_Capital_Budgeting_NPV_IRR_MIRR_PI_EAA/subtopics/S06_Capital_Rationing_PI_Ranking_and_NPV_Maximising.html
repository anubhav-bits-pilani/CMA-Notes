Capital Rationing: PI Ranking and NPV-Maximising Combinations

Scope: Capital rationing: PI ranking for divisible projects; NPV-maximising combinations for indivisible projects

Why it matters

In theory, a firm should accept every positive-NPV project. In practice, Indian companies face limits: a board-set annual capex envelope, lender covenants that cap borrowing, a promoter unwilling to dilute, a group treasury that allocates a fixed pool across subsidiaries, or management bandwidth. When capital is rationed, the goal becomes maximising total NPV within the budget. The ranking tool depends on whether projects are divisible (can be taken in part) or indivisible (all or nothing). Using IRR or NPV rankings alone under rationing gives the wrong portfolio.

Core notes

Hard vs soft rationing

  • Hard rationing: external limits. Lenders will not lend more, equity markets are closed to the firm, regulatory borrowing caps apply (for example, RBI exposure norms for the lending bank, or covenant headroom).
  • Soft rationing: internal limits set by management, such as a capex budget, a gearing ceiling or a dividend commitment. It is a control device against optimistic forecasts and execution overload, but if it persistently rejects positive-NPV projects, it destroys value.

Single-period rationing, divisible projects: rank by PI

The profitability index (c03):

\[ \text{PI} = \frac{\text{PV of future cash inflows}}{\text{PV of initial outlay}} = 1 + \frac{\text{NPV}}{\text{Initial outlay}} \]

PI measures NPV per rupee of the scarce resource (capital). With only Year 0 capital constrained and divisible projects (a project can be taken in part with proportional NPV), rank by PI in descending order and fund down the list until the budget is used, taking a fraction of the last project. This is the limiting-factor logic of t1 s06 applied to capital.

Single-period rationing, indivisible projects: search combinations

When projects are all-or-nothing, PI ranking can leave capital unused or skip a better combination. The correct approach is to evaluate every feasible combination (or solve an integer programme) and choose the one with the highest total NPV. Unused capital earns its opportunity cost (NPV of zero if invested at the cost of capital).

Illustration

A Pune group's capital budget for FY27 is ₹70 crore. All projects are independent with positive NPV at a 12% WACC.

Project Outlay (₹ crore) NPV (₹ crore) PI PI rank
A: automation 40 14.0 1.35 3
B: new SKU line 30 12.6 1.42 1=
C: warehouse 25 6.0 1.24 4
D: solar rooftop 20 8.4 1.42 1=
E: IT upgrade 15 3.3 1.22 5

Divisible: fund B (30) and D (20), using ₹50 crore. The remaining ₹20 crore buys half of A. Total NPV \(= 12.6 + 8.4 + 7.0 = \text{₹}28.0\) crore.

Indivisible, PI-greedy: B and D (₹50 crore); A does not fit, C does not fit (₹75 crore total), E fits. Total NPV \(= 12.6 + 8.4 + 3.3 = \text{₹}24.3\) crore, with ₹5 crore unused.

Indivisible, best combination: A and B use exactly ₹70 crore and give NPV \(= \text{₹}26.6\) crore. That beats the PI-greedy portfolio by ₹2.3 crore. The NPV ranking (A first) also does not by itself give the optimum; only the combination search does.

Multi-period rationing: linear and integer programming

If capital is constrained in more than one year (for example, ₹70 crore in FY27 and ₹50 crore in FY28), and projects need outlays in both years, a single PI cannot be computed (which year's capital is the denominator?). Formulate:

\[ \max \sum_j x_j \text{NPV}_j \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_j x_j I_{j,t} \le B_t \;\; \forall t, \quad x_j \in \{0, 1\} \text{ (or } 0 \le x_j \le 1\text{)} \]

where \(x_j\) is the proportion of project \(j\) accepted, \(I_{j,t}\) its outlay in year \(t\), and \(B_t\) the budget for year \(t\). Projects' cash inflows in a year can be allowed to relax that year's budget. Solve with Excel Solver (Simplex LP for divisible; set integer or binary constraints for indivisible). The shadow prices on the budget constraints show the value of an extra ₹1 of capital in each year.

Excel implementation

=1 + NPV_j / Outlay_j                        ' PI
=SUMPRODUCT(Select, NPV)                     ' objective (Select = 0/1 or fraction)
=SUMPRODUCT(Select, Outlay_FY27) <= Budget_FY27   ' constraint

Set up Solver with Select as decision variables, binary constraints for indivisible projects, and add dependency constraints (for example, D only if B: Select_D <= Select_B) and mutual-exclusivity constraints (Select_A + Select_C <= 1).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mutually exclusive projects inside the set: add constraints. PI ranking alone cannot handle them.
  • Contingent projects: a project that is only possible if another is accepted needs a dependency constraint.
  • Strategic or mandatory projects (safety, environmental compliance under pollution control board orders): fund them first; they reduce the budget for the rest.
  • Risk differences: PI and NPV should be computed at each project's risk-adjusted rate (t4), or the ranking favours risky projects.
  • Soft rationing that persists: if the shadow price of capital is consistently high, the right answer is to raise more capital, not to keep rationing. Present the shadow price to the board.
  • Unused capital: when it is returned to shareholders or used to prepay debt, compare with those alternatives (t4).

Key takeaways

  • Under single-period rationing with divisible projects, rank by PI and fund in order, taking a fraction of the last.
  • With indivisible projects, search combinations (or use integer programming) to maximise total NPV within the budget.
  • Neither PI-greedy nor NPV ranking guarantees the optimum for indivisible projects.
  • Multi-period constraints need linear or integer programming; Solver's shadow prices show the value of extra capital.
  • Persistent soft rationing of positive-NPV projects destroys value; quantify and report it.

Practice Questions

Q1. PI ranking [L1]

A company has ₹100 crore to invest. Projects (outlay, NPV in ₹ crore): P (50, 15), Q (40, 16), R (30, 6), S (20, 7), T (10, 1.5). Assuming divisibility, rank by PI and determine the optimal allocation and total NPV.

Q2. Indivisible projects [L2]

Using the data in Q1, assume the projects are indivisible. Identify the PI-greedy portfolio and the NPV-maximising portfolio, and quantify the difference.

💡 Hint: List feasible combinations that use close to ₹100 crore before comparing NPVs.

Q3. Two-period rationing [L2]

A company has budgets of ₹60 crore in Year 0 and ₹30 crore in Year 1. Project J needs ₹40 crore in Year 0 and ₹10 crore in Year 1 (NPV ₹18 crore); K needs ₹20 crore and ₹20 crore (NPV ₹12 crore); L needs ₹30 crore and ₹5 crore (NPV ₹10 crore); M needs ₹10 crore and ₹15 crore (NPV ₹6 crore). Formulate the integer programme and solve it by inspection, then describe the Excel Solver set-up.

Q4. Soft rationing debate [L3]

The board of a mid-cap pharma company caps capex at ₹300 crore a year to protect its AA rating. The FP&A team has identified ₹420 crore of positive-NPV projects, and the Solver shadow price of capital is ₹0.28 per rupee. Write a note to the CFO arguing whether the cap should be relaxed, covering the rating, funding options and the value forgone.
