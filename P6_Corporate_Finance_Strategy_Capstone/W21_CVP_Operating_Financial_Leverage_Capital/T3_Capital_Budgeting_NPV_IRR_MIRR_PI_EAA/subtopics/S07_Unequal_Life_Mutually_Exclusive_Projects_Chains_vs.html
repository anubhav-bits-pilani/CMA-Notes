Unequal-Life Mutually Exclusive Projects: Chains vs EAA

Scope: Unequal-life mutually exclusive projects: replacement chain vs equivalent annual annuity (EAA)

Why it matters

Many capex choices are between alternatives that last different lengths of time: a cheaper machine with a 3-year life against a premium machine lasting 6 years, a leased fleet replaced every 4 years against owned trucks kept for 8, a short-term software licence against a perpetual one. Comparing raw NPVs favours the longer-lived option simply because it generates cash for more years. If the service is needed on a continuing basis, each option will be replaced at the end of its life, so the comparison must be made over a common horizon. The two standard methods, the replacement chain (common life) and the equivalent annual annuity (EAA), give the same ranking when assumptions match. EAA is quicker and is the default in practice. Ranking such projects by IRR is a listed pitfall of this topic.

Core notes

When the adjustment is needed

  • The projects are mutually exclusive.
  • They have different lives.
  • The activity will continue and each project would be replaced with a similar one at the end of its life (repeatability).

If the activity ends when the project ends (a one-off contract), compare plain NPVs over each project's own life. If replacements will differ (technology improves, costs fall), a modified chain with explicit future costs is better.

Method 1: replacement chain (common life)

Repeat each project until both chains end in the same year (the lowest common multiple of the lives), and compute the NPV of each chain:

\[ \text{NPV}_{\text{chain}} = \text{NPV}_1 \left[1 + \frac{1}{(1 + r)^{n}} + \frac{1}{(1 + r)^{2n}} + \cdots \right] \]

for a project with life \(n\) and single-cycle NPV \(\text{NPV}_1\), repeated until the common horizon.

The method is intuitive and can accommodate changing replacement costs. It becomes clumsy when the LCM is large (for example 7 and 9 years gives 63).

Method 2: equivalent annual annuity

Convert each project's NPV into the level annual amount over its own life that has the same PV:

\[ \text{EAA} = \frac{\text{NPV}}{\text{AF}_{r,n}} = \text{NPV} \times \frac{r}{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}} \]

where \(\text{AF}_{r,n}\) is the annuity factor for \(n\) years at rate \(r\). Choose the project with the higher EAA (c04). For cost-only comparisons (no revenue), compute the equivalent annual cost (EAC) of each and choose the lower.

The EAA ranking equals the ranking of NPVs of infinite replacement chains, since the perpetuity value is EAA \(\div r\), and \(r\) is common to both. It also equals the replacement-chain ranking over the LCM horizon.

Illustration

A Ludhiana forging company must choose between two presses (₹ crore; WACC 12%):

Press A (standard) Press B (heavy-duty)
Cost (Year 0) 60 100
Annual net cash inflow 32 30
Life (years) 3 6
Annuity factor at 12% 2.4018 4.1114
NPV (single cycle) \(32 \times 2.4018 - 60 = 16.86\) \(30 \times 4.1114 - 100 = 23.34\)
EAA \(16.86 \div 2.4018 = 7.02\) \(23.34 \div 4.1114 = 5.68\)

Raw NPV favours B (₹23.34 crore against ₹16.86 crore). Replacement chain over 6 years: A twice, NPV \(= 16.86 + 16.86 \times 0.7118 = \text{₹}28.86\) crore, against ₹23.34 crore for B. So A wins. EAA gives the same answer: ₹7.02 crore a year for A against ₹5.68 crore for B.

Note the IRRs: A's IRR is about 27.8% and B's about 19.9%. Here IRR happens to agree with EAA, but IRR is not designed for this comparison and can rank wrongly when scale also differs (topic pitfall 4).

Optimal replacement cycle

The same EAC logic finds the economic life of an asset: compute the EAC of replacing every 1, 2, 3 … years (including rising maintenance costs and falling salvage values) and choose the cycle with the lowest EAC. This is common for vehicle fleets, IT hardware and dies.

Taxes, inflation and salvage

  • Compute NPVs from after-tax cash flows with tax depreciation and after-tax salvage (s03, c05). Under the block method, a replacement within the same block means the sale of the old asset reduces the block, and the new cost is added.
  • With inflation, repeating the same nominal cash flows is wrong: the replacement will cost more. Either work in real terms with a real discount rate (s08), or build a nominal chain with inflated replacement costs.

Excel implementation

=NPV(r, CF1:CFn) + CF0                 ' single-cycle NPV
=-PMT(r, n, NPV)                       ' EAA (PMT returns a negative of the annuity)
=NPV_cycle*(1 + 1/(1+r)^n)             ' two-cycle chain

=-PMT(r, n, NPV) is the quickest way to compute EAA, because PMT converts a present value into a level annuity.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Non-repeatable projects: if the opportunity ends (a five-year concession), do not use EAA; compare NPVs.
  • Technological change: if the replacement will be cheaper or better, a pure chain overstates the shorter-lived option's disadvantage, or vice versa. Model explicit future cash flows.
  • Different risk: compute each NPV at its own risk-adjusted rate before converting. EAA uses the same rate as the NPV.
  • Capacity mismatch: if the two options deliver different output, the comparison must include the value of output (or use cost per unit of output).
  • Real options: a shorter-lived option preserves flexibility to switch technology. That may justify a slightly lower EAA (topic related item on real options).

Key takeaways

  • For repeatable mutually exclusive projects with unequal lives, compare over a common horizon.
  • The replacement chain uses the LCM of lives; EAA converts NPV into a level annual amount and is quicker.
  • EAA \(=\) NPV \(\div\) annuity factor; choose the higher EAA (or the lower EAC for cost-only choices).
  • EAA ranking equals the infinite-chain ranking and the LCM-chain ranking under constant conditions.
  • Do not use IRR or raw NPV to rank unequal-life alternatives; adjust for inflation and technology change.

Practice Questions

Q1. EAA comparison [L1]

Two mutually exclusive machines: X costs ₹24 crore, generates ₹10 crore a year for 4 years; Y costs ₹40 crore, generates ₹11 crore a year for 7 years. WACC is 11%. Compute NPV and EAA for each and recommend.

Q2. Replacement chain [L2]

Using Q1, build a replacement chain over 28 years for both machines and show that the ranking matches EAA. Explain why 28 years is needed and why EAA is more practical.

💡 Hint: Use the perpetuity property of EAA to shortcut the chain arithmetic.

Q3. Equivalent annual cost for a fleet [L2]

A logistics company can buy trucks for ₹45 lakh each. Annual operating cost is ₹9 lakh in Year 1, rising by ₹2 lakh a year. Resale value is ₹30 lakh after 1 year, ₹22 lakh after 2, ₹15 lakh after 3, ₹10 lakh after 4 and ₹6 lakh after

  1. At 10% (ignoring tax), compute the EAC of replacing every 1 to 5 years and identify the optimal cycle.

Q4. Inflation and technology [L3]

A hospital is choosing between a CT scanner with a 5-year life (₹8 crore) and one with an 8-year life (₹12 crore). Technology is improving and prices of equivalent scanners are falling 5% a year in real terms, while general inflation is 5%. Explain how you would adapt the EAA or chain analysis and what data you would need.

Q5. Interview question [L1]

"Why can't we compare the NPVs of a 3-year and a 6-year project directly?" Answer in three sentences, and state when you can.
