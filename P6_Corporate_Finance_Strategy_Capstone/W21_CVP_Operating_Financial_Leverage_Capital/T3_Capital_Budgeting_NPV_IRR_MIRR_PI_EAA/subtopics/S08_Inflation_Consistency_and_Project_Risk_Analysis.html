Inflation Consistency and Project Risk Analysis Techniques

Scope: Inflation consistency (nominal vs real), risk analysis: sensitivity, scenario, Monte Carlo, certainty-equivalent

Why it matters

Two errors dominate the review of Indian project models. The first is inconsistency: cash flows projected at today's prices (real) are discounted at a WACC built from current bond yields (nominal), or vice versa. With Indian inflation of 4–6%, the bias over a 15-year project is large. The second is presenting a single-point NPV with no sense of how fragile it is. Boards, lenders and rating agencies expect sensitivity tables, downside scenarios and, for large projects, a probabilistic view. The Week 21 exercise and assessment require both consistency and a risk block (data tables, scenario switch, break-even utilisation).

Core notes

Nominal vs real: the consistency principle

  • Nominal cash flows include expected inflation; discount them at a nominal rate.
  • Real cash flows are in constant (today's) prices; discount them at a real rate.

The Fisher relation (c06) links the rates:

\[ (1 + r_{\text{nom}}) = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + i) \]

where \(i\) is expected inflation. Done correctly, both approaches give the same NPV, except for items that do not inflate with general prices. The main one is the tax shield on depreciation: tax depreciation is based on historical cost and is fixed in nominal terms. In a real model it must be deflated. That is why practitioners usually prefer nominal models.

Other items that break the equivalence:

  • Contracted prices fixed in nominal terms (PPAs with fixed tariffs, take-or-pay contracts without escalation).
  • Different inflation rates for different items (wage inflation of 8–10% against WPI of 3–4%; commodity prices).
  • Working capital, which grows with nominal revenue.
  • Debt service, which is nominal (relevant for FCFE models).

Illustration. Real cash flow of ₹100 crore in Year 3, inflation 5%, real rate 7%. Nominal cash flow \(= 100 \times 1.05^3 = 115.76\); nominal rate \(= 1.07 \times 1.05 - 1 = 12.35\%\). PV (nominal) \(= 115.76 \div 1.1235^3 = 81.63\). PV (real) \(= 100 \div 1.07^3 = 81.63\). ✓ But a nominal tax shield of ₹10 crore in Year 3 has PV \(= 10 \div 1.1235^3 = 7.05\), while treating it as real and discounting at 7% would give \(8.16\), an overstatement of 16%.

The same consistency principle extends to the cash-flow definition: FCFF with WACC, FCFE with Ke (week cross-cutting item), and currency (INR cash flows with an INR discount rate, USD with USD).

Sensitivity analysis

Change one input at a time, holding others at base, and record the effect on NPV (and IRR).

  • Tornado chart: rank inputs by the NPV swing from a defined range (for example ±10% or a realistic low/high), and plot horizontal bars. The top three or four inputs (often price, volume/utilisation, capex and a key input cost) are where diligence effort should go.
  • Break-even values: the input value at which NPV \(= 0\) (Goal Seek). Express it as % change from base: "NPV turns negative if realisation falls 8.4% or capex overruns 31%". Break-even utilisation is the Week 21 exercise requirement.
  • Data tables: one-way (input vs NPV) and two-way (utilisation × price; capex overrun × WACC).

Limitations: one-at-a-time changes ignore correlation (low volume often comes with low prices), and give no probabilities.

Scenario analysis

Change several inputs together to represent coherent states of the world:

Input Bear Base Bull
Utilisation (steady state) 65% 80% 90%
Realisation (₹/tonne) 4,900 5,400 5,800
Capex overrun +15% 0% −5%
Commissioning delay 9 months 0 0

Implement with a scenario switch:

=CHOOSE(Scenario, Bear_Util, Base_Util, Bull_Util)
=INDEX(Scenario_Table, MATCH(Input_Name, Input_List, 0), Scenario)

Report NPV, IRR, peak funding and minimum DSCR for each scenario. Probability-weighting scenarios gives an expected NPV, but the range is often more informative than the average.

Monte Carlo simulation

Assign probability distributions to key inputs (triangular, normal, lognormal, or discrete), with correlations where relevant. Draw thousands of combinations, compute NPV for each, and analyse the distribution: mean, standard deviation, percentiles (P10, P50, P90), and probability of NPV < 0.

Implementation options: Excel with RAND()-driven inputs and a data table on a dummy cell to generate iterations; NORM.INV(RAND(), mean, sd) for normal inputs; Python (NumPy) or add-ins (@RISK, Crystal Ball) for larger models. Correlated inputs need Cholesky decomposition or a copula, which is easier outside Excel.

Cautions: distribution parameters are judgements; garbage in, garbage out. Do not discount at a risk-adjusted rate and then also present the NPV spread as if it measured risk separately; the NPV distribution is a sensitivity tool, and the discount rate should still reflect systematic risk.

Certainty-equivalent approach

Replace each risky expected cash flow with a certain amount the decision-maker would accept instead, and discount at the risk-free rate:

\[ \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\alpha_t \, E[\text{CF}_t]}{(1 + r_f)^t} - I_0 \]

where \(\alpha_t\) (between 0 and 1) is the certainty-equivalent coefficient for year \(t\), and \(r_f\) is the risk-free rate (for example the government security yield for the matching tenor). The coefficients typically decline over time as uncertainty grows. The approach separates time value (handled by \(r_f\)) from risk (handled by \(\alpha_t\)), whereas a risk-adjusted discount rate compounds risk geometrically over time.

Consistency: if \(\alpha_t\) is derived to match a risk-adjusted rate \(k\), then \(\alpha_t = [(1 + r_f) \div (1 + k)]^t\). Never apply certainty equivalents and then discount at WACC; that double-counts risk (a t4 pitfall).

Other techniques

  • Risk-adjusted discount rate: a project-specific WACC with pure-play betas (t4).
  • Decision trees: for staged projects with decision points (pilot, then scale-up), the basis of real-options thinking (topic related item).
  • Payback and discounted payback as liquidity-risk indicators (c07).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mixed bases: a model with real revenue but nominal capex escalation and a nominal WACC is the most common error. Label every input "real" or "nominal" and include an inflation index row.
  • Differential inflation: if output prices lag cost inflation (regulated tariffs, competitive markets), margins compress over time. Model separate indices.
  • High uncertainty in early years (ramp-up): scenario delays matter more than percentage changes in steady state.
  • Correlated risks: commodity cycles affect price and volume together, so scenario analysis is more realistic than independent sensitivities.
  • Presenting Monte Carlo: decision-makers need P10/P50/P90 and the probability of loss, not the full histogram.

Key takeaways

  • Discount nominal cash flows at nominal rates and real at real rates; tax shields are nominal by construction.
  • Sensitivity analysis identifies key drivers and break-even values; tornado charts and data tables present them.
  • Scenario analysis changes correlated inputs together and is implemented with a CHOOSE or INDEX switch.
  • Monte Carlo gives a distribution of NPV, but its inputs are judgements and it does not replace the discount rate.
  • Certainty-equivalent cash flows are discounted at the risk-free rate; never combine them with a risk-adjusted rate.

Practice Questions

Q1. Nominal-real consistency [L1]

A project's real cash flows are ₹40 crore a year for 5 years after an outlay of ₹130 crore. Inflation is 5% and the nominal WACC is 12.5%. Compute NPV in real terms and in nominal terms, and show they are equal.

Q2. Tax shield inflation trap [L2]

A real-terms project model includes tax depreciation shields of ₹6 crore, ₹5.1 crore and ₹4.3 crore in Years 1–3, discounted at a real rate of 7%. Inflation is 5%. Compute the overstatement in PV and explain how to fix the model.

💡 Hint: Convert the real rate into a nominal rate and apply it to the nominal shields.

Q3. Tornado chart [L2]

For a ₹400 crore cement grinding unit with base NPV of ₹62 crore, the NPV impacts of low/high cases are: realisation ±5%: −₹78 / +₹78 crore; utilisation ±10 points: −₹55 / +₹50 crore; capex ±15%: −₹45 / +₹45 crore; fuel cost ±10%: −₹30 / +₹30 crore; WACC ±1 point: −₹28 / +₹33 crore. Describe how to build the tornado chart in Excel and what the chart tells management.

Q4. Certainty equivalents [L2]

Expected cash flows are ₹50 crore, ₹60 crore and ₹70 crore in Years 1–3, with an outlay of ₹140 crore. Certainty- equivalent coefficients are 0.95, 0.88 and 0.80, and the risk-free rate is 7%. Compute NPV. Then derive the coefficients implied by a risk-adjusted rate of 13% and compare.

Q5. Monte Carlo design [L3]

Design a Monte Carlo simulation for a 200 MW solar project in Rajasthan with a fixed-tariff PPA. Specify the uncertain inputs, their distributions and correlations, the outputs (NPV, equity IRR, minimum DSCR), how many iterations you would run, the tool you would use, and how you would present results to the investment committee.
