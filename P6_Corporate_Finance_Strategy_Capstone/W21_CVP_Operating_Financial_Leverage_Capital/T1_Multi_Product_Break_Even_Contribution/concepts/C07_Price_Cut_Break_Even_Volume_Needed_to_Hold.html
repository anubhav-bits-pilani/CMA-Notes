Key concept

Price-Cut Break-Even: Volume Needed to Hold Contribution

Concept: Price-cut break-even: required new volume = Current total CM ÷ New CM per unit

Statement

\[ Q_{\text{new}} = \frac{Q_0 \times (P_0 - V)}{P_1 - V}, \qquad \frac{Q_{\text{new}} - Q_0}{Q_0} = \frac{d}{c - d} \]

where \(Q_0\) is current volume, \(P_0\) the current price, \(P_1\) the new (lower) price, \(V\) variable cost per unit (assumed unchanged), \(c = (P_0 - V) \div P_0\) is the current P/V ratio, and \(d = (P_0 - P_1) \div P_0\) is the price cut as a fraction of the current price. A price cut is only worth considering if expected volume exceeds \(Q_{\text{new}}\) and fixed costs do not step up to deliver the extra volume.

Explanation

A price cut reduces contribution on every unit already being sold, not just on the extra units. The extra units must therefore make up the lost contribution on the base volume and still earn the new, thinner CM. Holding total contribution constant (and fixed costs constant), EBIT is unchanged at \(Q_{\text{new}}\).

The second form shows why low-margin businesses rarely win price wars. The required volume increase explodes as \(d\) approaches \(c\):

Current P/V \(c\) Price cut 5% Price cut 10% Price cut 15%
15% +50% +200% Impossible
30% +20% +50% +100%
50% +11% +25% +43%

Compare the required increase with a realistic price elasticity of demand \(\varepsilon\): the expected volume increase is roughly \(\varepsilon \times d\) for small changes. A brand with \(\varepsilon = -1.5\) and \(c = 30\%\) gains about 15% volume from a 10% cut, against 50% needed.

Extensions:

  • Variable costs that change: if a higher volume earns supplier rebates, use the new \(V\) in the denominator.
  • Step costs: if the extra volume crosses a threshold (s08), add the step to the target contribution.
  • Price increase: the same logic gives the maximum volume loss that can be tolerated, \(d \div (c + d)\).
  • GST: work with net realisation excluding GST; a GST rate change passed through is not a price cut.
  • Competitive response: if rivals match, the volume gain is mostly lost while the margin loss stays.

Illustration

A Kanpur leather-goods exporter sells 5 lakh bags a year at ₹2,000 (net of export incentives), with variable cost of ₹1,400. The buyer asks for a 7.5% price reduction and promises more volume.

Item Current After cut
Price (₹) 2,000 1,850
CM per bag (₹) 600 450
Total CM (₹ crore) 30.0 –
Required volume – 30.0 crore ÷ 450 = 6.67 lakh bags
Required increase – +33.3%

Formula check: \(d = 7.5\%\), \(c = 30\%\); \(0.075 \div 0.225 = 33.3\%\). ✓

If the buyer's volume commitment is 6 lakh bags, contribution falls to ₹27 crore: a ₹3 crore EBIT loss. The negotiating position is clear: accept the price only with a minimum volume of about 6.7 lakh bags, or trade the cut for a lower specification that reduces \(V\).

Connections

This concept underlies the price-cut vs volume-gain analysis in s07 and links to c03 (a thin MOS amplifies the damage of a margin cut) and to t2 (the EBIT effect is magnified into EPS by DFL). In FP&A it frames pricing committee decisions, distributor scheme design, and key-account negotiations. In valuation work it explains why a sector price war (telecom tariffs, airline fares) hits EBITDA so hard.

Practice Questions

Q1. Required volume [L1]

A cement dealer sells 80,000 tonnes at ₹5,800 per tonne with variable cost of ₹4,640. Compute the volume needed to hold contribution after a ₹290 per tonne price cut, and the required percentage increase.

Q2. Price cut with rebate [L2]

An FMCG company sells 3 crore packs at ₹50 with variable cost of ₹35. A 6% price cut is expected to raise volume by 30%, and at the higher volume a packaging supplier grants a rebate of ₹0.80 per pack on all packs. Is the price cut worthwhile? State the minimum volume increase needed with the rebate.

💡 Hint: Use the post-rebate variable cost in the new CM per pack.

Q3. Price increase tolerance [L2]

A software-enabled services firm has a P/V ratio of 55% and is considering a 12% price increase. Compute the maximum percentage volume loss it can absorb without reducing total contribution, and explain why the asymmetry with a price cut matters for pricing strategy.
