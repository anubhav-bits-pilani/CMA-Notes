Subtopic note

Volume Shocks, Price-Cut Trade-offs and Mix-Shift Scenarios

Scope: Volume-shock sensitivity: ±10–30% volume, price-cut vs volume-gain trade-offs, mix-shift scenarios

Why it matters

Budgets in Indian businesses are routinely stress-tested against demand shocks: a weak monsoon hitting rural FMCG and tractors, an export slowdown hitting textiles and IT, a GST rate change, or a competitor's price war. Boards and lenders want to see EBIT and cash at −10%, −20% and −30% volume, and the sales team will propose price cuts "to protect volume". CVP gives you the mechanics to answer three questions quickly. How much EBIT is lost for a given volume fall? How much extra volume does a price cut need just to stand still? And what happens when the shock does not hit all products equally? These outputs feed the scenario switch and data tables in the Week 21 exercise.

Core notes

Uniform volume shocks

With constant price, variable cost per unit and fixed costs:

\[ \Delta \text{EBIT} = \Delta Q \times (P - V), \qquad \%\Delta \text{EBIT} = \text{DOL} \times \%\Delta Q \]

DOL is measured at the base level (t2). Because DOL itself changes with volume, compute each scenario directly from the P&L rather than multiplying a single DOL by large shocks. The DOL multiplier is exact for the change from the base, but it should not be chained across scenarios.

Illustration. A tractor-component supplier: sales ₹600 crore, P/V 30%, fixed costs ₹126 crore, EBIT ₹54 crore, DOL \(= 180 \div 54 = 3.33\).

Volume shock Sales (₹ crore) Contribution EBIT % change in EBIT
+10% 660 198 72 +33.3%
Base 600 180 54 –
−10% 540 162 36 −33.3%
−20% 480 144 18 −66.7%
−30% 420 126 0 −100%

A 30% volume fall takes the business exactly to break-even (MOS was 30%). The table is symmetric in rupees but not in the DOL measured at each new level.

Price-cut vs volume-gain trade-off

A price cut lowers the CM per unit on every unit, so volume must rise disproportionately just to hold total contribution:

\[ Q_{\text{new}} = \frac{Q_{\text{old}} \times \text{CM}_{\text{old}}}{\text{CM}_{\text{new}}}, \qquad \text{Required \% volume increase} = \frac{\Delta P}{\text{CM}_{\text{old}} - \Delta P} \]

where \(\Delta P\) is the price cut per unit (and variable cost is unchanged). As a ratio of price, if the P/V ratio is \(c\) and the price cut is \(d\) (as a fraction of price), the required volume increase is \(d \div (c - d)\).

Illustration. Price ₹1,000, variable cost ₹700, CM ₹300 (P/V 30%). A 10% price cut drops CM to ₹200. Required volume increase \(= 100 \div 200 = 50\%\). A 5% cut needs \(50 \div 250 = 20\%\) more volume. Low-margin businesses are extremely sensitive: at a P/V of 15%, a 10% price cut needs a 200% volume increase.

Compare the required increase with the expected price elasticity. If elasticity is −1.5, a 10% price cut gives about 15% more volume, far short of the 50% needed. The price cut destroys contribution unless it also changes the cost structure (absorbing fixed costs at a higher utilisation does not count unless there is spare capacity and no step cost).

The reverse holds for price increases. A 10% increase at a P/V of 30% can afford to lose \(d \div (c + d) = 25\%\) of volume before contribution falls.

Mix-shift scenarios

Volume shocks rarely hit all products uniformly. Rural slowdowns hit entry-level SKUs; urban premiumisation lifts high-end products; export shocks hit one segment. Model the mix explicitly:

  1. Set base volumes by product.
  2. Apply product-specific shocks (for example −25% economy, −5% premium).
  3. Recompute total contribution, the new weighted P/V and the new BEP.

Illustration. Using the paint example from s02 (E: CM ₹60, P: CM ₹200, fixed ₹36 crore), base volumes of 36 lakh litres E and 24 lakh litres P give contribution of ₹21.6 + ₹48.0 = ₹69.6 crore and EBIT of ₹33.6 crore.

Scenario E volume P volume Contribution (₹ crore) EBIT (₹ crore) Weighted P/V
Base 36.0 24.0 69.6 33.6 36.3%
Uniform −15% 30.6 20.4 59.2 23.2 36.3%
Mix shift: E −30%, P 0% 25.2 24.0 63.1 27.1 37.0%
Mix shift: E 0%, P −22.5% 36.0 18.6 58.8 22.8 35.6%

Volumes are in lakh litres. The economy-shock scenario loses twice as many litres as the premium-shock scenario (10.8 lakh against 5.4 lakh), yet it earns ₹4.3 crore more EBIT. What matters is which product loses volume, not how much total volume falls.

Building the sensitivity in Excel

  • Put shocks as inputs (one per product) and a scenario selector:
=CHOOSE(Scenario_No, 0, -10%, -20%, -30%, Custom_Shock)
=Base_Volume * (1 + Shock)
  • Use a one-way data table on the uniform shock (−30% to +10%) with EBIT, EBIT margin, MOS % and DOL as outputs.
  • Use a two-way data table of price change × volume change with total contribution as the output, and shade the cells where contribution is above base (conditional formatting). This "iso-contribution" frontier is the clearest visual for a sales director.
  • Keep data tables on a separate sheet and set calculation to "Automatic except data tables" during build.

In Power BI, a what-if parameter for the volume shock (GENERATESERIES(-0.3, 0.1, 0.05)) with measures for EBIT gives an interactive slider for management.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Variable cost is not constant: volume falls can trigger lost volume rebates from suppliers, and volume rises can trigger overtime or spot purchases. Price variable cost by band if material.
  • Fixed costs respond with a lag: in a prolonged shock, management cuts discretionary fixed costs (ad spend, contract staff). A one-year shock model with all fixed costs constant overstates the downside; a three-year model without cost actions also overstates it.
  • Step costs (s08): a volume increase that crosses a capacity threshold adds a fixed-cost step.
  • Competitive response: a price cut matched by competitors yields little volume. The required-volume test assumes the rival does not respond.
  • Working capital and cash: volume falls release WC (receivables and inventory shrink), cushioning cash in the short run. Price cuts do not release WC.
  • GST pass-through: a GST rate cut passed on to customers is not a price cut in the CVP sense, because net realisation is unchanged.

Key takeaways

  • %ΔEBIT \(=\) DOL \(\times\) %Δvolume from the base, but recompute each scenario directly.
  • Required volume increase for a price cut \(= d \div (c - d)\); low-P/V businesses can rarely win with price cuts.
  • Mix shifts can matter more than total volume: model product-specific shocks.
  • Present results as a table at ±10–30% and a price × volume two-way grid.
  • Relax constant-cost assumptions for long shocks, step costs and competitor response.

Practice Questions

Q1. Volume-shock table [L1]

A company has sales of ₹250 crore, a P/V ratio of 40% and fixed costs of ₹70 crore. Prepare a table of sales, contribution, EBIT and % change in EBIT at volume changes of +10%, −10%, −20% and −30%. State the base DOL and the volume fall that brings EBIT to zero.

Q2. Price-cut evaluation [L2]

An FMCG brand sells a 1-kg detergent pack at a net realisation of ₹120, with variable cost of ₹84. Marketing proposes an 8% price cut and expects a 25% volume increase. Annual volume is 4 crore packs. Evaluate the proposal on contribution and state the minimum volume increase needed.

💡 Hint: Compute the new CM per pack, then the required volume using the current total contribution.

Q3. Mix-shift scenario [L2]

A two-wheeler maker sells 100cc motorcycles (CM ₹9,000 each, 6 lakh units) and premium 350cc motorcycles (CM ₹38,000 each, 1 lakh units). Fixed costs are ₹780 crore. Compare EBIT in two scenarios with the same 15% fall in total units: (a) the fall is uniform; (b) the entire fall is in the 100cc range. Explain the result.

Q4. Two-way data table design [L3]

Design a two-way data table for a cement plant's FY27 budget with realisation per tonne (−10% to +10% in 2.5% steps) on one axis and volume (−20% to +10% in 5% steps) on the other, with EBITDA as the output. Specify the layout, the input cells it must reference, how you would highlight the break-even contour, and two common errors that break data tables.

Q5. Price war case [L3]

A regional competitor cuts prices by 6% in your core market, where your P/V ratio is 22% and price elasticity is estimated at −2.0 if you do not match. Prepare a structured argument comparing (a) matching the cut and (b) holding price, stating every assumption you would need and the quantitative test you would apply.
