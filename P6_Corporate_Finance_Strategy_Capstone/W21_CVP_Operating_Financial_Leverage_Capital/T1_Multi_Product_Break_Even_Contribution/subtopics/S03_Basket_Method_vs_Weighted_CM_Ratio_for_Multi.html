Basket Method vs Weighted CM Ratio for Multi-Product BEP

Scope: Composite-unit (basket) method vs weighted-average CM ratio method for multi-product break-even

Why it matters

Once the mix basis is fixed (s02), there are two standard ways to compute a multi-product break-even. The composite-unit (basket) method works in physical units and suits capacity planning, production scheduling and plant utilisation. The weighted-average CM ratio method works in rupees and suits revenue budgets, segment analysis and situations with no common unit. Interviewers for FP&A roles often give a three-product case and watch whether you can do both, reconcile them, and explain when each is appropriate. In a model, building both and checking that they agree is a cheap and strong integrity check.

Core notes

Method 1: composite unit (basket)

Define a basket that holds products in their unit-mix proportions, using the smallest whole numbers (for example 3 units of A, 2 of B and 1 of C). Then:

  1. CM per basket \(= \sum n_i (P_i - V_i)\), where \(n_i\) is the number of units of product \(i\) in a basket.
  2. Break-even baskets \(= \text{Fixed costs} \div \text{CM per basket}\).
  3. Break-even units of product \(i\) \(=\) break-even baskets \(\times n_i\).
  4. Break-even revenue \(= \sum (\text{BE units}_i \times P_i)\).

A variant uses weights that sum to 1 (a "weighted-average CM per unit", see c01). This is the same calculation scaled to one composite unit.

Method 2: weighted-average CM ratio

  1. Compute each product's P/V ratio \(= (P_i - V_i) \div P_i\).
  2. Weighted P/V \(= \sum w_i^{\text{rev}} \times \text{PV}_i\), or more simply total CM \(\div\) total sales at the budgeted mix (c02).
  3. Break-even revenue \(= \text{Fixed costs} \div \text{weighted P/V}\).
  4. Split the break-even revenue by revenue mix; convert to units by dividing by price if needed.

Worked illustration

A Hyderabad consumer-durables maker has fixed costs of ₹42 crore. Budgeted volumes are as follows.

Product Units (lakh) Price (₹) Variable cost (₹) CM per unit (₹) Sales (₹ crore) CM (₹ crore)
Mixer-grinder (A) 3.0 3,000 2,100 900 90.0 27.0
Air cooler (B) 2.0 7,500 5,250 2,250 150.0 45.0
Water purifier (C) 1.0 12,000 7,200 4,800 120.0 48.0
Total 6.0 360.0 120.0

Budgeted EBIT \(= 120 - 42 = \text{₹}78\) crore.

Basket method. Basket = 3A + 2B + 1C. CM per basket \(= 3 \times 900 + 2 \times 2{,}250 + 4{,}800 = \text{₹}12{,}000\). BE baskets \(= 42{,}00{,}00{,}000 \div 12{,}000 = 35{,}000\). BE units: A 1,05,000, B 70,000, C 35,000. BE revenue \(= 1.05 \times 3{,}000 + 0.70 \times 7{,}500 + 0.35 \times 12{,}000\) (₹ lakh) \(= \text{₹}126\) crore.

Weighted CM ratio. Weighted P/V \(= 120 \div 360 = 33.33\%\). BE revenue \(= 42 \div 0.3333 = \text{₹}126\) crore. ✓

The methods agree because the revenue mix (25% : 41.7% : 33.3%) is the one implied by the 3:2:1 unit mix at budgeted prices. If someone had instead used a 3:2:1 revenue mix with the unit-based CMs, the answer would differ (pitfall 1 of this topic).

Comparison

Aspect Basket (composite unit) Weighted CM ratio
Output Units of each product Rupees of total sales
Needs Unit mix, unit CM Revenue mix or total CM and sales
Best for Plants, capacity, production plans Revenue budgets, services, segments
Data from annual reports Rarely possible (no units disclosed) Possible using segment revenue and results
Sensitivity to price change CM per basket changes directly Weighted P/V changes and the revenue mix shifts
Interpretation risk Basket is abstract when products are unrelated Hides which product carries the break-even

Excel build

Lay out products in rows, with columns for mix units, price, variable cost and CM. Then:

=SUMPRODUCT(MixUnits, Price - VarCost)               ' CM per basket
=FixedCosts / CM_per_basket                          ' BE baskets
=BE_baskets * MixUnits                               ' BE units by product (spill)
=SUMPRODUCT(Units, Price - VarCost) / SUMPRODUCT(Units, Price)   ' weighted P/V
=FixedCosts / Weighted_PV                            ' BE revenue
=ROUND(SUMPRODUCT(BE_units, Price) - BE_revenue, 0) = 0         ' reconciliation check

Use named ranges, and keep the mix input in one row, so scenario switches (s07) can change the mix without touching the formulas.

Contribution ranking inside the basket

Both methods show the average. For decisions, also show each product's share of total contribution and its P/V ratio. In the illustration, C is only 1/6 of units but 40% of contribution, so a shortfall in purifiers hurts break-even far more than a shortfall in mixers.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Product-specific fixed costs: if some fixed costs are traceable (a dedicated purifier line), compute each product's own break-even against its traceable fixed costs, and then the company break-even against common fixed costs using the remaining "segment margin". This is more informative than a single basket.
  • Non-integer baskets: round only at the end. Rounding baskets up gives a slightly conservative BEP.
  • Negative-CM products (loss leaders) reduce CM per basket. The basket method handles them correctly as long as they stay in the mix, but a negative-CM product with a large weight can make break-even impossible.
  • Joint products with common processing cost: the basket is defined technologically, and CM should be measured at split-off or after further processing as relevant.
  • Multi-unit businesses (tonnes and numbers): the basket can mix units, but the "total units" figure then has no meaning. Report product-level units only.

Key takeaways

  • Basket method: CM per basket from unit mix, then BE baskets, then units by product.
  • Weighted CM ratio: total CM \(\div\) total sales, then BE revenue, then split by revenue mix.
  • The two reconcile exactly when the revenue mix is the one implied by the unit mix at current prices.
  • Use the basket for capacity and production questions and the CM ratio for revenue and segment questions.
  • Show product-level contribution shares beside the composite answer, because the average hides concentration.

Practice Questions

Q1. Basket break-even for a dairy [L1]

A Maharashtra dairy sells toned milk (₹54 per litre, variable cost ₹44), curd (₹80 per kg, variable cost ₹52) and paneer (₹360 per kg, variable cost ₹250) in a physical mix of 10:3:1. Annual fixed costs are ₹27.3 crore. Compute break-even volumes of each product and break-even revenue using the basket method.

Q2. Reconcile both methods [L2]

Using the data in Q1, compute the weighted P/V ratio at the budgeted mix and confirm the break-even revenue. Then explain what would happen to the reconciliation if paneer's price rose to ₹400 per kg while the physical mix stayed the same.

💡 Hint: Re-derive the revenue mix after the price change before computing the weighted P/V.

Q3. Traceable vs common fixed costs [L2]

Of the ₹27.3 crore in Q1, ₹4.2 crore relates to a dedicated paneer plant and ₹1.8 crore to curd incubation rooms; the rest is common. Compute each product's segment margin at a budgeted volume of 1,000 lakh litres of milk (with the mix unchanged), and explain how this changes the conversation about the paneer line.

Q4. Choosing the method for a segment analysis [L3]

You are analysing an NSE-listed diversified company whose Ind AS 108 segment note gives revenue and segment results for three segments but no volumes, plus unallocable expenses of ₹310 crore. Explain which break-even method you can apply, the assumptions you must make about cost behaviour in each segment, and the limitations you would disclose in your note.

Q5. Interview question [L1]

An interviewer asks: "Why does the basket method give the same answer as the weighted CM ratio in one case but not in another?" Answer in no more than five sentences.
