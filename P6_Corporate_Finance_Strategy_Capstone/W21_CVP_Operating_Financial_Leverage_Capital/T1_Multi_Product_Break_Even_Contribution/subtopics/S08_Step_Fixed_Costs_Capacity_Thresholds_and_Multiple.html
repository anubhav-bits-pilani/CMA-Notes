Step-Fixed Costs, Capacity Thresholds and Multiple Break-Evens

Scope: Step-fixed costs and capacity thresholds (shift additions, second line) creating multiple break-even points

Why it matters

The textbook CVP chart has one straight fixed-cost line and one break-even point. Real plants do not behave like that. Adding a second shift brings supervisors, security, canteen and maintenance crews. Going beyond the first line's capacity needs a second line with its own depreciation, manpower and power demand charges. A warehouse fills up and a new one has to be leased. Each threshold adds a block of fixed cost, so profit can fall when volume rises just past a threshold, and a business can have two or more break-even points. Capacity expansion decisions, budget volume targets and the Week 21 exercise (break-even utilisation for a new plant) all depend on modelling these steps correctly.

Core notes

Step-fixed cost behaviour

A step-fixed cost is constant over a band of activity and jumps to a higher level when the band is exceeded:

\[ F(Q) = F_0 + \sum_{k} S_k \cdot \mathbf{1}\left[Q > C_k\right] \]

where \(F_0\) is base fixed cost, \(S_k\) the size of step \(k\), \(C_k\) the capacity threshold that triggers it, and \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) equals 1 when the condition holds. Typical Indian examples:

Step Trigger Typical cost content
Second / third shift Utilisation beyond single-shift capacity Supervisors, shift allowances, canteen, transport, security
Second production line Volume beyond line 1 capacity Depreciation, line manpower, maintenance, insurance
Power contract demand Peak load beyond sanctioned contract demand Higher fixed demand charges from the DISCOM
Warehouse / depot Inventory beyond existing space Lease (Ind AS 116 ROU depreciation + interest), staff
Compliance thresholds Headcount or turnover limits Factory licence category, statutory welfare officers, pollution consent

Multiple break-even points

With one step at threshold \(C_1\):

  • First BEP: \(Q_1 = F_0 \div (P - V)\), valid if \(Q_1 \le C_1\).
  • Second BEP: \(Q_2 = (F_0 + S_1) \div (P - V)\), valid if \(Q_2 > C_1\).

Between \(C_1\) and \(Q_2\) the business can be loss-making again even though it was profitable just below \(C_1\). The profit function is saw-toothed. There is a drop of \(S_1\) at each threshold, and then profit rises at the CM rate.

Illustration: adding a second shift

A Chennai auto-component plant has capacity of 60,000 units per shift-year. Price is ₹2,500 per unit, variable cost ₹1,500 (CM ₹1,000).

Band Volume range Fixed costs (₹ crore)
Single shift 0–60,000 4.0
Two shifts 60,001–1,20,000 6.8
Second line + three shifts 1,20,001–2,00,000 12.5
Break-even Calculation Units Valid?
BEP 1 4.0 crore ÷ 1,000 40,000 Yes (≤ 60,000)
BEP 2 6.8 crore ÷ 1,000 68,000 Yes (> 60,000)
BEP 3 12.5 crore ÷ 1,000 1,25,000 Yes (> 1,20,000)

Interpretation:

  • At 60,000 units EBIT \(= 6.0 - 4.0 = \text{₹}2.0\) crore.
  • At 61,000 units EBIT \(= 6.1 - 6.8 = -\text{₹}0.7\) crore, a loss caused by the shift step.
  • At 1,20,000 units EBIT \(= 12.0 - 6.8 = \text{₹}5.2\) crore.
  • At 1,21,000 units EBIT \(= 12.1 - 12.5 = -\text{₹}0.4\) crore, a second loss zone, because the second line's step of ₹5.7 crore exceeds the ₹5.2 crore of profit earned at the top of the two-shift band.
  • At 2,00,000 units EBIT \(= 20.0 - 12.5 = \text{₹}7.5\) crore.

The plant therefore has three break-even points (40,000, 68,000 and 1,25,000 units) and two loss zones above the first one (60,001–67,999 and 1,20,001–1,24,999 units).

The decision lesson: if demand is expected at 65,000 units, running a second shift is value-destroying unless the extra 5,000 units can be priced higher or the step can be made smaller (overtime, contract labour, subcontracting the overflow). Profit at 60,000 units (₹2.0 crore) exceeds profit at 65,000 units (−₹0.3 crore).

Minimum efficient volume above a step

For a step to be worth taking, incremental contribution above the threshold must cover the step:

\[ Q_{\min} - C_k \ge \frac{S_k}{P - V} \]

In the illustration, the second shift needs at least 2,800 extra units beyond 60,000 just to restore EBIT to its single-shift level (₹2.8 crore step \(\div\) ₹1,000), that is 62,800 units to break even on the step decision, and 68,000 units for overall break-even. These are two different questions.

Break-even utilisation with steps

For capacity planning, express each BEP as utilisation of installed capacity:

\[ \text{BE utilisation} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs at that capacity}}{\text{CM per unit} \times \text{Installed capacity}} \]

For the full three-shift, two-line configuration (capacity 2,00,000 units): BE utilisation \(= 12.5 \text{ crore} \div (1{,}000 \times 2{,}00{,}000) = 62.5\%\). This is the formula used in the Week 21 exercise.

Excel implementation

Model steps with a lookup table, not nested IFs:

=XLOOKUP(Volume, Band_Lower, Band_Fixed, , -1)      ' fixed cost for the band (next smaller match)
=Volume*CM_unit - XLOOKUP(Volume, Band_Lower, Band_Fixed, , -1)   ' EBIT

Band_Lower holds the lower bound of each band (0, 60,001, 1,20,001). To find all break-evens, compute, for each band, Band_Fixed / CM_unit and flag it valid only if it lies within that band's limits. A chart of EBIT against volume (in steps of 1,000 units) makes the saw-tooth visible and is far more persuasive to management than a table.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Steps are lumpy in time as well as volume: a second line takes 12–18 months to build. A demand forecast that crosses the threshold must be checked against lead time, and the step is a capex decision (t3), not just a cost line.
  • Hysteresis: once a second shift is hired, removing it is costly (retrenchment compensation, morale, union agreements). Steps are easier to add than to remove, so downside scenarios should not assume instant reversal.
  • Partial steps: overtime, temporary contract labour and outsourcing can convert a fixed step into a variable premium for small overshoots. Compare the variable premium with the step's cost per unit at expected volume.
  • Semi-variable within the step: shift additions also change variable costs (night-shift power tariffs, shift allowances per hour). Re-estimate \(V\) by band.
  • Utilisation norms: lenders typically assume 60–85% utilisation in steady state for plants. A business plan that requires >90% utilisation to cover a new step deserves scepticism.

Key takeaways

  • Step-fixed costs make the profit line saw-toothed, so a business can have several break-even points.
  • Profit can fall when volume crosses a threshold; check whether incremental volume above the threshold covers the step.
  • Compute a BEP for each band and keep it only if it lies inside that band.
  • Model steps with a lookup table and chart EBIT against volume to make the thresholds visible.
  • Distinguish break-even of the step decision from overall break-even, and remember lead times and the difficulty of removing steps.

Practice Questions

Q1. Multiple break-evens [L1]

A packaging unit sells at ₹40 per kg with variable cost of ₹28 per kg. Fixed costs are ₹2.4 crore up to 25 lakh kg, ₹3.9 crore from 25 lakh to 40 lakh kg and ₹5.6 crore from 40 lakh to 60 lakh kg. Find every valid break-even point.

Q2. Should we add a shift? [L2]

Using the Chennai illustration in these notes, demand for next year is forecast at 70,000 units. Alternatively, the plant can subcontract any volume above 60,000 units at a total variable cost of ₹2,150 per unit (no extra fixed cost). Compare EBIT under (a) a second shift and (b) subcontracting, and state the volume at which you would be indifferent.

💡 Hint: The indifference volume equates the step cost with the extra variable cost of subcontracting.

Q3. Break-even utilisation with steps [L2]

A new plant has installed capacity of 3 lakh tonnes. At up to 1.8 lakh tonnes it runs two shifts with fixed costs of ₹54 crore; above that it runs three shifts with fixed costs of ₹69 crore. CM is ₹3,500 per tonne. Compute break-even utilisation for each configuration and the minimum utilisation that makes the third shift worthwhile.

Q4. Excel build [L2]

Write out the column layout and formulas (using XLOOKUP or LOOKUP) for a worksheet that takes a volume input, returns fixed costs from a band table with four bands, computes EBIT, and lists all valid break-even points with a flag. Explain how you would test it.

Q5. Board judgement [L3]

The sales head wants to commit to a new OEM contract that would take volume from 58,000 to 64,000 units in the Chennai plant from these notes, at a price 4% below list. Prepare the analysis you would present to the board, including the effect of the shift step, the price concession, the hysteresis risk if the OEM volume is not renewed after two years, and your recommendation framework.
