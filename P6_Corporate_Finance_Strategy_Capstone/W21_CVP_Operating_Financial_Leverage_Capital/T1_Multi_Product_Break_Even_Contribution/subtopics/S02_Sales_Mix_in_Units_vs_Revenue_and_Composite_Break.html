Sales Mix in Units vs Revenue and Composite Break-Even

Scope: Sales-mix definitions: mix in units vs mix in revenue, and why the two give different composite break-evens

Why it matters

Almost no Indian business sells one product. A cement company sells OPC, PPC and premium composite cement. An FMCG company sells a portfolio of SKUs with very different margins. A hospital chain sells bed-days, OPD consultations and pharmacy. Whenever you quote "the" break-even of such a business, you are quietly assuming a sales mix. Business-unit heads often quote the mix as "60:40" without saying whether that is 60:40 in units, tonnes or rupees. The two readings give different break-evens, and the difference can be large when products differ in price. Getting the definition right is the first control in any multi-product CVP or flexible-budget model (and in sales-mix variances, Weeks 18/19).

Core notes

Two definitions of mix

Unit (physical) mix: the proportion of each product in the total number of units (or tonnes, litres, cases) sold:

\[ w_i^{\text{units}} = \frac{Q_i}{\sum_j Q_j} \]

Revenue (rupee) mix: the proportion of each product in total sales value:

\[ w_i^{\text{rev}} = \frac{P_i Q_i}{\sum_j P_j Q_j} \]

where \(Q_i\) is the quantity and \(P_i\) the price of product \(i\). The two are linked through price: products with a high price have a larger revenue share than unit share.

Matching each mix to the right margin measure

  • Unit mix pairs with CM per unit: weighted-average CM per unit \(= \sum w_i^{\text{units}} (P_i - V_i)\). Break-even comes out in total units, which you then split by the unit mix.
  • Revenue mix pairs with the P/V ratio: weighted P/V \(= \sum w_i^{\text{rev}} \times \text{PV}_i\). Break-even comes out in rupees, which you then split by the revenue mix.

Both routes give the same break-even if the revenue mix used is the one implied by the unit mix at current prices. They give different answers when someone holds one of the two mixes constant and calls it "the mix". Holding 60:40 constant in units is a different business plan from holding 60:40 constant in rupees.

Illustration: same "60:40", two break-evens

A paint company sells two products, with fixed costs of ₹36 crore per year.

Product Price (₹/litre) Variable cost (₹/litre) CM (₹/litre) P/V
Economy emulsion (E) 200 140 60 30%
Premium emulsion (P) 500 300 200 40%

Reading A: mix is 60:40 in litres. Weighted CM per litre \(= 0.6 \times 60 + 0.4 \times 200 = \text{₹}116\). BEP \(= 36{,}00{,}00{,}000 \div 116 = 31.03\) lakh litres, of which E is 18.62 lakh and P is 12.41 lakh litres. BEP revenue \(= 18.62 \times 200 + 12.41 \times 500\) (₹ lakh) \(\approx \text{₹}99.3\) crore. The implied revenue mix is \(120 : 200\), that is 37.5% E and 62.5% P.

Reading B: mix is 60:40 in rupees. Weighted P/V \(= 0.6 \times 30\% + 0.4 \times 40\% = 34\%\). BEP \(= 36 \div 0.34 = \text{₹}105.9\) crore, of which E is ₹63.5 crore (31.8 lakh litres) and P is ₹42.4 crore (8.5 lakh litres).

Reading B carries more economy product and therefore a lower blended margin, so its break-even is about ₹6.6 crore higher. Neither answer is "wrong". They describe different mixes. The error is to compute one and describe it as the other.

Why the two differ, in words

The P/V ratio of the basket is a revenue-weighted average of the individual P/V ratios. CM per unit is a unit-weighted average of individual CMs. If the unit mix is held constant and you convert to a revenue mix, the high-priced product gains weight. So any time prices differ across products, the same nominal percentages applied to units and to rupees give different baskets.

Which mix to use

Situation Natural mix Reason
Common physical unit (tonnes of steel grades, litres of paint) Units Capacity and logistics are planned in units
Heterogeneous products (hotel rooms vs F&B covers) Revenue No meaningful common unit
Budget prepared by sales value (FMCG category plans) Revenue Plans and incentives are set in ₹
Plant capacity / utilisation analysis Units Break-even utilisation is a physical concept
Segment reporting (Ind AS 108) data only Revenue Segment notes give revenue and results, not units

Converting between mixes

From units to revenue: multiply each unit share by its price and renormalise. From revenue to units: divide each revenue share by its price and renormalise. In Excel, with unit mix in C5:C7 and prices in D5:D7:

=C5*D5/SUMPRODUCT($C$5:$C$7,$D$5:$D$7)      ' revenue share of product 1
=(F5/D5)/SUMPRODUCT($F$5:$F$7,1/$D$5:$D$7)  ' unit share from revenue share in F

The second formula needs array evaluation in older Excel versions; in Microsoft 365 it evaluates natively.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mix by contribution is a third reading, sometimes used informally ("Premium gives 60% of contribution"). Never use it as a weight in a break-even formula without converting.
  • Bundled / joint products: where products are sold together in fixed physical proportions (a cement bag and its delivery, or joint products from a refinery), the unit mix is technologically fixed and the basket method (s03) is the natural approach.
  • Price changes alter the revenue mix even when the unit mix is unchanged. In a price-cut scenario, revisit which mix you are holding constant.
  • Promotional SKUs and free goods: free units carry variable cost but no revenue. Include them in the unit mix and in variable cost, or the basket margin is overstated.
  • Discounts and schemes in FMCG (trade schemes, cash discounts) must be netted off price before computing either mix. Ind AS 115 variable consideration means reported revenue is already net, but MIS "gross sales" may not be.

Key takeaways

  • Unit mix pairs with CM per unit; revenue mix pairs with the P/V ratio. Do not cross them.
  • The same nominal percentages applied to units and to rupees describe different baskets whenever prices differ.
  • Holding the unit mix constant means a shift in revenue mix towards high-price products, and vice versa.
  • Always state the mix basis in the model's assumptions block and in management commentary.
  • Convert mixes using prices, and reconcile both break-even routes to the same answer as a model check.

Practice Questions

Q1. Converting a unit mix to a revenue mix [L1]

A tyre maker sells two-wheeler tyres at ₹1,800, passenger-car tyres at ₹4,500 and truck tyres at ₹18,000, in a unit mix of 60:30:10. Compute the revenue mix to one decimal place.

Q2. Two readings of the same mix [L2]

A pharma formulations company has fixed costs of ₹54 crore. Product A sells at ₹120 per strip with variable cost of ₹72. Product B sells at ₹300 per strip with variable cost of ₹150. The sales head states that the mix is 70:30. Compute break-even sales in ₹ crore assuming (a) a 70:30 unit mix and (b) a 70:30 revenue mix, and explain the gap.

💡 Hint: Compute weighted CM per strip for (a) and weighted P/V for (b); then express both in rupees.

Q3. Model control [L2]

Design a check cell for an Excel CVP model that confirms the unit-based and revenue-based break-even calculations agree, and describe what inputs must be consistent for the check to pass.

Q4. Choosing the mix basis for a hotel [L3]

A Goa resort earns revenue from rooms (₹9,000 per room-night, variable cost ₹2,500), F&B (₹1,200 per cover, variable cost ₹600) and spa (₹3,500 per session, variable cost ₹1,400). Annual fixed costs are ₹18 crore. Explain which mix basis you would use for the break-even, what data you would need from the property's PMS/POS, and how you would present the result to the owner.
