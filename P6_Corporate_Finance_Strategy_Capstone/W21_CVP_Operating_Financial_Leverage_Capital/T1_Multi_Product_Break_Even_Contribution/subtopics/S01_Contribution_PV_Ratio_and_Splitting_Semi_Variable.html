Subtopic note

Contribution, P/V Ratio and Splitting Semi-Variable Costs

Scope: Contribution per unit, P/V (CM) ratio and segregation of semi-variable costs (high-low, regression) before CVP work

Why it matters

Every cost-volume-profit (CVP) number you produce in FP&A, whether a break-even chart for a plant review or a volume-sensitivity table in a budget pack, rests on one prior step: splitting costs into fixed and variable parts. If power, maintenance, freight or sales-force costs are mis-split, contribution is wrong, the P/V ratio is wrong, and every break-even point, margin of safety and DOL built on them is wrong. Cost ledgers in Tally or SAP are organised by nature (raw material, power, salaries, repairs) as Schedule III requires. They are not organised by behaviour, so the analyst has to reclassify them. Auditors and lenders' engineers (TEV studies) also test whether a project's break-even claims rest on a defensible cost split.

Core notes

Contribution per unit

Contribution is the amount each unit adds towards covering fixed costs and then profit:

\[ \text{CM per unit} = P - V \]

where \(P\) is the net realisation per unit (after trade discounts, before GST, which is a pass-through) and \(V\) is the variable cost per unit. Variable cost includes direct materials, direct labour where it is genuinely piece-rated, variable power and fuel, packing, outward freight, sales commission and any royalty that varies with volume. Under the marginal costing view from your CMA syllabus, fixed manufacturing overhead is a period cost, not a product cost, and that is why CVP work starts from contribution and not from absorption-costing gross margin.

Total contribution \(= (P - V) \times Q\). Profit (EBIT) \(=\) Total contribution \(-\) Fixed costs.

P/V ratio (contribution-margin ratio)

\[ \text{P/V ratio} = \frac{P - V}{P} = \frac{\text{Total contribution}}{\text{Total sales}} = \frac{\Delta \text{Profit}}{\Delta \text{Sales}} \]

The last form matters in practice. When you only have two periods of P&L data and the selling price and cost structure did not change, the change in profit divided by the change in sales gives the P/V ratio directly. Fixed costs cancel out of the difference. The P/V ratio is the rupee-based measure, used when products have different unit scales (tonnes of cement against bags of putty) or when you only know revenue.

Why semi-variable costs must be split first

Semi-variable (mixed) costs have a fixed component plus a variable rate. Examples are power with a fixed demand charge plus an energy charge per kWh, maintenance contracts with a retainer plus per-call fees, telecom, and supervisory salaries plus overtime. Leaving them wholly in fixed costs understates \(V\), overstates the P/V ratio and understates the break-even point. Leaving them wholly in variable costs does the opposite.

High-low method

Use the periods with the highest and lowest activity (not the highest and lowest cost):

\[ b = \frac{\text{Cost}_{\text{high}} - \text{Cost}_{\text{low}}}{\text{Activity}_{\text{high}} - \text{Activity}_{\text{low}}}, \qquad a = \text{Cost}_{\text{high}} - b \times \text{Activity}_{\text{high}} \]

where \(b\) is the variable rate per unit of activity and \(a\) the fixed cost per period.

Illustration. Monthly power cost at a Pune auto-component unit:

Month Machine hours Power cost (₹ lakh)
Apr 8,000 14.0
Jul 12,000 19.2
Oct 6,000 11.4
Jan 10,000 16.6

High = Jul (12,000 hours), Low = Oct (6,000 hours). \(b = (19.2 - 11.4) \div 6{,}000 = \text{₹}130\) per machine hour. \(a = 19.2 - 12{,}000 \times 0.0130 = \text{₹}3.6\) lakh per month. The weakness is visible: only two observations are used, and if either is an outlier (a breakdown month or a tariff revision) the split is distorted.

Least-squares regression

Regression uses all observations and minimises squared errors:

\[ b = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{n \sum x^2 - \left(\sum x\right)^2}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x} \]

where \(x\) is activity, \(y\) is cost, \(n\) is the number of observations and the bars denote means. Check \(R^2\) (explanatory power), the t-statistic on \(b\), and plot the residuals. A low \(R^2\) means the chosen activity driver is wrong (for instance, power may track machine hours better than units produced).

In Excel:

=SLOPE(C2:C25, B2:B25)        ' variable rate b
=INTERCEPT(C2:C25, B2:B25)    ' fixed cost a per period
=RSQ(C2:C25, B2:B25)          ' goodness of fit
=LINEST(C2:C25, B2:B25, TRUE, TRUE)   ' full statistics incl. standard errors

For multiple drivers (power driven by both machine hours and number of shifts), use LINEST with several x-columns or the Analysis ToolPak regression. In Power BI, a scatter chart with a trend line gives a quick visual check, but compute the coefficients in Power Query or Excel for audit trail.

Other segregation approaches

  • Account analysis / engineering estimates: classify each GL head by judgement, or derive from technical norms (kWh per tonne). This is fast, but subjective.
  • Scatter-graph: a visual fit used to spot outliers before regression.
  • Tariff decomposition: for power and telecom, the bill itself shows fixed demand charges and variable energy charges, and this is often more accurate than statistics.

Relevant range

Every split is valid only within the relevant range of activity observed. Extrapolating a regression fitted on 60–85% utilisation down to 30% or up to 110% is unsafe: overtime premia, extra shifts and step costs change the behaviour (see s08).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Labour: in Indian manufacturing, permanent workforce wages are largely fixed in the short run (Industrial Disputes Act retrenchment constraints, standing orders). Contract labour is closer to variable. Do not treat all "direct labour" as variable by default.
  • Inflation in the data: if the series spans tariff revisions or wage settlements, restate costs to current prices before regressing. Otherwise inflation shows up as a spurious variable rate.
  • Discretionary fixed costs (advertising, R&D) correlate with sales only because management budgets them that way. Regression will call them variable, but they are policy-fixed.
  • Lagged costs: maintenance often follows activity with a lag. Regressing same-month figures understates \(b\).
  • GST: input tax credit-eligible GST is excluded from both price and cost. Ineligible credits (blocked under Sec 17(5) CGST) are part of cost.
  • Negative intercept in a regression is a sign of extrapolation beyond the relevant range or of a missing driver. It is not a "negative fixed cost".

Key takeaways

  • Contribution \(= P - V\); the P/V ratio \(=\) contribution \(\div\) sales \(= \Delta\text{Profit} \div \Delta\text{Sales}\) when price and cost structure are stable.
  • Segregate semi-variable costs before any CVP calculation. Mis-splitting biases break-even in a predictable direction.
  • High-low uses the activity extremes and is quick but outlier-prone. Regression uses all data and gives fit statistics.
  • Choose the activity driver that best explains the cost, and validate it with \(R^2\) and residuals.
  • Cost behaviour holds only within the relevant range, so flag extrapolation in any model.

Practice Questions

Q1. High-low split of maintenance cost [L1]

A Gujarat chemicals unit reports maintenance cost of ₹22.5 lakh at 15,000 reactor hours (highest activity month), ₹13.5 lakh at 9,000 hours (lowest activity month) and ₹25.0 lakh at 13,500 hours (a month with an unplanned shutdown repair). Compute the variable rate per reactor hour and the monthly fixed cost using high-low, and state why the ₹25.0 lakh month should or should not influence your choice.

Q2. P/V ratio from two periods [L1]

A Kolkata FMCG distributor reports sales of ₹48 crore and EBIT of ₹3.6 crore in FY24, and sales of ₹56 crore and EBIT of ₹5.2 crore in FY25, with no price change or cost restructuring. Compute the P/V ratio, the annual fixed costs and the break-even sales.

💡 Hint: Use the change in profit over the change in sales, then back out fixed costs from either year.

Q3. Regression in Excel and diagnostics [L2]

You have 24 months of power cost (₹ lakh) and three candidate drivers: tonnes produced, machine hours and number of shifts operated. Describe the Excel build (functions, layout) to choose the best driver, the statistics you would report to the plant head, and what a negative intercept on one of the drivers would tell you.

Q4. Effect of mis-classification on break-even [L2]

A product sells at ₹500 per unit with variable cost of ₹300 per unit and fixed costs of ₹60 lakh per year. An analyst discovers that ₹20 lakh treated as fixed is actually a variable cost of ₹40 per unit at the current volume of 5 lakh units. Recompute contribution per unit, the P/V ratio and break-even units under the corrected split, and explain the direction of the original error.

Q5. Labour cost behaviour judgement [L3]

A steel re-rolling mill in Raipur employs 180 permanent workers and 120 contract workers, and pays overtime in peak months. The CFO wants all wages treated as variable in the FY27 CVP model. Write a short memo arguing how each element of labour cost should be classified, citing the practical constraints in Indian labour law and how you would evidence the split from payroll data.
