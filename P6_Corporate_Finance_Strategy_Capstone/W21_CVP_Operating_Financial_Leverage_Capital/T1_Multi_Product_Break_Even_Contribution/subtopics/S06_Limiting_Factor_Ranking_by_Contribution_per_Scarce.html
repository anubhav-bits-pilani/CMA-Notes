Subtopic note

Limiting-Factor Ranking by Contribution per Scarce Unit

Scope: Limiting-factor (key factor) ranking: contribution per machine hour / per kg of scarce input

Why it matters

Break-even and target-profit analysis assume that you can sell and make whatever mix you plan. In practice, something binds: a bottleneck machine, a quota of imported resin, a shortage of skilled welders, a power allocation in a state with load-shedding, or shelf space in modern trade. When one resource is scarce, the profit-maximising mix is no longer the mix with the highest contribution per unit. It is the mix that earns the most contribution per unit of the scarce resource. FP&A teams use this logic in S&OP (sales and operations planning) meetings, allocation of constrained capacity across customers, and in the case for debottlenecking capex. It also connects to throughput accounting and the theory of constraints (see the topic's related items).

Core notes

The rule for a single limiting factor

  1. Identify the binding constraint. Check every resource: compare the requirement at maximum demand with the availability. If more than one resource binds, use linear programming (below).
  2. Compute contribution per unit of the scarce resource for each product:

\[ \text{CM per unit of key factor}_i = \frac{P_i - V_i}{r_i} \]

where \(r_i\) is the quantity of scarce resource (machine hours, kg of input) consumed per unit of product \(i\).

  1. Rank products in descending order of this ratio.
  2. Allocate the scarce resource to products in rank order up to their demand limits (and after meeting any minimum contractual or strategic quantities first).
  3. Fixed costs do not change the ranking, because they are incurred regardless of the mix within the relevant range.

Illustration: machine hours

A Coimbatore pump manufacturer has 30,000 hours on its CNC machining centre per year. Fixed costs are ₹9 crore.

Pump Price (₹) Variable cost (₹) CM per unit (₹) CNC hours per unit CM per CNC hour (₹) Max demand (units) Rank
Domestic (D) 6,000 4,200 1,800 0.5 3,600 24,000 1
Agricultural (A) 18,000 12,600 5,400 2.0 2,700 8,000 3
Industrial (I) 45,000 30,000 15,000 5.0 3,000 2,000 2

Hours needed at full demand \(= 12{,}000 + 16{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 38{,}000\), against 30,000 available, so CNC hours bind.

Allocation: D uses 12,000 hours (24,000 units). I uses 10,000 hours (2,000 units). The remaining 8,000 hours go to A, giving 4,000 units.

Total CM \(= 24{,}000 \times 1{,}800 + 2{,}000 \times 15{,}000 + 4{,}000 \times 5{,}400 = \text{₹}9.48\) crore. EBIT \(= 9.48 - 9.00 = \text{₹}0.48\) crore.

Ranking by CM per unit would have put I first, A second and D last. With the same 30,000 hours (I 10,000, A 16,000, leaving 4,000 hours for 8,000 units of D), total CM would be ₹3.0 + ₹4.32 + ₹1.44 \(= \text{₹}8.76\) crore, which is ₹72 lakh worse.

Scarce material (per kg)

The same logic applies to a scarce input. For example, a specialty polymer allocated by a single domestic supplier: rank by CM per kg of polymer. If the scarcity is temporary and an import route exists at a premium, the decision becomes a relevant-costing question. Import if the extra cost per kg is less than the CM per kg forgone on the marginal product.

Shadow price (opportunity cost of the constraint)

The CM per unit of key factor of the marginal product (the last one partly produced) is the shadow price of the constraint. In the illustration, one extra CNC hour is worth ₹2,700 (it would make half an agricultural pump). This number:

  • sets the maximum premium worth paying for overtime, subcontracting or a second shift on that machine;
  • is the hurdle for any order that uses CNC hours, so a special order must earn at least its own variable cost plus ₹2,700 per CNC hour used;
  • feeds a debottlenecking capex case: annual value of extra hours \(\times\) shadow price, less incremental fixed costs, discounted (t3).

More than one constraint

When two or more resources bind (CNC hours and skilled assembly hours, say), single-ratio ranking can give conflicting orders. Formulate a linear programme: maximise \(\sum (P_i - V_i) Q_i\) subject to \(\sum r_{ki} Q_i \le R_k\) for each resource \(k\), and \(0 \le Q_i \le \text{Demand}_i\). Solve it with Excel Solver (Simplex LP). The Solver sensitivity report gives the shadow price of each constraint and the allowable range over which it holds.

Excel implementation

=CM_unit / Hours_per_unit                         ' ratio column
=RANK.EQ(G5, $G$5:$G$7, 0)                        ' rank
' Allocation with a running balance, sorted by rank:
=MIN(Demand*Hours_per_unit, Hours_available - SUM(prior allocations))
=Hours_allocated / Hours_per_unit                 ' units produced

For dynamic sorting, use SORTBY on the ratio in Microsoft 365, then a running SCAN or cumulative SUM to allocate hours.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Minimum quantities: contracts with OEMs or government tenders, or strategic SKUs that keep a dealer network loyal, must be scheduled first even if they rank low. Compute the cost of the constraint explicitly.
  • Demand interdependence: dropping the low-ranked product may lose a customer who buys the whole range. The ranking assumes independent demand.
  • The constraint moves: once you debottleneck CNC, assembly may become the binding constraint. Re-run the analysis at each capacity level.
  • Labour as a constraint: if skilled labour is the constraint and it is a fixed cost (permanent employees), labour cost is excluded from \(V\). Using it in \(V\) double-penalises labour-intensive products.
  • Short-run vs long-run: the ranking is a short-run tool. Over the long run, products that consistently rank low should be reviewed for pricing, redesign or exit.

Key takeaways

  • With one binding constraint, rank by contribution per unit of the scarce resource, not by CM per unit or P/V.
  • Fixed costs are irrelevant to the ranking within the relevant range.
  • The ratio of the marginal product is the shadow price, which caps what you should pay to relax the constraint.
  • Multiple binding constraints need linear programming (Excel Solver), not ratio ranking.
  • Strategic minimums and demand linkages can override the pure ranking, but quantify the cost of doing so.

Practice Questions

Q1. Ranking with scarce raw material [L1]

A Surat textile processor has 1,20,000 kg of imported dye intermediate for the year. Product X earns CM of ₹420 per metre using 0.6 kg; Y earns ₹300 using 0.3 kg; Z earns ₹650 using 1.3 kg. Maximum demand is 80,000, 1,50,000 and 60,000 metres respectively. Determine the optimal production plan and total contribution.

Q2. Shadow price and overtime [L2]

Using the pump illustration in these notes, the plant head proposes running the CNC centre for an extra 2,000 hours on overtime at an incremental cost of ₹1,900 per hour (labour premium, power and tooling). Evaluate the proposal and state the maximum premium per hour you would accept.

💡 Hint: Identify which pump the extra hours would produce and its contribution per CNC hour.

Q3. Special order under a constraint [L2]

Continuing the pump case, a state irrigation department offers to buy 500 agricultural pumps at ₹15,500 each (above the plan). Variable cost is unchanged. Should the company accept if CNC hours remain capped at 30,000? Show your reasoning.

Q4. Two binding constraints [L3]

A Pune auto-ancillary has 20,000 press hours and 15,000 assembly hours. Product P earns CM of ₹800 and uses 2 press hours and 1 assembly hour; product Q earns CM of ₹900 and uses 1 press hour and 2 assembly hours. Demand is unlimited. Set up the linear programme, describe how you would solve it in Excel Solver, and explain why ranking on either single ratio could mislead.

Q5. Strategic minimum [L3]

A company's lowest-ranked product is supplied to its largest dealer, who buys 30% of the company's high-margin range as well. Explain how you would quantify and present to the sales director the cost of keeping that product in the mix under a capacity constraint.
