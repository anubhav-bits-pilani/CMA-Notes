Subtopic note

Taxation of Dividends and Buybacks: DDT, Sec 115QA and the 2024 Shift

Scope: Shareholder taxation changes: DDT abolished (FY21), buyback tax moved from company (Sec 115QA) to shareholders from 1 Oct 2024; verify latest Finance Act

Why it matters

Whether a company returns cash as a dividend or a buyback used to be largely a company-level tax arbitrage. Under Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT), dividends cost the company about 20.56% extra. Buybacks of listed shares were for a long time entirely tax-free in the hands of shareholders. Successive Finance Acts removed that arbitrage and then moved the burden to shareholders. For an FP&A or IR professional, the after-tax value received by each shareholder class now drives the choice between a dividend and a buyback, and so does who actually participates in a tender offer. Promoters, resident individuals, mutual funds, FPIs and corporates are each taxed differently. Analysts comparing historical payout policies (FY15–FY25) must also adjust for the regime that applied in each year.

Core notes

Timeline of regimes

Period Dividends Buybacks (listed company)
Up to 31 Mar 2020 DDT u/s 115-O at an effective ~20.56% on the grossed-up amount, paid by the company. Exempt for most shareholders u/s 10(34), with Sec 115BBDA taxing resident non-corporate recipients above ₹10 lakh at 10% Before 5 Jul 2019: listed buybacks outside Sec 115QA, so shareholders paid capital gains (often exempt LTCG before 2018). From 5 Jul 2019: Sec 115QA extended to listed companies, company pays 23.296%, shareholders exempt u/s 10(34A)
1 Apr 2020 to 30 Sep 2024 DDT abolished (Finance Act 2020). Dividends taxable in the shareholder's hands. TDS u/s 194 / 195 Sec 115QA: company pays 20% + 12% surcharge + 4% cess = 23.296% on "distributed income". Shareholders exempt
From 1 Oct 2024 Unchanged: shareholder taxation Sec 115QA no longer applies. Buyback consideration is a deemed dividend in shareholders' hands (Sec 2(22)(f)) and is taxed at the applicable rate. Cost of acquisition is a capital loss (Sec 46A read with Sec 48)

Dividends after DDT abolition (from FY21)

  • Resident individuals and HUFs are taxed at slab rates, up to 30% plus surcharge (surcharge on dividend income is capped at 15%) plus 4% cess. Interest expense to earn the dividend is deductible up to 20% of the dividend (Sec 57). Nothing else is deductible.
  • TDS u/s 194 is 10% for resident shareholders where dividends exceed a threshold. The threshold was ₹5,000, raised to ₹10,000 from 1 April 2025 by Finance Act 2025.
  • Domestic companies pay tax at their corporate rate (25.168% under Sec 115BAA). Sec 80M deduction is available for dividends received to the extent they are distributed onward at least one month before the return due date. This prevents cascading in holding structures.
  • Non-residents and FPIs are taxed at 20% u/s 115A plus surcharge and cess, or at the lower DTAA rate (often 10–15%) if a Tax Residency Certificate and Form 10F are provided. TDS u/s 195 or 196D applies.

Sec 115QA regime (company-level buyback tax), up to 30 September 2024

Distributed income was the consideration paid by the company less the amount received by the company when it issued the shares. It was not the shareholder's cost. The company paid 23.296% on this as a final tax, not deductible, and the shareholder was exempt. For an IT major buying back at ₹1,800 shares originally issued at ₹5, almost the whole payout was taxed at 23.296%. That matched or exceeded the DDT burden, which removed the earlier arbitrage. In the company's Ind AS books, buyback tax was debited directly to equity, since it arose from a transaction with owners.

The 1 October 2024 shift (Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024)

For buybacks on or after 1 October 2024:

  1. The entire buyback consideration is deemed dividend in the shareholder's hands under Sec 2(22)(f), taxed at slab or applicable rates, with no deduction for cost.
  2. The shareholder's cost of acquisition of the shares bought back is treated as a capital loss. The capital gain is computed as nil consideration less cost. It is short- or long-term depending on the holding period, can be set off against capital gains, and can be carried forward for 8 years.
  3. The company deducts TDS u/s 194 at 10% for residents and at applicable rates for non-residents. It no longer pays Sec 115QA tax.
  4. Effect. High-slab individuals pay up to ~35.9% (30% + 15% surcharge + 4% cess) on the gross proceeds. The capital loss is valuable only if they have capital gains to absorb it. Long-term capital gains on listed equity are taxed at 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh (from 23 July 2024), so the loss is worth at most 12.5% or 20% of cost.

Illustration. A resident individual in the 30% slab (surcharge 15%, cess 4%; effective 35.88%) tenders 1,000 shares at ₹1,500 each. The shares were bought at ₹900 more than 12 months ago. They have other LTCG of ₹10 lakh.

Item ₹
Buyback proceeds, deemed dividend 15,00,000
Tax at 35.88% 5,38,200
Capital loss (cost) 9,00,000
Tax saved by setting off against LTCG at 12.5% + surcharge + cess (≈ 14.95%) ≈ 1,34,550
Net tax on buyback ≈ 4,03,650
Compare: selling in the market, LTCG of 6,00,000 at ≈ 14.95% ≈ 89,700

The individual is far better off selling in the market than tendering. In tender offers after October 2024, therefore, high-slab residents often do not tender. Low-slab residents, exempt entities and some treaty-protected FPIs may find tendering attractive. The investor base that actually accepts the offer changes how much value is transferred, and to whom.

Corporate and promoter shareholders

A corporate promoter on 25.168% pays that rate on the deemed dividend. It can claim Sec 80M if it distributes onward, and it gets a capital loss for the cost. If it tenders its proportionate entitlement, its ownership is unchanged, so the buyback is economically a dividend plus a capital loss. That loss has value only if the promoter has capital gains to absorb it within 8 years. The old advantage has gone: before October 2024, the company paid 23.296% once and the promoter received the proceeds tax-free. Promoter groups that used to prefer buybacks are now roughly indifferent, or prefer dividends if the capital loss is unusable and the buyback carries extra transaction costs.

Verify the latest Finance Act

This area changes frequently. Before advising, check:

  • The Income-tax Act, 2025, which replaces the 1961 Act for tax years beginning on or after 1 April 2026. Section numbers (2(22)(f), 46A, 115QA, 194, 80M) are renumbered in the new Act. Use the official concordance table rather than assuming the old numbers.
  • The Finance Act, 2026. Budget 2026 proposals sought to tax buyback consideration again as capital gains for shareholders, with an additional levy on promoters. Confirm the enacted text, the effective date and the rates before modelling any buyback from FY27.
  • Surcharge caps, Sec 194 thresholds and DTAA positions for FPIs.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Ind AS presentation. After October 2024 there is no company-level buyback tax to debit to equity. TDS withheld is a liability until deposited.
  • Mutual funds are exempt (Sec 10(23D)), so tendering is tax-efficient for them. This skews acceptance towards institutions.
  • Small-shareholder reservation. Small holders in low slabs may now face higher tax than under 115QA. Communicate this in the letter of offer.
  • Comparing historical payout ratios. Pre-FY21 dividends were declared net of a company-level DDT cost. Gross up by about 20.56% when comparing distribution intensity across regimes.
  • Unlisted companies. Sec 115QA applied to them from 2013 and the October 2024 shift applies equally. Check the treatment of shares issued at a premium.

Key takeaways

  • DDT was abolished for dividends distributed from 1 April 2020 (FY21). Dividends are now taxed in shareholders' hands, with TDS u/s 194 or 195.
  • Sec 115QA (23.296% at company level) applied to listed buybacks from 5 July 2019 to 30 September 2024.
  • From 1 October 2024, buyback proceeds are a deemed dividend for shareholders, and the cost becomes a capital loss.
  • High-slab individuals usually prefer to sell in the market rather than tender. Exempt and low-tax holders prefer to tender.
  • Always confirm the Income-tax Act 2025 numbering and any Finance Act 2026 changes before modelling.

Practice Questions

Q1. Regime identification [L1]

For each event, state who pays tax, at what rate, and under which provision: (a) final dividend paid in August 2019, (b) interim dividend paid in November 2021, (c) buyback completed in June 2023, (d) buyback completed in December 2024.

Q2. Tender vs sell decision [L2]

A resident individual in the highest slab (effective 35.88% on dividends) holds 2,000 shares bought 3 years ago at ₹420. A tender offer is at ₹700, while the market price is ₹660. They have no other capital gains this year or in carried-forward losses. LTCG on listed equity is 12.5% plus 4% cess above ₹1.25 lakh. Compare the after-tax cash from tendering all 2,000 shares with selling all 2,000 in the market.

💡 Hint: Without other gains, the capital loss from tendering only has carry-forward value. Treat it explicitly.

Q3. Corporate promoter analysis [L2]

A corporate promoter (Sec 115BAA) holds 55% of a listed company that will distribute ₹1,000 crore. The promoter's cost of the shares is ₹40 crore, and it plans to pass on 60% of whatever it receives as dividend to its own shareholders within the Sec 80M time limit. Compare the promoter's tax under a dividend versus a buyback in which it tenders its proportionate entitlement.

Q4. Historical payout comparison [L2]

A company paid dividends of ₹400 crore in FY19 (with DDT) and ₹480 crore in FY23. Adjust both to a comparable gross distribution measure and comment on whether distribution intensity rose or fell, given PAT of ₹1,600 crore and ₹2,100 crore respectively.

Q5. Advisory note under the new Act [L3]

You are asked to model a ₹2,000 crore distribution for FY27 (tax year 2026-27). Draft a checklist of the tax points you must verify under the Income-tax Act 2025 and the Finance Act 2026, and explain how each could change the dividend vs buyback recommendation.
