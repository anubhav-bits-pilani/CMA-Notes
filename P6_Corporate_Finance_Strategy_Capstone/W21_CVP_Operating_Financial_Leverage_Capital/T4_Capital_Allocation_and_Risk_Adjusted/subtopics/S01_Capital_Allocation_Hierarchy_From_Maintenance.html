Capital Allocation Hierarchy: From Maintenance Capex to Buybacks

Scope: Capital allocation hierarchy: maintenance capex, growth capex, M&A, debt reduction, dividends, buybacks

Why it matters

Every rupee of operating cash flow a company generates has to go somewhere. It can be reinvested in the existing asset base, used to build new capacity, spent on acquisitions, used to repay lenders, or returned to shareholders. How a board chooses among these uses decides long-run ROIC, the credit rating and the share price far more than quarterly margin moves do. In FP&A you will build the sources and uses of cash bridge in the long-range plan, draft the board note on "capex vs buyback", and defend the capex budget against a CFO who wants to protect the rating. In equity research and valuation, the capital allocation record (how much was reinvested, at what incremental return, and how much was handed back) is the main input into the reinvestment rate and growth assumptions of a DCF. Analysts covering NSE-listed groups also judge management quality largely on allocation discipline: idle cash on the balance sheet, value-destroying diversification and promoter-entity transactions all show up here.

Core notes

The hierarchy and the logic behind its order

Practitioners describe a rough order of claims on free cash flow. It is a decision sequence, not a law:

Rank Use Nature Typical test
1 Maintenance (sustaining) capex Keeps existing capacity, safety and compliance intact Is it needed to sustain current EBITDA? (Almost always yes)
2 Growth (expansion) capex New capacity, new products, debottlenecking NPV > 0 at a project-specific hurdle; ROIC > WACC
3 M&A Buying capacity, market share or capability NPV of synergies > control premium paid; post-deal leverage
4 Debt reduction Lowers fixed charges and financial risk After-tax Kd vs return on alternatives; rating/covenant headroom
5 Dividends Regular, sticky distribution Dividend distribution policy, Sec 123 profits, FCFE capacity
6 Buybacks Flexible, episodic distribution Price paid vs intrinsic value; Sec 68 limits; tax treatment

Maintenance capex comes first because skipping it quietly destroys the existing cash flow base: plant availability falls, safety incidents rise, and regulators (pollution control boards, PESO, DGMS) can shut lines. Growth capex and M&A compete for the same pool and must clear a hurdle rate. Debt reduction is next because it is a guaranteed, low-risk "return" equal to the after-tax cost of debt avoided. Distributions come last because they are what is left once all positive-NPV uses have been funded. The order is not fixed. A company in breach of covenants moves debt reduction to the top. A cash-rich IT services firm with no large capex needs may run almost all of its free cash flow through dividends and buybacks.

Maintenance vs growth capex: the split that matters

Companies rarely disclose the split. The cash flow statement shows "purchase of property, plant and equipment" as one line. Common ways to estimate maintenance capex are:

  • Depreciation proxy. Maintenance capex is roughly equal to depreciation. This is crude and understates it in inflationary periods, because depreciation is based on historical cost.
  • Capacity-based. Maintenance capex is whatever is spent beyond the cost of new capacity added. For example, total capex less (new tonnes of capacity × capex per tonne).
  • Management guidance. Cement and steel companies often guide sustaining capex in ₹ per tonne. Telecom companies split network capex into coverage (growth) and capacity/upgrade spend.
  • Greenwald method. Maintenance capex = total capex − (PPE-to-sales ratio × increase in sales).

The split matters because "owner earnings", or sustainable free cash flow, is operating cash flow less maintenance capex only. Growth capex is discretionary and has to be judged on its own return.

M&A in the hierarchy

M&A ranks below organic growth capex in most frameworks because organic capex is usually bought at replacement cost, while an acquisition is bought at market value plus a control premium. An acquisition creates value only if:

\[ \text{PV(synergies)} > \text{Price paid} - \text{Standalone intrinsic value of target} \]

Indian groups also face Competition Commission of India approval (including the deal-value threshold introduced in 2024), SEBI Takeover Regulations open offers for listed targets, and Ind AS 103 purchase price allocation, which creates goodwill that is tested for impairment under Ind AS 36. Week 22 covers acquisition debt capacity in detail.

Debt reduction, dividends and buybacks as "residual" uses

Once positive-NPV investment is funded, surplus cash should go to the uses whose risk-adjusted return beats what shareholders can earn elsewhere. There are three options:

  1. Debt reduction earns the after-tax cost of debt with almost no risk. It also has option value through a rating upgrade, lower Ke as beta falls, and covenant headroom (see s03).
  2. Dividends are a sticky commitment. Markets punish cuts, so companies set the regular dividend at a level they can sustain through the cycle (see s06).
  3. Buybacks are flexible and can be timed. They create value only if the price paid is below intrinsic value (see s04 and c04).

Sources and uses framework in the plan

In a 5-year plan, build the allocation as an explicit cash waterfall:

₹ crore FY26 FY27 FY28
Cash from operations (after tax, after WC) 1,800 2,050 2,300
Less: maintenance capex (450) (480) (500)
Discretionary free cash flow 1,350 1,570 1,800
Less: growth capex (approved projects) (900) (1,100) (400)
Less: scheduled debt repayment (200) (200) (200)
Less: dividend at policy payout (250) (280) (320)
Residual surplus / (deficit) 0 (10) 880
Allocation of residual: prepay / buyback / build cash — revolver draw to be decided

The residual line is where the board debate happens. FY28's ₹880 crore has to be tested three ways: prepayment (c05), buyback (c04) and holding cash for future capex. Each test runs against the risk-adjusted hurdle (c06) and the leverage target from topic t5.

Indian context

Indian companies show some patterns of their own:

  • PSU dividend guidelines. DIPAM's 2016 guidelines (revised in 2020 and later) require CPSEs to pay a minimum dividend, as a % of PAT or of net worth, whichever is higher, and to consider buybacks where cash and net worth are large. Allocation is therefore partly set by the government's fiscal needs.
  • Promoter-group transactions. Cash is sometimes routed to group entities as inter-corporate deposits or loans (Sec 185/186), or into unrelated diversification. Analysts discount this (see the related item on agency costs in the topic note).
  • IT services majors. Several have stated policies to return about 85–100% of free cash flow over a multi-year window through dividends and buybacks. This makes the hierarchy explicit.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Capex that is "maintenance" in name only. Debottlenecking that adds 8% capacity is often booked as maintenance. Re-classify it, or sustainable FCF will be understated and growth returns overstated.
  • Regulatory or ESG capex. Flue-gas desulphurisation for thermal power and effluent treatment plants earn no incremental revenue but are mandatory. Treat them as maintenance: the alternative is shutdown, so the NPV is measured against losing the whole cash flow, not against zero.
  • Counter-cyclical allocation. Buying assets in a downturn (distressed IBC assets in steel and cement) can be the highest-return use, but it competes with deleveraging precisely when the rating is under pressure.
  • Holding cash is a decision too. Cash earns a post-tax treasury yield, about 7% × (1 − 25.17%), which is well below WACC. Holding it is justified only as option value for known future capex or as a liquidity buffer.
  • Sequencing vs simultaneity. In practice, all six uses are funded at the same time within one budget. The hierarchy guides what gets cut first when cash is short: buybacks, then M&A, then growth capex. Maintenance capex is cut last.

Key takeaways

  • Maintenance capex comes first. Growth capex and M&A must clear project-specific hurdles. Distributions get the residual.
  • Estimate the maintenance/growth split explicitly, because owner earnings deduct only maintenance capex.
  • M&A needs synergies worth more than the premium paid over standalone value, and it adds regulatory and accounting complexity.
  • Debt reduction is a low-risk return equal to the after-tax cost of debt, plus rating and covenant option value.
  • Dividends are sticky and buybacks are flexible. Neither creates value by itself.
  • Model allocation as a sources-and-uses waterfall with an explicit residual line that is tested against the alternatives.

Practice Questions

Q1. Estimating maintenance capex [L1]

A cement company reports FY25 capex of ₹2,400 crore and depreciation of ₹1,100 crore. During the year it added 3.0 million tonnes of grinding and clinker capacity at a capex of ₹6,000 per tonne. Estimate maintenance capex using (a) the depreciation proxy and (b) the capacity-based approach. Explain why the two differ and which you would use in a DCF.

Q2. Building the allocation waterfall [L2]

A listed auto-component company forecasts the following for FY27: CFO after tax and WC of ₹950 crore, maintenance capex of ₹220 crore, approved growth capex of ₹380 crore, scheduled term-loan repayment of ₹120 crore, and a dividend policy of 30% of PAT, with PAT forecast at ₹600 crore. Gross debt is ₹900 crore at a pre-tax cost of 9.2%, and the tax rate is 25.168%. Build the waterfall, compute the residual, and list the three competing uses of the residual together with the test you would apply to each.

💡 Hint: Compute the residual first. Then match each alternative to its hurdle: after-tax Kd for prepayment, intrinsic value versus price for a buyback.

Q3. Regulatory capex classification [L2]

A thermal power generator must install FGD units costing ₹1,500 crore by FY28 or face closure of 2 × 500 MW units. The units generate EBITDA of ₹420 crore per year with 12 years of remaining life. The FGD adds ₹60 crore per year to operating costs. Explain how you would evaluate this capex in the allocation framework and why a standalone "NPV against zero" framing is wrong.

Q4. Board debate on surplus cash [L3]

An IT services company holds ₹12,000 crore of cash and liquid investments, earning 7.2% pre-tax. It has no debt, capex runs at 2% of revenue, and the stock trades at 24× earnings. The board is considering three options: (i) a ₹4,000 crore acquisition of a US digital firm, (ii) a ₹4,000 crore buyback, and (iii) a one-time special dividend. Draft a one-page memo setting out the decision criteria, the information you need, and how the hierarchy applies.

Q5. Interview: cut order under stress [L1]

In an interview you are asked: "Operating cash flow has just fallen 30% below plan. In what order would you cut the uses of cash, and why?" Prepare a structured two-minute answer.
