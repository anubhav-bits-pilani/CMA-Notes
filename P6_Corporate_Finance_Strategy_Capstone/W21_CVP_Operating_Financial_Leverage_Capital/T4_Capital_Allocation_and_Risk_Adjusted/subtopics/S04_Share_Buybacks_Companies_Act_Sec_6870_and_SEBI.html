Subtopic note

Share Buybacks: Companies Act Sec 68–70 and SEBI Regulations 2018

Scope: Buybacks: Companies Act Sec 68–70 limits, SEBI Buy-back Regulations 2018 (tender-offer route; open-market route phased out from 1 Apr 2025)

Why it matters

Buybacks are the flexible way for Indian companies to return cash. IT services majors, PSUs and cash-rich mid-caps use them regularly. Before a board can approve one, the finance team must confirm the legal headroom (how much can be bought back and from which reserves), choose the route, model the post-buyback debt-equity ratio on standalone and consolidated figures, prepare the declaration of solvency, and work out the capital redemption reserve entries. Auditors certify the computation of permissible capital. A wrong reserve base or a missed cooling period can stop the offer. The legal regime also changed sharply in 2023–2025: the open-market route was phased out and taxation moved to shareholders (s05). Older textbook notes are therefore partly out of date.

Core notes

Sources of funds (Sec 68(1))

A company may buy back its own shares or other specified securities only out of:

  1. its free reserves,
  2. the securities premium account, or
  3. the proceeds of an issue of shares or other specified securities. A buyback of a kind of shares cannot be funded from the proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind (Sec 68(1) proviso).

Free reserves (Sec 2(43)) are reserves available for distribution as dividend. They exclude unrealised gains, notional gains, revaluation reserve and fair-value changes on assets or liabilities. Under Ind AS this means excluding OCI reserves such as the FVOCI equity reserve and the cash-flow hedge reserve, as well as any deemed-cost fair-value uplift on transition that is unrealised. ICAI guidance and the facts matter here. Auditors typically require a reconciliation.

Conditions and limits (Sec 68(2))

Condition Requirement
Authorisation Authorised by the articles
Approval Special resolution. Board resolution suffices if the buyback is ≤ 10% of total paid-up equity capital + free reserves
Overall size ≤ 25% of aggregate paid-up capital + free reserves
Equity-specific cap Equity shares bought back in a financial year ≤ 25% of total paid-up equity capital of that year
Leverage Post-buyback ratio of aggregate secured and unsecured debt to paid-up capital + free reserves ≤ 2:1 (the Central Government may prescribe a higher ratio for a class of companies)
Shares Fully paid-up
Listed companies In accordance with SEBI regulations
Gap between offers No offer within one year of the preceding offer
Completion Within one year of the special/board resolution

Paid-up capital and free reserves are taken from the latest audited financial statements. For listed companies, SEBI rules require the leverage condition to be tested on both standalone and consolidated figures.

Process requirements (Sec 68(3)–(10))

  • Explanatory statement with full disclosure (Sec 68(3)).
  • Declaration of solvency filed in Form SH-9, signed by at least two directors, one of whom is the MD. The directors confirm a full inquiry and that the company can meet its liabilities and will not become insolvent within one year (Sec 68(6)).
  • Extinguish and physically destroy the securities within 7 days of the last date of completion (Sec 68(7)).
  • No fresh issue of the same kind of shares or securities within 6 months, except bonus issues and the discharge of subsisting obligations such as warrant or preference-share conversion, ESOPs and sweat equity (Sec 68(8)).
  • Maintain a register of securities bought back in Form SH-10 (Sec 68(9)). File a return with the RoC in Form SH-11 within 30 days of completion (Sec 68(10)).

Capital redemption reserve (Sec 69)

Where shares are bought back out of free reserves or securities premium, an amount equal to the nominal value of the shares bought back must be transferred to the capital redemption reserve (CRR). CRR can be used only to issue fully paid bonus shares.

Illustration. A buyback of 2 crore shares of ₹10 face value at ₹1,500 each costs ₹3,000 crore, funded from securities premium of ₹1,200 crore and retained earnings (free reserves).

Under Ind AS 32.33, consideration paid for own equity instruments is deducted from equity, with no gain or loss in P&L. Transaction costs (merchant banker, legal, stamp duty) are also deducted from equity, net of any tax effect. Buyback-tax treatment depends on the period (s05).

Prohibitions (Sec 70)

A company cannot buy back its shares:

  • through a subsidiary, including its own subsidiaries, or through an investment company or group of investment companies;
  • if it has defaulted in repaying deposits, interest, redemption of debentures or preference shares, dividends, or repayment of a term loan or interest to a bank or financial institution. It may do so once the default has been remedied and three years have elapsed;
  • if it has not complied with Sec 92 (annual return), 123 (dividend), 127 (payment of dividend) or 129 (financial statements).

SEBI (Buy-back of Securities) Regulations, 2018

For listed companies, the SEBI regulations add their own requirements:

  • Routes. (a) Tender offer to existing shareholders on a proportionate basis. (b) Open market, either through the stock-exchange mechanism or through book-building. Buyback from odd-lot holders is a rarely used route.
  • Phase-out of the open-market stock-exchange route. Following the 2023 amendments, the permitted size of open-market buybacks was reduced in steps through FY24 and FY25. From 1 April 2025, buybacks through the stock exchange open-market route are no longer permitted. The tender offer is now the main route. SEBI's concern was that the open-market route bought from whoever happened to be selling, so not all shareholders benefited equitably.
  • Tender offer features.
  • Fixed price. A 15% reservation for small shareholders, defined as those holding shares worth ≤ ₹2 lakh on the record date.
  • Promoters may participate. They must disclose their intention, and their post-buyback holding may trigger takeover-code considerations.
  • Timelines are compressed under the 2023 amendment: a shorter tendering period and faster settlement.
  • Escrow. The company must deposit a specified percentage of the offer size in escrow as security.
  • Post-buyback restrictions. No further capital may be raised for one year from the expiry of the buyback period, except to discharge subsisting obligations. Promoters cannot deal in shares during the buyback period.
  • Debt-equity test applied on both standalone and consolidated bases. Specified carve-outs for subsidiaries that are NBFCs or HFCs are prescribed in the regulations.
  • Public announcement and letter of offer, a merchant banker's due diligence, and the auditor's report on the permissible capital computation and the solvency opinion.

Computing the permissible buyback: worked example

From standalone audited figures at 31 March 2025 (₹ crore): paid-up equity capital 500 (face value ₹10, so 50 crore shares), securities premium 3,500, general reserve 2,000, retained earnings 8,000, FVOCI equity reserve 600, and total borrowings 1,200.

Step ₹ crore
Paid-up capital + free reserves (500 + 3,500 + 2,000 + 8,000; exclude FVOCI reserve) 14,000
25% limit (special resolution) 3,500
10% limit (board-only route) 1,400
Equity cap: 25% of paid-up equity = 12.5 crore shares Binding only if price ≤ ₹280 per share
Post-buyback D/E at ₹3,500 crore outlay: 1,200 ÷ (14,000 − 3,500) 0.11 : 1, well within 2:1

At a buyback price of ₹1,750, a ₹3,500 crore buyback buys 2 crore shares (4% of shares), so the 25%-of-equity cap does not bind. Note that securities premium is included in the base for the Companies Act limit.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Reserves under Ind AS. Retained earnings may include Ind AS transition adjustments, such as fair value as deemed cost, that are unrealised. Many auditors exclude them from free reserves. Agree the computation early.
  • Consolidated vs standalone D/E. A holding company with low standalone debt but heavily levered subsidiaries may pass Sec 68 yet fail the SEBI consolidated test.
  • Promoter participation and creeping acquisition. Non-participation by promoters raises their percentage holding. Check SEBI SAST exemptions for buyback-driven increases and minimum public shareholding (25%) under SCRR.
  • Interaction with QIP or ESOP plans. The 6-month (Companies Act) and 1-year (SEBI) fresh-issue restrictions can block a planned capital raise.
  • Unlisted companies follow Sec 68 and the Share Capital and Debentures Rules (Rule 17). SEBI rules do not apply.
  • Defaults in group companies do not trigger Sec 70 directly, but lenders' negative covenants may prohibit buybacks.

Key takeaways

  • Fund buybacks only from free reserves, securities premium or proceeds of a different kind of issue.
  • Limits: 25% of paid-up capital + free reserves (10% with board approval only), 25% of paid-up equity per year, post-buyback D/E ≤ 2:1, and a 1-year gap between offers.
  • Transfer the nominal value to CRR when funding from reserves or premium, and extinguish the shares within 7 days.
  • Sec 70 bars buybacks through subsidiaries and for 3 years after a remedied default.
  • Under SEBI, the stock-exchange open-market route has been unavailable since 1 April 2025. The tender offer remains, with a 15% small-shareholder reservation.

Practice Questions

Q1. Permissible buyback computation [L2]

At 31 March 2025 a listed company has (₹ crore): equity share capital 120 (face value ₹1), securities premium 900, general reserve 1,400, retained earnings 6,200 (including ₹350 crore of Ind AS deemed-cost uplift on land that is unrealised), cash-flow hedge reserve 80, and borrowings 2,400 standalone and 9,800 consolidated (consolidated capital + free reserves of ₹10,500 crore). Compute the maximum buyback under (a) a board resolution and (b) a special resolution, and test the SEBI debt-equity condition on both bases.

💡 Hint: Decide first which reserves qualify as free reserves. Then test leverage after deducting the buyback outlay from the equity base.

Q2. Journal entries and CRR [L1]

A company buys back 50 lakh equity shares of ₹10 each at ₹640 per share through a tender offer. It uses ₹180 crore of securities premium, and the balance comes from retained earnings. Transaction costs are ₹2.4 crore. Pass all journal entries, including the CRR transfer, and state the Ind AS 32 basis.

Q3. Sec 70 eligibility [L2]

A company delayed repayment of a term-loan instalment by 45 days in August 2022 and cured the default in October

  1. In November 2024 it repaid all outstanding term loans. It now (December 2025) proposes a buyback. Assess

eligibility under Sec 70 and any other conditions you would check.

Q4. Route selection after the 2025 phase-out [L2]

A promoter-held (62%) listed mid-cap wants to return ₹800 crore and expects its promoters not to participate. Explain the route now available, the effect on promoter shareholding and minimum public shareholding, the 15% reservation, and the capital-raising restrictions that will apply afterwards.

Q5. Planning conflict [L3]

The board of a listed company approves a tender-offer buyback in May 2025 that is completed in August 2025. In December 2025 the CFO proposes a QIP to fund an acquisition. Draft a note on the legal constraints, the alternative funding options, and the timing you would recommend.
