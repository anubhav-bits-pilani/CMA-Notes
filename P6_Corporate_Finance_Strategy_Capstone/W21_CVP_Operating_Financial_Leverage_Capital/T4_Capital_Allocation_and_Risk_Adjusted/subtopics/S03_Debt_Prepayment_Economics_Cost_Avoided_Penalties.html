Subtopic note

Debt Prepayment Economics: Cost Avoided, Penalties and Covenants

Scope: Debt prepayment economics: return = after-tax cost of debt avoided, plus prepayment penalties and covenant relief

Why it matters

After the 2015–2020 stress cycle, Indian corporates (steel, cement, infrastructure, auto ancillaries) used much of their free cash flow to prepay term loans and redeem NCDs early. Treasury and FP&A teams are routinely asked whether surplus cash should repay a 9.5% term loan, fund capex, or go back to shareholders. Answering this well means getting four things right: the after-tax return on prepayment, the prepayment charges and the accounting write-off of unamortised fees, the liquidity you give up, and the non-cash benefits of a better rating and more covenant headroom. Lenders' sanction letters, NCD information memoranda and the Ind AS 109 EIR schedule are the working documents.

Core notes

The return on prepayment

Repaying ₹100 of debt removes ₹100 × Kd of interest each year. Because interest is tax-deductible (Sec 36(1)(iii) for capital borrowed for business), the cash saved is after tax:

\[ \text{Return on prepayment} = K_d \times (1 - t) \]

With Kd = 9.4% and t = 25.168%, the return is 7.03%. This is close to a risk-free return to the company, because the saving is contractual. It is not risk-free to shareholders in a broader sense: prepayment uses up liquidity that might be needed in a downturn.

Prepayment charges and their effect on the effective return

Corporate term loans and NCDs usually carry a prepayment premium, often 1–2% of the amount prepaid, waived on reset or call dates. An NCD may have a call option at par or at a premium on specified dates. The effective return on prepaying with a penalty, over the remaining life of \(n\) years, is the IRR of:

  • outflow at t = 0: principal + penalty × (1 − t), provided the penalty is deductible as a revenue expense (check facts; it is generally allowed as a cost of borrowing), and
  • inflows each year: interest saved × (1 − t), plus the principal that would otherwise have been repaid on schedule.

A quick approximation:

\[ \text{Effective return} \approx K_d(1-t) - \frac{\text{Penalty} \times (1-t)}{\text{Average remaining life (years)}} \]

Illustration. Prepay ₹200 crore of a 9.4% loan with 3 years of bullet maturity remaining. The penalty is 1.5%, or ₹3 crore, and t = 25.168%. The pre-penalty return is 7.03%. The penalty cost after tax is ₹2.25 crore, which spread over 3 years is about 0.37% per year. The effective return is roughly 6.66%. Compare this with the risk-adjusted return available on the alternative use of the cash (c05).

RBI rules on prepayment charges

RBI has long barred foreclosure charges on floating-rate term loans to individuals for non-business purposes. The RBI (Pre-payment Charges on Loans) Directions, 2025 extend this, for loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026, to floating-rate loans to individuals and micro and small enterprises for business purposes (subject to conditions for some lender categories). Large corporate loans and fixed-rate loans are still governed by contract. Verify the current text before advising a client, because the scope varies by lender type and loan size.

Accounting: Ind AS 109 on prepayment

  • Borrowings are carried at amortised cost using the effective interest rate (EIR), with upfront fees deducted from the initial carrying amount (Ind AS 109.5.1.1, B5.4).
  • On full prepayment, the liability is derecognised. The difference between the carrying amount (which still has unamortised fees netted off) and the consideration paid (principal + penalty) goes to P&L as finance cost (Ind AS 109.3.3.3).
  • On partial prepayment, the carrying amount is re-estimated using revised cash flows discounted at the original EIR (catch-up adjustment, Ind AS 109.B5.4.6).
  • A prepayment option embedded in a host debt instrument is closely related, and is not separated, if the exercise price is approximately equal to amortised cost at each exercise date (Ind AS 109.B4.3.5(e)).

Covenant relief and rating benefits

The strategic payoff of prepayment is often bigger than the interest saving:

  • Covenant headroom. Lower net debt improves Net Debt/EBITDA and DSCR. That reduces the risk of breach-driven penal charges, cash sweeps or acceleration (see t5 s06).
  • Rating upgrade. Moving from A to A+ or AA− lowers the spread on all remaining and future borrowing, by perhaps 30–75 bps in the Indian corporate bond market depending on tenor and cycle. That benefit can be valued as the PV of the spread saving on the remaining debt.
  • Release of security. Repaying the secured lenders frees charged assets and removes negative covenants on dividends, capex and further borrowing, restoring strategic flexibility.
  • Lower Ke. Lower D/E reduces relevered beta (c02), although the WACC effect is partly offset by the lost tax shield (t5).

Costs and risks of prepayment

  • Liquidity given up. Re-borrowing in a downturn may be impossible or expensive. Keep a minimum cash buffer (for example, 2–3 months of fixed costs plus the next 12 months of debt service) and undrawn committed lines.
  • Lost tax shield. The tax shield on the debt repaid is lost. This is already reflected in the after-tax Kd.
  • Opportunity cost. If a project earns a risk-adjusted 14% and WACC is 11.5%, prepaying at 7% after tax destroys value relative to the project, unless the leverage constraint binds.
  • Reinvestment risk for fixed-rate debt raised in a low-rate period. Prepaying a 7.2% NCD issued in 2021 to hold cash yielding 7.5% pre-tax makes no economic sense.

Decision framework

Step Question
1 What is Kd(1 − t) after penalties and fee write-offs?
2 What is the risk-adjusted return on the best alternative use of the cash (c05, c06)?
3 Is there a binding constraint (covenant, rating trigger, refinancing wall) that makes deleveraging worth more than its interest saving?
4 Does the remaining liquidity, cash plus undrawn lines, cover the stress-case requirement?
5 Is prepayment cheaper than refinancing at a lower rate instead?

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Floating-rate loans in a falling-rate cycle. After repo cuts, MCLR- or EBLR-linked loans reprice downward anyway, which reduces the benefit of prepaying.
  • Foreign currency ECBs. The true cost includes the hedging cost or the expected depreciation of INR. Unhedged ECB prepayment also crystallises exchange differences.
  • Prepaying low-cost subsidised loans (state soft loans, PLI-linked concessional funding) rarely makes sense.
  • Sec 94B disallowance. Where interest to non-resident AEs is partly disallowed above 30% of EBITDA, the true after-tax cost is higher than Kd(1 − t), which strengthens the case for prepaying that debt first (t5 s07).
  • Loss-making companies with unabsorbed losses get no current tax shield, so the effective return is the pre-tax Kd until the losses are used up.

Key takeaways

  • The base return on prepayment is Kd × (1 − t), a near-certain return.
  • Penalties and unamortised upfront fees reduce the effective return and create a one-off Ind AS 109 loss.
  • RBI's 2025 Directions remove prepayment charges for specified individual and MSE floating-rate loans. Corporate loans remain contractual.
  • The covenant, rating and security-release benefits can exceed the interest saving and should be valued explicitly.
  • Prepay only when the after-tax cost of debt exceeds the risk-adjusted return on alternatives, or when a leverage constraint binds, and never below the minimum liquidity buffer.

Practice Questions

Q1. Effective return with a penalty [L1]

A company can prepay ₹150 crore of a term loan carrying 9.8% interest, with 4 years to bullet maturity. The prepayment charge is 2% of the amount prepaid and is tax-deductible. The tax rate is 25.168%. Compute the after-tax return on prepayment before the penalty and the approximate effective return after the penalty.

Q2. Ind AS 109 entries on full prepayment [L2]

A ₹300 crore term loan was drawn on 1 April 2023 with an upfront processing fee of ₹4.5 crore. The EIR amortisation schedule shows unamortised fees of ₹2.1 crore on 30 September 2025, when the loan is fully prepaid with a 1% prepayment charge. Pass the journal entries and show how the transaction appears in the statement of profit and loss and in the cash flow statement for FY26.

Q3. Prepay vs capex vs hold [L3]

A mid-cap auto-ancillary has surplus cash of ₹250 crore. The alternatives are: (a) prepaying a 9.6% loan (penalty 1%, 3 years remaining); (b) an expansion with an expected IRR of 15% and a project-specific WACC of 13.5%; (c) holding the cash in liquid funds at 7% pre-tax. Its Net Debt/EBITDA covenant is 3.0x. Current net debt is ₹900 crore and LTM EBITDA is ₹320 crore, with EBITDA expected to fall 15% next year. Tax is 25.168%. Recommend an allocation, quantifying covenant headroom under each option.

💡 Hint: Compute headroom after the EBITDA fall before comparing returns. A binding covenant can override a simple rate comparison.

Q4. Valuing a rating upgrade [L2]

Management expects that prepaying ₹500 crore would move the rating from A to AA−, cutting the spread by 50 bps on the remaining ₹2,000 crore of debt, which has an average remaining life of 5 years. Tax is 25.168% and the discount rate is 8.5%. Estimate the PV of this spread benefit and add it to the direct prepayment saving.

Q5. Interview: should a cash-rich company ever prepay cheap debt? [L1]

Explain, with reasons, when a company with ₹3,000 crore of cash and 7.2% fixed-rate NCDs maturing in 2029 should and should not redeem them early.
