Roadmap topic: Capital Allocation & Hurdle Rates: Evaluating expansion Capex vs debt prepayment vs share buybacks/dividends against risk-adjusted WACC

Overview

Capital allocation is the decision a CFO and board make every year about where free cash flow goes. The options are sustaining the existing business, growing it organically or through M&A, repaying lenders, or returning cash to shareholders through dividends or buybacks. Topic t3 gave you the tools to evaluate a single project (NPV, IRR, EAA). This topic is about choosing between fundamentally different uses of the same rupee. Each use must be judged against the right hurdle: the project-specific, risk-adjusted cost of capital for capex, the after-tax cost of debt for prepayment, and intrinsic value per share for a buyback.

For FP&A and corporate finance roles in India, the topic has a strong regulatory layer that textbooks often get wrong or leave out of date. Companies Act Sec 68–70 limits buybacks. SEBI's 2018 Buy-back Regulations eliminated the stock-exchange open-market route from 1 April 2025. Shareholder taxation has changed three times in six years: DDT abolished in FY21, Sec 115QA from 2019 to September 2024, and deemed-dividend treatment from 1 October 2024, with the Income-tax Act 2025 and Finance Act 2026 to be verified. Dividends are governed by Sec 123 distributable profit rules and the LODR Reg 43A policy. A board note that recommends "buyback over dividend" without the post-2024 tax analysis, or a capex approval at the group WACC for a new-segment project, is a credibility problem.

The learning sequence is as follows. Start with the allocation hierarchy (s01) to see the full menu and its logic. Then learn the value-creation test for capex, the ROIC–WACC spread and EVA (s02, c01), and the hurdle-rate construction that makes that test risk-appropriate: pure-play betas, unlevering and relevering (s07, c02), and when explicit premia are justified (c06). Next, study the alternatives to capex: debt prepayment (s03, c05), buybacks (s04, c03, c04) and dividends (s06), with the tax overlay that now drives the dividend vs buyback choice (s05). Topic t5 then supplies the leverage and rating constraints that decide how much of any of this can be funded with debt.

Related concepts to study

Week 16: CAPM, ERP and WACC for Indian companies

→ Week 16: CAPM, ERP and WACC construction for Indian companies

Every hurdle rate in this topic is built from the Week 16 toolkit:

  • the risk-free rate from the 10-year G-sec;
  • an Indian ERP, from historical or implied estimates, with its sources and range;
  • beta estimation with =SLOPE and the Blume adjustment;
  • the cost of debt from rating-implied spreads;
  • market-value weights.

The difference here is granularity. Week 16 builds one WACC for a company. This topic builds one per division or project, by unlevering comparables and relevering at project gearing. Revisit the Week 16 debates on whether to add a country risk premium to an Indian ERP, how to treat lease liabilities in weights, and the use of 25.168% versus the old-regime marginal rate. All of them reappear when you construct divisional rates.

Topic t5: capital structure targets constrain debt-funded capex

→ Week 21 t5: capital structure targets constrain how much capex can be debt-funded

A positive-NPV project can still be infeasible if funding it with debt pushes Net Debt/EBITDA above the covenant or rating threshold. Topic t5 computes debt capacity as target multiple × EBITDA − existing net debt. That figure sets the financing envelope within which this topic's allocation decisions are made. It also explains the overrides to the simple prepayment test. When headroom is thin, deleveraging is worth more than its after-tax interest saving, because it avoids covenant breach, penal charges and a rating downgrade that would raise Kd on all borrowings. Build the two topics together: the allocation waterfall must reconcile with the debt schedule.

Week 8: peer benchmarking of payouts, ROCE and reinvestment

→ Week 8: peer benchmarking of payout ratios, ROCE and reinvestment rates

The capital allocation record is best judged against peers. Compute, over five to ten years:

  • reinvestment rate = (capex − depreciation + ΔNWC) ÷ NOPAT;
  • incremental ROCE = ΔEBIT ÷ Δcapital employed;
  • payout ratio, including buybacks;
  • cash conversion.

A cement company that reinvests heavily at an incremental ROCE below its WACC should have returned more cash. An IT services company with a 90% payout and a stable ROCE above 30% is consistent with low reinvestment needs. Use annual-report cash flow statements and the dividend distribution policies. Remember to gross up pre-FY21 dividends for DDT so that payouts are comparable across tax regimes.

FCFE as the capacity for dividends and buybacks

Free cash flow to equity (FCFE) as the capacity for dividends/buybacks

FCFE = net income + D&A − capex − ΔNWC + net borrowing. Equivalently, FCFF − interest × (1 − t) + net borrowing. It is the cash available to equity holders after all reinvestment and debt flows. A company whose dividends plus buybacks persistently exceed FCFE is funding distributions with debt or by running down cash. That is acceptable only if leverage is below target. Comparing cumulative distributions with cumulative FCFE over 5 years is a quick test of sustainability. In the plan, cap the distribution line at FCFE, adjusted for the target change in leverage. Use FCFE discounted at Ke for equity valuation, consistently with the cross-cutting principle of matching flows and rates.

Agency costs of free cash flow and promoter-driven allocation

Agency costs of free cash flow (Jensen) and promoter-driven allocation in Indian groups

Jensen (1986) argued that managers with free cash flow beyond positive-NPV needs tend to waste it on empire building and low-return acquisitions. Debt and dividends discipline them by committing that cash away. In India the agency problem is often between promoters and minority shareholders rather than managers and owners. Examples include:

  • cash parked as inter-corporate deposits to group entities;
  • related-party purchases at non-arm's-length prices;
  • diversification into the promoter's pet sectors;
  • reluctance to pay dividends when promoters prefer to hold cash in the company.

Safeguards are SEBI LODR related-party approval (Reg 23), Sec 185/186, independent directors and audit committees, and investor pressure. The DIPAM CPSE payout guidelines are a government-shareholder version of the same pressure.

Common pitfalls

Corporate WACC used for a riskier (or safer) project

Using the corporate WACC for a riskier new-segment project (or a safer one), misallocating capital.

The mistake. Applying the group's single WACC to every project.

Why people make it. It is simple, it is already approved by the board, and it seems "fair" to all divisions.

Correct treatment. Build a project-specific WACC from pure-play βu relevered at the project's target gearing (s07, c02, c06). A renewable project in a cement group needs a lower rate. A new consumer-tech venture in a utility group needs a much higher one.

How to detect it in review. The same discount rate appears across project files with very different businesses. Divisions with high systematic risk win a disproportionate share of capex. Realised ROIC in the risky divisions falls below their true cost of capital.

EPS accretion mistaken for value creation

Calling a buyback "value-creating" because it is EPS-accretive, ignoring the price paid vs intrinsic value.

The mistake. Presenting EPS accretion as proof that a buyback creates value.

Why people make it. Accretion is easy to compute and is how sell-side and management communicate. Low-yield cash makes almost any buyback at a moderate P/E look accretive.

Correct treatment. Accretion only tells you that the earnings yield exceeds the after-tax cost of funds (c04). Value is created for continuing shareholders only if the buyback price is below intrinsic value per share. A debt-funded buyback also raises financial risk and Ke.

How to detect it in review. The board note contains no DCF or intrinsic-value range. The buyback premium over market price is not justified. EPS rises while the P/E falls after the buyback.

Double-counting risk: premium plus haircut

Adding a subjective risk premium to the discount rate and also haircutting cash flows (double-counting risk).

The mistake. Haircutting cash flows for execution risk and also adding a premium to the discount rate for the same risk.

Why people make it. Different teams adjust different things: engineering haircuts the volumes, while treasury adds a premium. Conservatism also feels prudent.

Correct treatment. Count each risk once (c06). Systematic risk goes in beta. Diversifiable, project-specific risk goes in probability-weighted cash flows. Use an explicit premium only as a disclosed shortcut when the cash flows are an unadjusted base case.

How to detect it in review. The model has both a "risk haircut" row and a "project risk premium" input. Accepted projects consistently beat their plans. Positive-NPV projects are routinely rejected.

Ignoring the shift of buyback taxation to shareholders

Ignoring the shift of buyback taxation to shareholders when comparing buyback vs dividend.

The mistake. Comparing a buyback and a dividend using pre-October 2024 rules, for example treating the buyback as tax-free for shareholders, or deducting Sec 115QA tax at company level.

Why people make it. Older templates, textbooks and precedent board notes still reflect Sec 115QA.

Correct treatment. From 1 October 2024 the buyback consideration is a deemed dividend taxed in shareholders' hands, and the cost becomes a capital loss (s05). Model the after-tax receipt by shareholder class and the expected participation. Check the Income-tax Act 2025 numbering and any Finance Act 2026 amendments.

How to detect it in review. A 23.296% company-level tax line appears in a post-2024 buyback model. The analysis has no shareholder-class breakdown. The board note cites Sec 10(34A) exemption for current buybacks.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated allocation decision for a cement group [L3]

An NSE-listed cement group has the following FY26 position (₹ crore):

  • LTM EBITDA 4,000; net debt 7,600 (including leases 400); covenant Net Debt/EBITDA ≤ 2.5x;
  • surplus cash available after maintenance capex and the policy dividend: 2,500;
  • tax rate 25.168%.

It has four options:

  • (a) a 6 MTPA greenfield cement expansion costing ₹4,500 crore over 2 years, expected IRR 14.5%;
  • (b) a 300 MW captive solar project costing ₹1,500 crore, with stable cost-saving cash flows and expected IRR 11.5%;
  • (c) prepaying ₹2,500 crore of 8.9% term loans, with a penalty of 0.5%;
  • (d) a tender-offer buyback at a price 15% above the current market price of ₹9,000 per share, against a DCF value of ₹9,800 per share.

Market data: Rf 6.8%, ERP 7.0%, cement pure-play βu 0.85, renewable pure-play βu 0.45. The target D/E is 0.4 for cement and 2.0 for the solar project, with Kd of 8.9% and 9.2% respectively.

Required:

  1. Compute the hurdle rate for (a) and (b).
  2. Compute the after-tax return on (c).
  3. Assess value creation and the EPS effect of (d), given PAT of ₹2,200 crore, 25 crore shares, and cash earning 7% pre-tax.
  4. Test the covenant under each option, assuming an EBITDA downside of −15%.
  5. Recommend an allocation.

Q2. Buyback vs dividend for a promoter-held IT company [L3]

A listed IT services company with no debt plans to return ₹6,000 crore in FY26. The data are:

  • 400 crore shares, market price ₹1,500, DCF value ₹1,650;
  • paid-up equity ₹400 crore (face value ₹1), securities premium ₹1,000 crore, free reserves ₹38,000 crore;
  • shareholding: promoters 60% (a corporate promoter on 25.168%, with negligible cost base); FPIs 15% (treaty dividend rate 15%); mutual funds 10% (exempt); resident individuals 15% (assume all in the 30% slab, effective 35.88%).

Required:

  1. Confirm the Sec 68 capacity and the approval route.
  2. Describe the SEBI route available.
  3. Compute the after-tax receipt by shareholder class under (i) a dividend and (ii) a buyback at ₹1,650 in which only promoters, mutual funds and FPIs tender, in proportion to their holdings.
  4. Compute the change in promoter holding.
  5. State which method you would recommend and why.

💡 Hint: Under post-October 2024 rules, treat the buyback proceeds as deemed dividend and value the capital loss only if usable.

Q3. Dividend capacity and policy design [L2]

A listed chemicals company reports FY25 standalone PAT of ₹520 crore. This includes ₹60 crore of unrealised FVTPL gains. Consolidated FCFE over the past five years averaged ₹300 crore. The current Reg 43A policy states a "25–40% payout of consolidated PAT". Lender covenants restrict dividends to 50% of standalone PAT if DSCR falls below 1.5x, and forecast DSCR is 1.45x. Last year's DPS was ₹9 on 20 crore shares, and the Lintner speed of adjustment is 0.35.

Required:

  1. Compute distributable profit under Sec 123.
  2. Forecast DPS with the Lintner model at a 35% target payout of consolidated PAT of ₹600 crore.
  3. Test the forecast against the covenant and against FCFE.
  4. Propose amendments to the policy.

Q4. Divisional hurdle-rate audit [L3]

You are reviewing a conglomerate's capex approvals for the past three years. All projects were approved at a group WACC of 11.8%. The approvals were:

  • ₹2,000 crore in a hospitality division (pure-play βu 1.0, target D/E 0.7);
  • ₹1,500 crore in a regulated transmission business (βu 0.40, D/E 2.5);
  • ₹1,000 crore in an FMCG division (βu 0.70, D/E 0.1).

Market data: Rf 7.0%, ERP 7.0%, Kd 9.5%, 8.6% and 9.0% respectively, and t = 25.168%. The approved base-case IRRs were 13%, 10.8% and 12.5%, and the hospitality projects also carried a 2% "execution premium" while using haircut cash flows.

Required:

  1. Recompute each divisional hurdle.
  2. Identify the misallocated projects.
  3. Flag the double-counting.
  4. Write a short memo setting out the revised hurdle-rate policy.
