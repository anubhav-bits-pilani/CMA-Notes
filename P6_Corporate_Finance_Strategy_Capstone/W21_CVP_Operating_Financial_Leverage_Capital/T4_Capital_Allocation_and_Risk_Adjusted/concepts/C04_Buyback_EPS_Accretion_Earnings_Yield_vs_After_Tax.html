Key concept

Buyback EPS Accretion: Earnings Yield vs After-Tax Cost of Funds

Concept: Buyback EPS effect: accretive if earnings yield (1 ÷ P/E) < after-tax yield on cash used (or after-tax cost of debt raised)

Statement

A buyback of shares at price \(P\), funded with cash that earned (or debt that costs) a pre-tax rate \(r\), changes EPS as follows:

\[ \text{EPS}_{\text{new}} = \frac{\text{PAT} - C \times r \times (1 - t)}{N - C/P} \]

where \(C\) is the cash spent, \(N\) the shares outstanding before the buyback, and \(t\) the tax rate. Setting \(\text{EPS}_{\text{new}} > \text{EPS}_{\text{old}} = \text{PAT}/N\) and simplifying gives the correct condition:

\[ \text{Buyback is EPS-accretive} \iff \frac{\text{EPS}}{P} = \frac{1}{\text{P/E}} > r \times (1 - t) \]

Note on direction. The concept text above, quoted verbatim from the brief, states the inequality the other way round. The algebra shows that a buyback is accretive when the earnings yield exceeds the after-tax yield forgone on cash (or the after-tax cost of new debt). It is dilutive when the earnings yield is lower. Equivalently, it is accretive when \(\text{P/E} < 1/[r(1-t)]\). Learn the corrected form.

Explanation

When you buy back shares you "buy" the earnings attached to them, and the earnings yield is the return on that purchase. You pay with the after-tax income the cash would have earned, or with the after-tax interest on debt raised. If the yield bought is higher than the yield given up, EPS rises.

  • Cash-funded. With treasury yields of about 7% pre-tax and t = 25.168%, the after-tax yield is about 5.24%. Break-even P/E is 1 ÷ 0.0524, or about 19.1×. A company trading below about 19× P/E increases EPS by spending idle cash on a buyback. One trading at 30× dilutes EPS.
  • Debt-funded. At Kd = 9% the after-tax cost is 6.73%, and the break-even P/E is about 14.9×.
  • Tax considerations. Before 1 October 2024, Sec 115QA tax of 23.296% on distributed income was an additional cost of a buyback, so the effective price per share was higher. After that date the tax falls on shareholders (s05) and does not enter company-level EPS maths.

The key limitation is that EPS accretion is not value creation. Value is created for continuing shareholders only if the price paid is below intrinsic value per share. A buyback at a P/E of 12× is accretive, but if intrinsic value implies 9×, the company has overpaid and transferred value to the selling shareholders. Accretion also ignores the higher financial risk after a debt-funded buyback, which raises Ke (c02) and should lower the P/E.

Illustration

PAT is ₹1,200 crore, there are 100 crore shares, EPS is ₹12, and the price is ₹180 (P/E 15×, earnings yield 6.67%). The company spends ₹1,800 crore of cash earning 7.2% pre-tax, with t = 25.168%.

Item Value
Shares bought 1,800 ÷ 180 = 10 crore
Income lost after tax 1,800 × 7.2% × 0.74832 = ₹97.0 crore
New PAT ₹1,103.0 crore
New shares 90 crore
New EPS ₹12.26, accretive by 2.1%

The after-tax yield on cash is 5.39%, which is below the 6.67% earnings yield, so the buyback is accretive. The same buyback at ₹300 per share (P/E 25×, earnings yield 4%) buys only 6 crore shares and gives a new EPS of ₹11.73 (1,103.0 ÷ 94), which is dilutive.

Practice Questions

Q1. Break-even P/E [L1]

A company's cash earns 7.5% pre-tax and the tax rate is 25.168%. Compute the P/E above which a cash-funded buyback dilutes EPS. Repeat the calculation for a debt-funded buyback at 8.8% pre-tax.

Q2. Accretion and value [L2]

PAT is ₹450 crore, there are 30 crore shares, and the market price is ₹210. A DCF gives an intrinsic value of ₹175 per share. The company borrows ₹630 crore at 9.2% to buy back shares at ₹210, and the tax rate is 25.168%. Compute the EPS effect and the value transferred between selling and continuing shareholders. Comment on whether the buyback creates value.

💡 Hint: Value transfer per share bought equals the price paid minus intrinsic value, multiplied by the shares bought.

Q3. Interview: the accretion pitch [L1]

An investment banker says that a buyback is "obviously good" because it is 4% EPS-accretive. Give three counter-arguments a CFO should consider.
