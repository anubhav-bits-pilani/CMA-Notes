Key concept

Prepayment Test: After-Tax Kd vs Risk-Adjusted Alternative Return

Concept: Prepayment test: prepay if after-tax cost of debt > risk-adjusted return on the best alternative use of cash

Statement

\[ \text{Prepay if } K_d^{\text{eff}} \times (1 - t) > r^{*}_{\text{alt}} \]

where:

  • \(K_d^{\text{eff}}\) = the effective pre-tax cost of the debt to be repaid, adjusted for prepayment penalties and any lost subsidies, so that it reflects the true rate saved over the remaining life;
  • \(t\) = the marginal tax rate at which interest is actually deducted. Use zero if the company is in a tax-loss position and the shield is not being used;
  • \(r^{*}_{\text{alt}}\) = the risk-adjusted return on the best alternative use of the same cash. That is either the IRR of a project, for comparison with its own hurdle, or the after-tax yield on treasury investments. On a like-for-like risk basis this means comparing NPVs, not raw IRRs.

A cleaner equivalent: prepayment is a riskless investment yielding \(K_d(1-t)\), so compare NPV per rupee of each use, with each discounted at the rate appropriate to its own risk.

Explanation

Repaying debt "earns" the interest you no longer pay, net of the tax shield you lose. Because the saving is contractual, its risk is roughly that of the debt itself. That return must be compared with alternatives of similar risk. Comparing a 7% riskless saving with an 18% IRR on a risky project is misleading unless the project IRR is judged against its own hurdle. The correct statement is: invest in the project if its NPV at the project-specific WACC is positive, and the company can fund it without breaching its leverage targets. Otherwise, prepay.

Overrides to the simple test:

  • Binding constraints. If covenant headroom or the rating is at risk (t5), deleveraging has extra value, the avoided cost of breach. That can justify prepaying even when \(K_d(1-t)\) is below the alternative's return.
  • Liquidity. Never prepay below the minimum cash buffer plus committed undrawn lines needed for the stress case.
  • Refinancing alternative. If cheaper debt is available, refinancing may dominate prepayment.
  • Holding cash yields \(r_{\text{treasury}}(1-t)\), which is usually below \(K_d(1-t)\). Gross debt carried alongside idle cash ("negative carry") is a signal to prepay, unless the cash is earmarked.

Illustration

A company has ₹300 crore of surplus cash and t = 25.168%. Its options are:

Option Pre-tax rate After-tax return Risk Comment
A. Prepay term loan, 9.6%, penalty 1%, 3 years remaining 9.6% ≈ 7.18% − 0.25% ≈ 6.9% Near riskless Penalty spread over 3 years
B. Liquid funds 7.0% 5.24% Very low Negative carry vs A
C. Capacity project IRR 14% — Project WACC 13.5% Small positive NPV, sensitive to assumptions

Option A dominates B. Between A and C, the right comparison is C's NPV at 13.5% (positive but thin) against A's certain saving. If C's downside scenario has a negative NPV and net debt/EBITDA is close to the covenant, prepaying (A) is preferred. If C is robustly positive and leverage headroom is ample, fund C.

Practice Questions

Q1. Simple test [L1]

A company has a 10.2% term loan, a tax rate of 25.168%, and surplus cash earning 7.3% pre-tax in liquid funds. There is no prepayment penalty. Should it prepay? Quantify the annual after-tax benefit on ₹100 crore.

Q2. Tax-loss company [L2]

A company has ₹600 crore of brought-forward business losses that are expected to be absorbed over the next 3 years. It can prepay a 9% loan. Explain how the effective after-tax cost of the debt changes over the next 3 years and afterwards, and how that affects the prepayment decision against a project with an after-tax IRR of 10%.

💡 Hint: While losses are being absorbed, the deduction defers tax rather than saving it immediately. Think about timing.

Q3. Risk-mismatched comparison [L2]

A director argues: "Prepaying our 9% loan earns only 6.7% after tax, while our new retail venture's IRR is 16%, so we should obviously invest." The venture's project-specific WACC is 17%. Evaluate the argument.
