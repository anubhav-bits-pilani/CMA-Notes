Capital Structure Theories: MM, Trade-Off, Pecking Order, Timing

Scope: Theories: Modigliani–Miller with taxes, trade-off theory, pecking order, market timing

Why it matters

Capital structure theory explains why companies borrow as much (or as little) as they do. It also gives you the vocabulary to defend a leverage recommendation to a board. In interviews for corporate finance, FP&A and valuation roles you will be asked to:

  • derive the MM propositions;
  • explain why a company does not borrow 100% if debt is tax-advantaged;
  • explain why cash-rich IT companies carry no debt while utilities carry a lot.

In practice, the trade-off framework underlies the optimal D/E search (s08). Pecking-order and market-timing behaviour explain Indian patterns: heavy reliance on internal accruals, QIP waves in bull markets, and the deleveraging after 2016. The Indian evidence is consistent with these theories, but no single theory explains all of it.

Core notes

MM without taxes (1958): the benchmark

In perfect markets (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no information asymmetry, investors able to borrow at the same rate as firms):

  • Proposition I. \(V_L = V_U\). Capital structure is irrelevant to firm value, because investors can replicate or undo corporate leverage through "homemade leverage".
  • Proposition II.

\[ K_e = K_u + (K_u - K_d)\frac{D}{E} \]

Ke rises linearly with leverage, exactly offsetting the cheaper debt, so WACC stays at Ku.

The value of the benchmark is that it tells you where to look for value effects of capital structure: in the imperfections, namely taxes, distress costs, agency costs and information.

MM with corporate taxes (1963)

Interest is tax-deductible, so debt creates a tax shield. With permanent debt \(D\) and tax rate \(t\), the PV of the shield discounted at Kd is \(tD\):

\[ V_L = V_U + tD \]

\[ K_e = K_u + (K_u - K_d)(1 - t)\frac{D}{E}, \qquad \text{WACC} = K_u\left(1 - t\frac{D}{V}\right) \]

WACC falls continuously with leverage. Taken literally, the result implies 100% debt, which nobody observes. The reason is that the model omits distress costs and personal taxes. At India's 25.168% rate (Sec 115BAA), ₹1,000 crore of permanent debt adds ₹252 crore of value in this model, versus ₹349 crore under the old 34.944% regime. The 2019 rate cut reduced the tax advantage of debt for Indian companies.

Miller (1977) with personal taxes. If interest income is taxed at the personal rate \(t_d\) and equity income at \(t_e\), the gain from leverage is:

\[ G = \left[1 - \frac{(1 - t_c)(1 - t_e)}{1 - t_d}\right] D \]

In India, interest is taxed at slab rates of up to about 39% for top-bracket individuals. Equity income is taxed as dividends at slab rates, or as capital gains at 12.5% for LTCG. The net tax advantage of corporate debt is therefore smaller than \(t_c\) for retail-held debt, and roughly \(t_c\) for tax-exempt holders such as provident funds and mutual funds.

Static trade-off theory

The firm balances the PV of tax shields against the PV of expected financial distress costs:

\[ V_L = V_U + \text{PV(tax shields)} - \text{PV(expected distress costs)} \]

The optimum is reached where the marginal tax benefit equals the marginal expected distress cost. The predictions are:

  • Firms with tangible assets and stable cash flows (utilities, toll roads, cement, real estate leasing) should carry more debt. Their distress costs are lower because assets keep their value, and the tax shields are more certain.
  • Firms with intangible assets and volatile cash flows (IT services, pharma R&D, consumer brands, start-ups) should carry little debt.
  • Profitable firms should borrow more, since they have more taxable income to shield. Empirically, though, more profitable firms borrow less. That is the main puzzle the theory cannot explain, and pecking order can.

The dynamic trade-off adds adjustment costs. Firms let leverage drift within a band and rebalance only when it moves far from target.

Pecking order theory (Myers and Majluf, 1984)

Managers know more than outside investors. Issuing equity signals that management thinks the shares are overvalued, so the price falls on announcement. To avoid this adverse-selection cost, firms prefer:

  1. internal funds (retained earnings, cash);
  2. debt, safest first: bank loans, then bonds, then convertibles;
  3. equity, as a last resort.

Under this theory there is no target leverage ratio. Leverage reflects the cumulative financing deficit. Profitable firms have low leverage because they need no external funding. The theory fits Indian promoter-controlled companies well. Promoters avoid equity issues that dilute control, and internal accruals fund most capex, as the RBI's studies of corporate finances show.

Market timing theory (Baker and Wurgler, 2002)

Firms issue equity when their valuations are high, measured by market-to-book or past returns, and repurchase or borrow when they are low. Current capital structure is the cumulative result of past timing attempts. Indian evidence:

  • record QIP and IPO volumes in the bull markets of FY21–FY25;
  • buybacks when managements believed shares were cheap;
  • companies refinancing with bonds when corporate bond spreads were tight.

Comparing the theories

Theory Target leverage? Key driver Predicts high leverage for
MM (no tax) Irrelevant — —
MM (tax) 100% debt Tax shield Everyone
Static trade-off Yes, interior optimum Tax shield vs distress Tangible, stable, taxable firms
Pecking order No Information asymmetry Firms with large financing deficits
Market timing No Mispricing windows Firms whose shares were cheap when they needed funds
Agency (Jensen) Yes, moderate Disciplining free cash flow Mature cash-generative firms

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Tax-loss companies get no current shield, so their effective \(t\) is near zero until losses are absorbed. The trade-off optimum falls sharply.
  • Sec 94B interest limits (s07) cap the shield for interest paid to foreign AEs, which flattens the MM tax benefit above 30% of EBITDA.
  • Debt overhang. A heavily levered firm may forgo positive-NPV projects because the gains go mainly to creditors (Myers, 1977). This is an indirect distress cost (s05).
  • Interest rates and tax shields. MM assumes the tax shield's risk equals the debt's. If debt is rebalanced to a constant D/V, the shields should be discounted at Ku. This is the Harris–Pringle and Miles–Ezzell refinement used in APV.
  • Theory vs practice. CFO surveys (Graham and Harvey) find that companies care most about financial flexibility and the credit rating, which is the trade-off theory implemented through rating targets.

Key takeaways

  • MM (1958): capital structure is irrelevant in perfect markets, and Ke rises linearly with D/E.
  • MM with taxes: \(V_L = V_U + tD\). Value rises with debt, but at India's 25.168% rate the advantage is smaller than under the old regime.
  • Trade-off: the optimum balances the tax shield against expected distress costs. Tangible, stable firms should borrow more.
  • Pecking order: internal funds first, then debt, then equity. It explains low leverage at profitable, promoter-controlled companies.
  • Market timing: issue equity when it is expensive, buy back when it is cheap. Indian QIP and IPO waves fit the pattern.

Practice Questions

Q1. MM with taxes [L1]

An unlevered company has EBIT of ₹500 crore in perpetuity, a tax rate of 25.168%, and Ku = 13%. It plans to issue ₹1,200 crore of permanent debt at 9% and use the proceeds to buy back shares. Compute VU, VL, the value of equity, Ke and WACC after recapitalisation under MM with taxes. Repeat at a 34.944% tax rate.

Q2. Miller personal tax effect [L2]

Using Miller's formula, compute the gain from ₹1,000 crore of permanent debt when the corporate rate is 25.168%, the personal tax on interest is 35.88%, and the personal tax on equity income is 14.95%. Recompute for debt held by a tax-exempt provident fund. Interpret the difference.

💡 Hint: For the tax-exempt holder, set both personal rates to zero.

Q3. Which theory fits? [L2]

For each Indian company profile, state which theory best explains its capital structure and why:

  • (a) a large IT services company with zero debt and ₹40,000 crore cash;
  • (b) a regulated power transmission company at 70% debt;
  • (c) a mid-cap specialty chemical company that raised a QIP at 60× P/E in 2021 and repaid debt;
  • (d) a promoter-controlled steel company that funds capex with internal accruals and bank debt and has never issued equity since its IPO.

Q4. Interview: why not 100% debt? [L1]

"If interest is tax-deductible, why don't companies finance entirely with debt?" Give a structured answer covering at least four reasons, with Indian examples.
