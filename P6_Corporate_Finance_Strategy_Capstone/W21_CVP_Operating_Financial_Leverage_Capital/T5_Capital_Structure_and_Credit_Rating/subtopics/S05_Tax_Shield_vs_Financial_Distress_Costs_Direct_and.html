Subtopic note

Tax Shield vs Financial Distress Costs, Direct and Indirect

Scope: Tax shield vs distress costs: direct (insolvency/IBC costs) and indirect (lost customers, covenant breaches)

Why it matters

The trade-off theory says the optimal leverage is where the marginal tax shield equals the marginal expected cost of distress. The tax shield is easy to compute. Distress costs are harder to estimate, and in India they are large. IBC resolution has historically taken well over the 330-day outer limit on average, recoveries for financial creditors have averaged around a third of admitted claims, and the indirect damage starts long before any insolvency petition. Customers switch, suppliers demand advances, and key employees leave. FP&A teams modelling an aggressive leverage plan must show the board both sides of the ledger, and lenders' credit committees will ask how the company would fare in a downturn at the proposed leverage.

Core notes

Valuing the tax shield

The annual shield is \(t \times \text{Interest}\). Its PV depends on how debt evolves:

  • Permanent debt (MM): PV = \(tD\), discounting at Kd.
  • Debt on a fixed schedule: \(\text{PV} = \sum_t t \times \text{Interest}_t / (1 + K_d)^t\).
  • Debt rebalanced to a target D/V: discount the shields at Ku, or at Kd for one period and then Ku (Miles–Ezzell). The PV is lower.
  • Expected, not promised, shields: the shield is lost in years when the company has no taxable profit, and when Sec 94B disallows interest (s07). In distress states it is worth less, which itself reduces the benefit of the marginal rupee of debt.

Illustration. ₹2,000 crore of debt is held constant for 10 years and then repaid, at Kd 9% and t 25.168%. The annual shield is 2,000 × 9% × 25.168% = ₹45.3 crore. PV at 9% over 10 years is 45.3 × 6.418 = ₹290.7 crore. If there is a 20% chance each year that the company is loss-making and the shield is deferred indefinitely, the expected PV falls towards ₹230 crore.

Direct distress costs

These are out-of-pocket costs of formal insolvency or restructuring:

  • IBC process costs. Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, these include the interim resolution professional and resolution professional fees, the CoC's legal and advisory fees, valuers (two registered valuers for fair and liquidation value), forensic auditors, and the costs of running the company as a going concern during CIRP. All are paid as insolvency resolution process costs in priority (Sec 53).
  • Timelines. CIRP must be completed within 180 days, extendable by 90 days, with an outer limit of 330 days including litigation (Sec 12). In practice average durations have been much longer, and admission alone can take months. Delay erodes value.
  • Liquidation costs. If no resolution plan is approved, the company is liquidated (Sec 33), with fire-sale values and the Sec 53 waterfall.
  • Out-of-court restructuring. The RBI Prudential Framework (June 2019) provides for an inter-creditor agreement and a resolution plan within 180 days of the review period, with additional provisioning for delays. There are legal, advisory and TEV study costs, and banks' haircuts.

Empirical studies put direct costs at a few per cent of firm value for large firms. That is small relative to indirect costs, but higher for small firms.

Indirect distress costs

These start when distress becomes likely, not when it happens:

  • Lost customers. Buyers of durable goods, capital equipment, or anything needing warranties, spares or long-term service avoid a supplier that might fail. Airlines and real-estate developers are classic Indian examples: bookings and home sales fall precisely when cash is needed.
  • Supplier tightening. Credit terms shrink to advance payment or LC-backed terms, raising working capital just as liquidity is short.
  • Employee attrition, especially of key talent, and management distraction.
  • Underinvestment (debt overhang). Equity holders refuse to fund positive-NPV projects whose benefits go to creditors.
  • Asset substitution (risk shifting). Equity holders favour risky gambles, because they have limited downside.
  • Covenant breaches. Penal charges apply, since RBI's 2023 framework requires penal charges, not penal interest, from 2024. Other consequences are cash sweeps, dividend blocks, step-up coupons, acceleration and cross-default.
  • Classification. SMA-1 and SMA-2 classification (31–60 and 61–90 days overdue), and NPA at 90 days, which restricts further lending and triggers CRILC reporting for large borrowers.
  • Rating downgrade below investment grade. Mandate-constrained investors exit, Kd jumps, and CP market access closes.
  • Fire sales of non-core assets at distressed prices.

Expected distress cost

\[ \text{PV(expected distress costs)} = \text{PD (cumulative, at the given leverage)} \times \text{Cost of distress (as \% of firm value)} \times V_U \]

PD can be proxied from the rating-implied default rates in CRA default studies. Cumulative 5-year default rates rise steeply from AA (low single digits) to BBB and below. Distress cost estimates of 10–25% of firm value, combining direct and indirect costs, are commonly used. They are higher for intangible-heavy, customer-sensitive businesses.

Worked illustration: trade-off at three leverage levels

\(V_U\) = ₹10,000 crore, t = 25.168%, permanent debt, and distress cost = 20% of \(V_U\) if distress occurs.

Debt (₹ cr) Implied rating 5-yr cumulative PD PV tax shield (tD) Expected distress cost Net benefit
2,000 AA 1% 503 20 483
4,000 A− 5% 1,007 100 907
6,000 BB+ 20% 1,510 400 1,110
8,000 B 40% 2,013 800 1,213

At these numbers the net benefit is still rising. The table is deliberately simplified, though. At high leverage the tax shield is not \(tD\) (profits are insufficient), Kd rises, and indirect costs escalate. A rigorous version, the rating-implied WACC curve in s08, typically finds the optimum where the rating stays around A to AA−.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Asset tangibility. Steel and cement plants retain value in resolution (several large IBC steel cases recovered significant value), while IT services or branded-consumer firms lose most of their value.
  • Government or promoter support can reduce expected distress costs (implicit PSU support). Rating agencies notch up accordingly.
  • Personal guarantees by promoters, which are standard in Indian bank lending, shift distress costs to promoters and affect their incentives.
  • Pre-packaged insolvency (PPIRP) for MSMEs, introduced in 2021, reduces direct costs.
  • Cash flow volatility matters more than the level of debt. Two firms at 3x Net debt/EBITDA can have very different PDs.

Key takeaways

  • The tax shield is worth \(tD\) only for permanent, fully usable debt. Losses, Sec 94B and distress reduce its expected value.
  • Direct distress costs include IBC process costs, delay beyond the 330-day outer limit, liquidation discounts and restructuring fees.
  • Indirect costs (lost customers, supplier tightening, debt overhang, covenant penalties, downgrades) start well before default and are usually larger.
  • Expected distress cost = PD × cost of distress. PD rises steeply as the rating falls towards BBB and below.
  • Optimal leverage balances the two. Firms with intangible assets and volatile cash flows should borrow far less.

Practice Questions

Q1. PV of a tax shield on scheduled debt [L1]

A company borrows ₹1,000 crore at 9.5% repayable in five equal annual instalments of principal, with interest on the opening balance. The tax rate is 25.168%. Compute the PV of the tax shields at Kd. Compare the result with \(tD\) and explain the difference.

Q2. Expected distress cost [L2]

A real-estate developer has a VU of ₹6,000 crore. Management proposes raising debt from ₹1,500 crore to ₹3,000 crore. CRA default studies suggest 5-year cumulative PDs of 3% at the current A rating and 15% at the expected BBB−. The estimated distress cost is 30% of VU, because customer bookings would collapse. With t = 25.168% and permanent debt, compute the change in the PV of tax shields and in the expected distress cost, and conclude.

💡 Hint: Compare marginal values: the change in tD against the change in PD × cost × VU.

Q3. Indirect costs in a case [L2]

An airline's lenders report that it breached its DSCR covenant in Q2. Describe the sequence of indirect distress costs likely to follow over the next 6–12 months, linking each to the relevant contractual or regulatory trigger (penal charges, SMA classification, rating action, cross-default).

Q4. Interview: tangible vs intangible [L1]

Explain why an IT services company and a cement company with identical EBITDA might rationally choose very different debt levels under the trade-off theory.
