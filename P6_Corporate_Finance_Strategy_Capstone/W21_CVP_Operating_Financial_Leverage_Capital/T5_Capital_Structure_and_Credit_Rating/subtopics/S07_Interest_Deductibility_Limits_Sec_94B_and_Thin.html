Subtopic note

Interest Deductibility Limits: Sec 94B and Thin Capitalisation

Scope: Interest deductibility limits: Sec 94B (30% of EBITDA for interest to non-resident AEs), thin capitalisation

Why it matters

The tax shield is the main benefit of debt in the trade-off theory, and tax law can cap it. Indian subsidiaries of multinationals, and Indian companies borrowing from overseas group entities through ECBs, face Sec 94B. It limits deductible interest on debt from (or guaranteed by) non-resident associated enterprises to 30% of EBITDA, so interest above that loses its shield. This affects:

  • the WACC (the effective tax rate on interest falls);
  • the debt vs equity funding choice for Indian subsidiaries;
  • the cash tax forecast in the model;
  • transfer pricing documentation.

FP&A and tax teams must model the limitation and its carry-forward explicitly. Otherwise the post-tax cost of intra-group debt is understated.

Core notes

Background: BEPS Action 4 and thin capitalisation

"Thin capitalisation" describes multinationals funding subsidiaries with excessive debt rather than equity. Interest is deductible in the high-tax subsidiary and taxed at a lower rate in the lender's jurisdiction, which shifts profits. The OECD/G20 BEPS Action 4 report (2015) recommended a fixed-ratio rule limiting net interest deductions to 10–30% of EBITDA. India adopted this through Sec 94B, inserted by the Finance Act 2017 with effect from AY 2018-19. Before that, India had no general thin-cap rule. It relied on transfer pricing (arm's-length interest rates) and RBI's ECB rules (all-in-cost ceilings and debt-equity limits for borrowing from foreign equity holders).

Sec 94B: the mechanics

Who it applies to. An Indian company, or the permanent establishment of a foreign company in India, that is a borrower and:

  • incurs interest or similar expenditure exceeding ₹1 crore in a previous year;
  • in respect of debt issued by a non-resident associated enterprise (AE, as defined in Sec 92A).

Debt from a non-associated lender is also covered where an AE provides an implicit or explicit guarantee, or deposits a matching amount of funds with the lender (a back-to-back arrangement).

Limitation. Interest is disallowed to the extent of the excess interest, which is the lower of:

  1. total interest paid or payable in excess of 30% of EBITDA of the previous year; and
  2. the interest paid or payable to the AEs (including the AE-guaranteed portion).

\[ \text{Excess interest} = \min\left(\text{Total interest} - 30\% \times \text{EBITDA}, \; \text{Interest on AE-related debt}\right), \quad \text{floored at 0} \]

Carry forward. Disallowed interest can be carried forward for 8 assessment years and deducted in future years to the extent of the shortfall below the 30% limit in those years.

Exclusions. Banking and insurance businesses are excluded. Later amendments excluded interest paid by Indian branches (PEs) of foreign banks, and specified classes of NBFCs notified by the Government. Check the latest notification.

EBITDA. The section does not define EBITDA in detail. It is generally computed from the books (profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation), but positions vary on tax-adjusted EBITDA and on the treatment of exempt income. Rulings and practice differ, so document the basis adopted.

Worked illustration

An Indian subsidiary of a German group, FY25 (₹ crore):

Item Amount
EBITDA 300
30% of EBITDA 90
Interest on ECB from German parent (AE) 85
Interest on Indian bank loan guaranteed by parent 20
Interest on unguaranteed Indian bank loan 15
Total interest 120
Excess over 30% limit: 120 − 90 30
AE-related interest: 85 + 20 105
Disallowed (lower of 30 and 105) 30

At 25.168%, the tax cost of the disallowance is about ₹7.6 crore this year, with a carry-forward of ₹30 crore usable only if future interest falls below 30% of future EBITDA. The effective after-tax cost of the marginal AE debt is Kd × (1 − 0) on the disallowed slice, not Kd × (1 − t).

Other interest-deductibility provisions (thin-cap in a broad sense)

  • Transfer pricing (Sec 92–92F). The interest rate on AE loans must be at arm's length, benchmarked to the borrower's standalone credit rating and market rates. Excess interest is adjusted. Safe-harbour rules exist for some inbound loans.
  • Sec 36(1)(iii). Interest on capital borrowed for business is deductible. Interest attributable to capital acquired for extending an existing business must be capitalised until the asset is first put to use, as tax depreciation is.
  • Sec 43B. Interest on loans from scheduled banks, NBFCs and other specified institutions is deductible only on actual payment. Converting interest into a new loan does not count as payment.
  • Sec 40(a)(i). Interest paid to non-residents without TDS is disallowed until TDS is deducted and paid. TDS is generally 20% u/s 195, or a concessional rate for qualifying ECBs and bonds, such as 5% u/s 194LC where applicable. Check sunset dates.
  • Sec 14A. Interest attributable to exempt income is disallowed (Rule 8D).
  • GAAR (Chapter X-A). Arrangements whose main purpose is a tax benefit, such as back-to-back debt structures, can be recharacterised.
  • Hybrid instruments. CCDs and redeemable preference shares may be recharacterised as debt or equity for tax purposes, depending on the terms.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 renumbers these provisions for tax years from 2026-27. Use the official mapping. The substance of the interest-limitation rule is carried forward, but verify the details.

RBI ECB framework interface

Borrowing from foreign equity holders under the ECB framework is subject to:

  • all-in-cost ceilings over the benchmark rate;
  • end-use restrictions;
  • a debt-to-equity ratio limit for borrowings from direct foreign equity holders above a threshold.

RBI revises the ECB framework periodically. Verify the current master direction. The combined effect is that the tax and regulatory frameworks both push intra-group financing towards equity.

Modelling Sec 94B

In the tax schedule:

  1. compute the 30% EBITDA cap each year;
  2. compute the excess interest as the lower of the two limbs;
  3. add it back to taxable income;
  4. track the carry-forward pool with its expiry (8 years);
  5. utilise it against later headroom;
  6. reflect the effect in the cash tax and the effective Kd(1 − t) used in WACC.

In Excel:

=MAX(0, MIN(TotalInterest - 0.3*EBITDA, AE_Interest))
=MIN(CF_Pool_Open, MAX(0, 0.3*EBITDA - TotalInterest))

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Low-EBITDA or loss years. In ramp-up years the 30% cap is small, so most AE interest is disallowed. The carry-forward may expire before EBITDA recovers. Consider equity or CCD funding during construction.
  • Capitalised interest. Whether interest capitalised into an asset counts as "interest" for Sec 94B in the year incurred is debated. The capitalised amount is recovered through depreciation, which is not interest. Document the position.
  • Guarantee fees charged by the parent are separately subject to transfer pricing, and the guarantee itself brings third-party debt within Sec 94B.
  • Group restructuring. Converting AE debt into equity (or CCDs) removes the limitation but changes repatriation: dividends taxed at DTAA rates against interest TDS.
  • Effect on WACC. For a company over the cap, the marginal tax shield on AE debt is zero, so the debt's after-tax cost equals its pre-tax cost. Relevered beta formulas should use the effective tax rate on interest.

Key takeaways

  • Sec 94B (from AY 2018-19) caps deductible interest on debt from, or guaranteed by, non-resident AEs at 30% of EBITDA, where interest exceeds ₹1 crore.
  • The disallowance is the lower of the interest in excess of 30% of EBITDA and the AE-related interest. It can be carried forward 8 years.
  • Banks, insurers and notified NBFCs are excluded. EBITDA is not precisely defined, so document the basis.
  • Thin capitalisation is also policed through transfer pricing, Sec 43B, Sec 40(a)(i), Sec 14A, GAAR and RBI's ECB limits.
  • Model the limitation and its carry-forward explicitly. For a company over the cap, the effective after-tax Kd on marginal AE debt equals the pre-tax Kd.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute the disallowance [L1]

An Indian subsidiary has EBITDA of ₹180 crore. Its interest comprises ₹48 crore on a loan from its Singapore parent, ₹12 crore on an Indian bank loan backed by a parent guarantee, and ₹10 crore on working-capital loans with no group support. Compute the Sec 94B disallowance and the tax cost at 25.168%.

Q2. Carry-forward utilisation [L2]

Using a disallowance of ₹25 crore in FY25, project utilisation over FY26–FY28, where EBITDA is ₹200 crore, ₹260 crore and ₹320 crore and total interest is ₹62 crore, ₹66 crore and ₹70 crore. Show the carry-forward pool each year.

💡 Hint: Utilisation in a year equals the shortfall of interest below 30% of that year's EBITDA, capped at the pool.

Q3. Debt vs equity funding for a subsidiary [L3]

A US parent must fund ₹800 crore of capex in its Indian subsidiary, whose EBITDA is ₹150 crore, rising to ₹400 crore over 4 years. Compare (a) an ECB from the parent at 8%, (b) equity, and (c) CCDs at 8% with conversion in year 5. Consider Sec 94B, transfer pricing, TDS, dividend repatriation tax under the DTAA (assume 15%), and the RBI ECB constraints. Recommend a structure.

Q4. Interview: thin capitalisation [L1]

Explain what thin capitalisation is, why tax authorities limit it, and how India's approach compares with the OECD BEPS Action 4 recommendation.
