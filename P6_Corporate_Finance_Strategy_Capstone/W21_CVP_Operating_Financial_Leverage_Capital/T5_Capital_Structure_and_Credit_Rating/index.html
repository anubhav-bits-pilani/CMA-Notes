Roadmap topic: Capital Structure & Credit Rating Impact: Modeling interest coverage, Net Debt/EBITDA thresholds, and tax shield benefits vs financial distress costs

Overview

Capital structure is the choice of how much debt a company carries relative to equity, and of what kind. Theory frames this as a trade-off: debt adds value through interest tax shields, and destroys value through expected financial distress costs. In practice Indian CFOs implement the trade-off through credit metrics and ratings. The board sets a target rating, typically "A+ or better" or "AA category". Lenders impose covenants on Net debt/EBITDA, DSCR and TOL/TNW. The finance team then sizes debt so that the plan stays within those thresholds in a downside case. This topic teaches both layers: the theory that tells you why there is an optimum, and the metrics, methodologies and covenants that tell you where the binding constraints actually are.

For FP&A, corporate finance and credit roles, the deliverables are concrete:

  • a credit-metric dashboard in the 3-statement model, using lender-exact definitions;
  • covenant headroom on TTM and quarterly bases;
  • an implied rating and spread for each plan year;
  • the debt capacity available for capex, M&A or buybacks;
  • a WACC curve showing the optimal D/E range;
  • a Sec 94B-adjusted tax shield for multinationals' Indian subsidiaries.

Consistency errors are common. Typical examples are leases left out of net debt while EBITDA is post-Ind AS 116, book weights in WACC, and annual figures used when lenders test TTM. Avoiding them distinguishes credible analysis.

The learning sequence runs as follows:

  1. Measurement. Credit metrics (s01, c01) and the definition of net debt (s03, c02).
  2. Rating framework. How agencies use those metrics (s02).
  3. Theory. MM with taxes, the trade-off, pecking order and market timing (s04, c03, c04), with tax shields weighed against distress costs (s05).
  4. Contracts and tax law. How covenants (s06) and interest limitation rules (s07) bound the choice.
  5. Integration. The rating-implied WACC curve (s08, c05) and debt capacity (c06), which feed straight back into topic t4's allocation decisions.

Related concepts to study

Week 6/7: solvency ratios and forensic red flags

→ Week 6/7: solvency ratios and forensic red flags (CWIP build-up, related-party loans)

The credit metrics here are the forward-looking version of the solvency analysis in Weeks 6–7. The forensic red flags matter because they distort the inputs:

  • A growing CWIP balance with no commissioning means debt has been drawn for assets not yet producing EBITDA. Leverage is understated if you look only at the current ratio, and capitalised interest (Ind AS 23) inflates EBIT coverage.
  • Loans to related parties, inter-corporate deposits to promoter entities, and corporate guarantees for group companies are hidden leverage.
  • Window-dressed year-end cash flatters net debt.

Use the Week 7 checklist alongside the s03 net-debt build. Adjust for these items before you map metrics to a rating.

Week 15: debt schedules, revolver logic and interest circularity

→ Week 15: debt schedules, revolver logic and interest circularity in 3-statement models

Every metric in this topic is an output of the Week 15 debt schedule:

  • opening debt, drawdowns, scheduled repayments, cash sweeps and revolver draws;
  • the minimum cash balance;
  • interest on average balances, which creates circularity.

Covenant tests need the schedule at quarterly granularity, or at least TTM at each quarter end. The cash sweep in s06 and the Kd-depends-on-rating loop in s08 add new circular references. Reuse the Week 15 techniques: a circuit-breaker toggle, interest on opening balances, or a copy-paste macro. Make sure the credit-metric block reads from the same debt and cash lines as the balance sheet, so the balance check still holds.

Week 22: acquisition debt capacity and post-deal leverage

→ Week 22: acquisition debt capacity and post-deal leverage in accretion/dilution models

Acquisitions are where debt capacity (c06) is tested hardest. In Week 22 you will:

  • combine acquirer and target net debt, plus new acquisition debt, against pro-forma combined EBITDA (including or excluding synergies, depending on the covenant definition);
  • check whether the combined entity keeps its rating;
  • size the equity or hybrid component needed.

Rating agencies usually tolerate a temporary leverage spike if there is a credible deleveraging path, typically back below the trigger within 18–24 months. They may still place the rating on Watch Negative. Accretion/dilution analysis also uses the earnings-yield vs after-tax-cost logic from topic t4.

SEBI large-corporate framework and NCD issuance

SEBI large-corporate framework (incremental borrowing via debt securities) and NCD issuance under SEBI NCS Regulations

SEBI's large-corporate (LC) framework, introduced in 2018 and revised from FY25, requires listed entities above a borrowing and rating threshold to raise a minimum share of incremental borrowings through debt securities. The thresholds have been revised over time, for example outstanding long-term borrowings of ₹1,000 crore or more and a rating of AA or above. The aim is to deepen the corporate bond market. The share-raising requirement is tested over a block of years, and shortfalls must be disclosed.

NCD issuance is governed by the SEBI (Issue and Listing of Non-Convertible Securities) Regulations, 2021. It requires:

  • a credit rating;
  • a debenture trustee;
  • a security cover (where secured);
  • a recovery expense fund;
  • continuous disclosure under LODR Chapter V.

For capital structure decisions, the LC framework shifts the funding mix towards bonds. That brings incurrence-style covenants, bullet maturities and market pricing that is linked directly to the rating.

Ind AS 109 EIR amortisation and Ind AS 107 liquidity disclosures

Ind AS 109 EIR amortisation of upfront fees; Ind AS 107 liquidity risk disclosures

Under Ind AS 109, borrowings are carried at amortised cost. Upfront processing fees, arranger fees and other transaction costs are deducted from the initial carrying amount and amortised through the effective interest rate. Finance cost in the P&L is therefore higher than the coupon. Covenant definitions may use either figure, so check which.

On prepayment or substantial modification (the 10% test), unamortised fees are written off (t4 s03).

Ind AS 107 requires liquidity-risk disclosures:

  • a maturity analysis of financial liabilities on contractual undiscounted cash flows;
  • how liquidity risk is managed;
  • disclosure of covenant breaches and defaults during the period (Ind AS 107.18–19).

These notes are the analyst's primary source for refinancing walls and covenant history.

Common pitfalls

Lease liabilities excluded from net debt with post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA

Excluding lease liabilities from net debt while using post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA (inconsistent ratio).

The mistake. Computing Net debt/EBITDA with borrowings only in the numerator, while reported EBITDA, which already excludes rent, is used as the denominator.

Why people make it. Old covenant templates and peer databases define debt as "borrowings". Lease liabilities sit in a separate line under Schedule III.

Correct treatment. Match the perimeters (s03, c02). Either include lease liabilities in net debt with post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA, or exclude them and use EBITDA after deducting rent. For lease-heavy sectors (retail, QSR, airlines, hospitals) the difference can exceed 1–2x.

How to detect it in review. Lease-heavy companies appear to have very low leverage. The ratio changes sharply between FY19 and FY20 (the Ind AS 116 transition) with no change in the business. Peers on different bases are compared side by side.

Ignoring that Kd rises as leverage pushes the rating down

Ignoring that the cost of debt itself rises as leverage pushes the rating down.

The mistake. Holding Kd constant at today's rate across all leverage scenarios. WACC then appears to fall indefinitely as debt rises.

Why people make it. Kd is usually a single input cell copied from the current borrowing rate, and modelling the rating-spread link takes extra work.

Correct treatment. Map coverage or leverage to a synthetic rating and spread at each debt level (s08). Recompute Kd and iterate for circularity. Remember that a downgrade reprices new debt at once and existing floating-rate debt at reset, and it may trigger step-up clauses.

How to detect it in review. The WACC curve has no minimum. Kd is identical across scenarios with very different ICRs. The model has no rating or spread lookup.

Book-value weights in WACC

Using book-value weights from the balance sheet instead of market-value/target weights in WACC.

The mistake. Weighting Ke and Kd by book equity and book debt.

Why people make it. Book values are easy to read off the balance sheet. Some textbook CMA problems use them. Market value seems "circular" in a valuation.

Correct treatment. Use market values, or a target capital structure, consistent with the D/E used to relever beta (c05). For high-ROE Indian companies, market equity can be 10–20 times book, so book weights hugely overstate the debt weight and understate WACC. For the valuation circularity, use a target ratio or iterate.

How to detect it in review. D/V in the WACC calculation matches the balance-sheet D/E exactly. The relevered beta uses a different D/E from the weights. WACC is implausibly low for a high-P/B company.

Annual covenant testing when lenders test TTM or quarterly

Testing covenants on annual figures when lenders test on trailing-twelve-month or quarterly bases.

The mistake. Checking covenant compliance only at 31 March using annual EBITDA, when the facility tests at each quarter end on TTM figures. Some facilities test half-yearly, or on annualised quarterly EBITDA.

Why people make it. The model is built on annual periods, and the budget is annual.

Correct treatment. Build a quarterly (or at least quarter-end TTM) covenant module. Use the exact test dates and definitions in the facility agreement. Show headroom by quarter in base and downside cases (s06). For seasonal businesses, check the peak-debt quarter.

How to detect it in review. The model has no quarterly EBITDA or debt figures. Compliance certificates have been signed on figures that differ from the model. Breaches are discovered after the quarter close rather than forecast.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated credit review of an expansion plan [L3]

A listed cement company (rated AA−; downgrade trigger Net debt/EBITDA > 2.25x sustained; covenant 3.0x tested quarterly on TTM) has FY25 figures (₹ crore):

  • EBITDA 2,400 (post-Ind AS 116); EBIT 1,750; finance cost 310;
  • borrowings 4,300; lease liabilities 350; cash and liquid funds 900 (of which 120 is DSRA);
  • tax rate 25.168%.

It plans a ₹5,000 crore expansion, spent 50:50 in FY26 and FY27, with incremental EBITDA of ₹700 crore from FY28. Base EBITDA grows 5% a year, and FCF before expansion capex and dividends is ₹1,300 crore a year. Dividends are ₹300 crore a year.

Required:

  1. Compute current Net debt/EBITDA and ICR.
  2. Compute year-end leverage in FY26–FY28 if the expansion is fully debt-funded.
  3. Identify whether the rating trigger or the covenant binds, and when.
  4. Compute the equity or hybrid needed to keep leverage ≤ 2.25x throughout.
  5. Recommend a funding plan.

Q2. Optimal capital structure for a debt-free company [L3]

A debt-free listed specialty chemicals company has stable EBIT of ₹900 crore, βu 0.85, t 25.168%, Rf 7.0%, ERP 7.0%, market value of ₹14,000 crore and FCFF growth of 5%. Using the rating grid in s08, build the WACC curve for D/V of 0% to 50% in 10% steps.

Required:

  1. Identify the WACC-minimising D/V and its rating.
  2. Compute the debt that could be raised at the optimum.
  3. Use the trade-off framework to argue whether the company should borrow up to the optimum to fund a buyback. Consider distress costs for a business with key-customer concentration of 40%.
  4. List the regulatory steps required for such a leveraged buyback under Sec 68 and the SEBI regulations.

💡 Hint: Iterate Kd at each step, and apply the Sec 68 post-buyback D/E test alongside the rating floor.

Q3. Indian subsidiary of a multinational: debt vs equity [L2]

An Indian subsidiary of a Japanese group needs ₹1,200 crore for capacity expansion. Its EBITDA is ₹250 crore. The options are:

  • (a) an ECB from the parent at 6.5%;
  • (b) an Indian bank term loan at 9.0%, guaranteed by the parent;
  • (c) an equity infusion.

The tax rate is 25.168%, and dividend repatriation is taxed at 10% under the DTAA.

Required:

  1. Compute the Sec 94B disallowance under (a) and (b).
  2. Compute the effective after-tax cost of each debt option.
  3. Compute the resulting ICR and Net debt/EBITDA.
  4. Recommend a structure, considering transfer pricing and the RBI ECB framework.

Q4. Covenant reset negotiation [L3]

A QSR chain has the following figures:

  • post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA of ₹520 crore and rent of ₹260 crore;
  • borrowings of ₹700 crore, lease liabilities of ₹1,600 crore and cash of ₹180 crore.

Its bank covenant (signed in 2018) is "Debt/EBITDA ≤ 2.0x", where debt means borrowings and EBITDA is pre-Ind AS 116. The CFO's team reports compliance using post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA with borrowings only.

Required:

  1. Compute the ratio on the bank's original basis, on the CFO's basis, and on a fully consistent post-Ind AS 116 basis.
  2. Explain the inconsistency.
  3. Draft the revised covenant definitions and levels you would propose to the bank.
  4. Set out how the issue should be disclosed under Ind AS 107 if a breach is confirmed.
