Key concept

Trade-Off Theory: Tax Shields Less Expected Distress Costs

Concept: Trade-off: V_L = V_U + PV(tax shields) − PV(expected financial distress costs)

Statement

\[ V_L = V_U + \text{PV(tax shields)} - \text{PV(expected financial distress costs)} \]

where:

  • \(V_U\) = the unlevered firm value;
  • PV(tax shields) = the present value of interest tax savings, given the debt policy (\(tD\) for permanent debt, less for rebalanced debt or when deductions may be lost);
  • PV(expected financial distress costs) = Σ over time of [probability of distress × cost of distress], discounted. It covers direct costs (IBC process, legal, liquidation losses) and indirect costs (lost sales, supplier tightening, underinvestment, covenant penalties).

The optimal debt level \(D^*\) satisfies:

\[ \frac{\partial\, \text{PV(tax shields)}}{\partial D} = \frac{\partial\, \text{PV(distress costs)}}{\partial D} \]

Explanation

At low leverage the probability of distress is negligible, so each extra rupee of debt adds close to \(t\) of value. As leverage rises, default probability rises non-linearly. The expected tax shield also becomes less certain, because the firm may not have taxable profits in bad states. The marginal benefit falls and the marginal cost rises, so the value curve is concave with an interior maximum.

What shifts the optimum:

Factor Effect on D*
Higher marginal tax rate ↑ (bigger shield)
Tangible, redeployable assets (cement plants, toll roads) ↑ (lower loss given distress)
Stable, contracted cash flows ↑ (lower PD at any leverage)
Intangible assets, brand or human capital (IT, pharma R&D) ↓
Products needing long-term customer trust (aircraft, durables, housing) ↓ (high indirect costs)
Non-debt tax shields (large tax depreciation, Sec 35AD, losses) ↓ (shield less valuable)
Implicit government or promoter support ↑ (lower expected costs)

Limitations:

  • The theory cannot explain why the most profitable firms, which have the biggest taxable profits, often borrow least. Pecking order explains this.
  • It also cannot explain why firms do not rebalance quickly. Dynamic trade-off, with adjustment costs, explains that.

In practice, firms implement the trade-off through target rating policies.

Illustration

\(V_U\) = ₹8,000 crore and t = 25.168%. Distress cost if it occurs is 25% of \(V_U\), or ₹2,000 crore. Permanent debt.

Debt (₹ cr) PV tax shield (tD) PD (cumulative) PV expected distress cost \(V_L\)
0 0 0.5% 10 7,990
2,000 503 2% 40 8,463
3,000 755 6% 120 8,635
4,000 1,007 15% 300 8,707
5,000 1,258 30% 600 8,658

Value peaks near ₹4,000 crore of debt. Between 4,000 and 5,000 the marginal shield (+251) is smaller than the marginal expected distress cost (+300).

Practice Questions

Q1. Find the optimum [L2]

VU is ₹5,000 crore, t = 25.168%, permanent debt, and the distress cost is 20% of VU. Cumulative PDs are 1%, 3%, 8%, 18% and 35% at debt of ₹0, ₹1,000 crore, ₹2,000 crore, ₹3,000 crore and ₹4,000 crore. Compute VL at each level and identify the optimum.

💡 Hint: Compare the marginal change in tD with the marginal change in expected distress cost between adjacent levels.

Q2. Sector comparison [L2]

Using the factors table, rank the following by expected optimal leverage and justify the ranking: a regulated power distribution company, an IT services firm, a real-estate developer selling under-construction flats, and an FMCG company.

Q3. Non-debt tax shields [L1]

Explain why a company claiming large accelerated tax depreciation, or carrying brought-forward losses, has a lower optimal debt ratio under the trade-off theory.
