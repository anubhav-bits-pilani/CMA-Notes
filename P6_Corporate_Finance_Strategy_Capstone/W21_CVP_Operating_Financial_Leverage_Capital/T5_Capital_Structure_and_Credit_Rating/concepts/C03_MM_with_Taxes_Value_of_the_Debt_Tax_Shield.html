Key concept

MM with Taxes: Value of the Debt Tax Shield

Concept: MM with taxes (perpetual debt): V_L = V_U + t × D; PV of tax shield = t × D

Statement

With corporate taxes, perpetual (permanent) debt and no distress costs:

\[ V_L = V_U + t \times D, \qquad \text{PV(tax shield)} = \frac{t \times K_d \times D}{K_d} = t \times D \]

where:

  • \(V_L\) = value of the levered firm;
  • \(V_U\) = value of the identical unlevered firm, \(\text{EBIT}(1 - t)/K_u\) for a no-growth firm;
  • \(t\) = the corporate tax rate at which interest is deducted;
  • \(D\) = the market value of permanent debt;
  • \(K_d\) = the cost of debt, used as the discount rate for the shield because the shield is as risky as the debt.

The accompanying cost-of-equity relation (MM Proposition II with taxes):

\[ K_e = K_u + (K_u - K_d)(1 - t)\frac{D}{E} \]

Explanation

Each year the company saves \(t \times K_d \times D\) of tax. If the debt is permanent, this is a perpetuity, and discounting at Kd gives \(tD\). Government's claim on the firm (taxes) falls by exactly that amount, so the claims of debt plus equity rise.

The assumptions are essential to the result:

  • Permanent, fixed debt. If debt is rebalanced to a constant D/V, the shields fluctuate with firm value and should be discounted at Ku (Miles–Ezzell or Harris–Pringle). The PV is then lower than \(tD\).
  • Full and immediate use of the deduction. Loss-making companies, or interest capped by Sec 94B (s07), get less.
  • No personal taxes. Miller (1977) shows that personal taxes on interest can offset much of the corporate gain.
  • No distress costs. Adding them gives the trade-off theory (c04).

The literal implication, maximum value at 100% debt, is why MM with taxes is a starting point, not a policy. In India, the Sec 115BAA rate of 25.168% makes the shield worth about ₹25 per ₹100 of permanent debt, against about ₹35 under the 34.944% old-regime rate.

Illustration

An unlevered company has EBIT of ₹400 crore in perpetuity, t = 25.168%, and Ku = 12%.

  • \(V_U = 400 \times 0.74832 / 0.12 = 2,494\), that is, ₹2,494 crore.
  • It issues ₹1,000 crore of permanent debt at 9% and buys back shares.
  • \(V_L = 2,494 + 0.25168 \times 1,000 = 2,746\), that is, ₹2,746 crore.
  • \(E = 2,746 - 1,000 = 1,746\), that is, ₹1,746 crore.
  • \(K_e = 12\% + (12\% - 9\%) \times 0.74832 \times 1,000/1,746 = 12\% + 1.29\% = 13.29\%\).
  • WACC = (1,746/2,746)(13.29%) + (1,000/2,746)(9% × 0.74832) = 8.45% + 2.45% = 10.90%. Check: 400 × 0.74832 ÷ 0.1090 ≈ ₹2,746 crore.

Connections

  • s04 places this result among the theories. s05 values tax shields for scheduled and rebalanced debt.
  • c04 extends it with distress costs. c05 shows the WACC form, where the tax effect appears as Kd(1 − t).
  • t4 c02: the Hamada beta formula follows from these assumptions.
  • Week 16 and Week 17: APV valuation (unlevered value + PV of tax shields) is the practical application in LBO and project valuation.

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply MM with taxes [L1]

An unlevered firm has EBIT of ₹250 crore in perpetuity, t = 25.168%, and Ku = 13%. It issues ₹800 crore of permanent debt at 8.5%. Compute VU, VL, E, Ke and WACC, and verify that WACC equals Ku(1 − tD/V).

Q2. Regime change [L2]

A company moves from the old tax regime (34.944%) to Sec 115BAA (25.168%) and carries ₹3,000 crore of permanent debt. Compute the change in the PV of its tax shield under MM, and discuss whether its optimal leverage should change.

💡 Hint: Consider both the direct change in tD and what the trade-off theory implies about the marginal benefit of debt.

Q3. Interview: when is tD wrong? [L2]

Give three situations in which the PV of an Indian company's tax shield is materially less than tD, and say how you would value it instead in each case.
