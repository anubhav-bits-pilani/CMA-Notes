Key concept

Blume-Adjusted Beta and After-Tax Cost of Debt

Concept: Blume-adjusted beta = 0.67 × Raw beta + 0.33 × 1; Kd (after tax) = (Rf + rating spread) × (1 − t)

Statement

\[ \beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.67 \times \beta_{\text{raw}} + 0.33 \times 1 \]

\[ K_d^{\text{after-tax}} = (R_f + s_{\text{rating}}) \times (1 - t) \]

where \(\beta_{\text{raw}}\) is the regression (historical) beta of the stock against a market index, 1 is the market beta, \(R_f\) the risk-free rate (10-year G-sec yield for INR), \(s_{\text{rating}}\) the credit spread for the company's actual or synthetic credit rating at a comparable tenor, and \(t\) the marginal tax rate at which interest is deductible (25.168% for a domestic company under Sec 115BAA of the 1961 Act, carried into the Income-tax Act, 2025).

Explanation

Blume adjustment. Marshall Blume's 1971 work found that betas tend to revert towards the market average of 1 over time. The 0.67/0.33 weighting (often quoted as 2/3 and 1/3) shrinks extreme betas: a raw beta of 1.6 becomes 1.40, a raw beta of 0.5 becomes 0.67. Bloomberg's "adjusted beta" uses this formula. Defence points:

  • It is a forward-looking estimate for valuations with long horizons; it reduces estimation error from the regression.
  • Apply it to raw betas before unlevering when building peer betas, and state consistently that you have done so.
  • It is not appropriate to "adjust" an already adjusted beta from a data provider.
  • Alternatives: Vasicek (Bayesian shrinkage using the beta's standard error), industry/peer median betas.

Cost of debt from rating spread. The cost of debt must be the marginal, market-based rate at which the company could borrow today for a long tenor, not the historical book coupon. Building it as risk-free plus a rating spread anchors it to observable market data (corporate bond spreads by rating from FBIL/FIMMDA matrices, rating agency publications, traded NCDs). For unrated companies, a synthetic rating from interest coverage (EBIT ÷ interest) maps to a spread. The tax shield applies only if the company has taxable profits to absorb interest; for loss-making companies, use a lower effective rate. Interest deductibility limits under Sec 94B (thin capitalisation, 30% of EBITDA for interest paid to non-resident associated enterprises) can also restrict the shield for MNC subsidiaries.

Illustration

An Indian logistics company: raw 2-year weekly beta vs Nifty 500 = 1.30; rating AA−; 10-year G-sec 6.6%; AA− 10-year spread 1.6%; tax 25.168%.

  • \(\beta_{\text{adj}}\) = 0.67 × 1.30 + 0.33 = 0.871 + 0.33 = 1.20
  • Pre-tax Kd = 6.6% + 1.6% = 8.2%
  • After-tax Kd = 8.2% × (1 − 0.25168) = 6.14%
  • With ERP 7%: Ke = 6.6% + 1.20 × 7% = 15.0% (versus 15.7% with raw beta) — the adjustment alone moves Ke by 70 bp.

Practice Questions

Q1. Adjust and compute [L1]

Raw betas for three listed peers are 0.62, 1.05 and 1.48. Compute Blume-adjusted betas, explain the direction of each adjustment, and compute after-tax Kd for a company rated A+ (spread 2.2%) with G-sec at 6.5% and tax at 25.168%.

💡 Hint: The adjustment always moves beta towards 1.

Q2. Synthetic rating [L2]

An unlisted company has EBIT ₹90 crore and interest ₹30 crore. Using an illustrative mapping (coverage above 4.0× = AA, spread 1.3%; 2.5–4.0× = A, spread 2.4%; 1.5–2.5× = BBB, spread 3.6%), determine its synthetic rating, pre-tax and after-tax Kd (G-sec 6.6%, tax 25.168%), and compare with its 10.5% bank loan rate. Which would you use and why?

Q3. Tax shield challenge [L2]

A start-up with ₹400 crore of accumulated tax losses (expected to be used by FY30) borrows at 11%. An interviewer asks what tax rate to use in Kd for FY27–FY29 and the terminal period. Give a structured answer and explain how you would reflect it in a DCF.
