Subtopic note

Working Capital Drivers and the "Profits Up, Cash Down" Case

Scope: Working capital drivers and cash conversion questions ("profits up but cash down — why?")

Why it matters

"Profit is up 20% but operating cash flow fell — explain" is the most common FP&A interview case after variance analysis, and a staple question for credit analysts, KPO equity research and controllership roles. It tests whether you can move from accrual accounting to cash, identify which working capital driver moved, quantify it in days and rupees, and propose actions. Indian businesses are particularly working-capital intensive (distributor credit, GST input credit timing, government receivables, MSME payment rules), so interviewers expect India-specific causes in the answer.

Core notes

From profit to cash: the bridge

\[ \text{CFO} = \text{PAT} + \text{Non-cash charges} - \Delta \text{NWC} - (\text{Tax paid} - \text{Tax expense}) \]

and free cash flow subtracts capex. When PAT rises but CFO falls, the culprit is almost always one of:

  1. Working capital build — receivables, inventory, contract assets up; payables, advances down.
  2. Non-cash income — FV gains, reversal of provisions, unrealised FX gains, Ind AS 115 revenue recognised ahead of billing.
  3. Tax timing — advance tax, MAT payments, refunds stuck.
  4. Classification — items moving between operating, investing and financing (e.g., interest received is investing under Ind AS 7.33, so higher treasury income raises PAT but not CFO).

For interviews, open with working capital, quantify in days, then mention the other three as checks.

The working capital drivers

Driver Metric Typical causes of deterioration Indian context
Receivables DSO = Receivables ÷ Revenue × 365 Channel stuffing at quarter-end, extended credit to win share, disputes, slower government/PSU payments DISCOM dues, state government contractors, distributor credit in FMCG/pharma
Inventory DIO = Inventory ÷ COGS × 365 Safety stock build for supply risk, new SKUs, slow-moving stock, forecast errors Pre-buying before price hikes or anti-dumping duties; festive season builds
Payables DPO = Payables ÷ COGS × 365 Suppliers demanding faster payment, loss of credit terms, MSME constraints Sec 43B(h): payments to micro/small enterprises beyond 45 days (or agreed shorter period) disallowed until paid, pushing faster payment from FY24
Contract assets/advances Unbilled revenue days; customer advance days Milestones delayed; customers stop prepaying EPC and IT services unbilled revenue; real estate customer advances under RERA escrow
Other GST input credit, TDS receivable, export incentives Refund delays, ITC mismatches (GSTR-2B), RoDTEP timing Cash locked in statutory receivables

Cash conversion cycle: \(CCC = DSO + DIO - DPO\). Each day of CCC ties up roughly \(\text{Revenue} \div 365\) of cash (more precisely, revenue for DSO days and COGS for DIO/DPO days).

Worked example

An Indian consumer durables company, ₹ crore:

FY25 FY26
Revenue 3,650 4,380
COGS 2,555 3,066
PAT 219 263
Receivables 400 600
Inventory 420 588
Payables 350 336
DSO (days) 40.0 50.0
DIO (days) 60.0 70.0
DPO (days) 50.0 40.0
CCC (days) 50.0 80.0

Net working capital rose from 470 to 852 — a ₹382 crore outflow. Splitting it:

  • Growth effect (holding FY25 days at FY26 volumes): receivables 4,380 × 40 ÷ 365 = 480 (+80), inventory 3,066 × 60 ÷ 365 = 504 (+84), payables 3,066 × 50 ÷ 365 = 420 (+70) → net +94.
  • Efficiency effect (change in days at FY26 volumes): receivables +120 (10 days × 12.0/day), inventory +84 (10 days × 8.4/day), payables +84 (10 days lost × 8.4/day) → net +288.

Headline: "PAT rose ₹44 crore, but working capital absorbed ₹382 crore — ₹94 crore is growth, which is acceptable, and ₹288 crore is a 30-day deterioration in the cash cycle: DSO +10 days from extended dealer credit, DIO +10 days from a pre-festive inventory build, and DPO −10 days as we paid MSME suppliers faster to comply with Sec 43B(h)."

Diagnostic routine in an interview

  1. Restate: "Profit is up X, CFO is down Y — the gap is Z."
  2. Bridge PAT to CFO: non-cash items, working capital, tax.
  3. Decompose working capital into days, separating growth from efficiency.
  4. Name the business causes behind each day movement (ask clarifying questions if the case allows).
  5. Quantify the cash prize of fixing it: "Bringing DSO back to 40 days releases ₹120 crore."
  6. Recommend actions and risks: credit policy tightening by segment, collection incentives, supply chain finance / TReDS for suppliers, inventory SKU rationalisation, vendor term renegotiation within MSME limits, dynamic discounting.

Model implementation

In a three-statement model, drive working capital by days, not by % of revenue, so FP&A can discuss levers:

Receivables_t = Revenue_t * DSO_t / 365
Inventory_t   = COGS_t * DIO_t / 365
Payables_t    = COGS_t * DPO_t / 365
Change in NWC (CFS) = -(NWC_t - NWC_{t-1})

In Power BI, a DAX measure for DSO on a rolling basis avoids seasonality distortion:

DSO (Rolling 3M) =
DIVIDE(
    [Closing Receivables],
    CALCULATE([Revenue], DATESINPERIOD('Date'[Date], MAX('Date'[Date]), -3, MONTH))
) * 91

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Year-end vs average balances: seasonal businesses show misleading year-end days; use average or monthly balances.
  • Gross vs net receivables: ECL allowances reduce receivables; a big write-off can "improve" DSO without cash.
  • Factoring and supply chain finance: derecognised receivables lower DSO and boost CFO; reverse factoring can inflate DPO — analysts adjust and the IASB 2023 supplier finance disclosures target this.
  • Revenue recognition: over-time revenue ahead of billing grows contract assets; include unbilled revenue in "receivables days" for a true picture.
  • Inflation and price: input price rises inflate inventory and payables in rupees even with constant days.
  • Advance tax and GST: high profit growth raises advance tax instalments (June, September, December, March), reducing cash before the tax expense is "final".

Key takeaways

  • Start with the PAT-to-CFO bridge; working capital is the usual explanation.
  • Express movements in days and separate growth from efficiency effects.
  • Tie each day movement to a business cause, with an Indian context (dealer credit, MSME 45-day rule, GST refunds).
  • Quantify the cash prize of each lever and recommend actions with risks.
  • Model working capital by days so the levers are visible in forecasts.

Practice Questions

Q1. Diagnose the cash gap [L2]

A pharma formulations company reports (₹ crore) FY25 → FY26: revenue 2,920 → 3,358; COGS 1,460 → 1,679; PAT 290 → 336; receivables 480 → 690; inventory 400 → 506; payables 240 → 230; CFO 310 → 180. Compute DSO, DIO, DPO and CCC for both years, split the working capital change into growth and efficiency effects, and draft a two-minute answer to the CFO.

💡 Hint: Use FY26 revenue and COGS per day to value both the growth and the efficiency components.

Q2. Non-working-capital causes [L1]

List and briefly explain four reasons other than working capital why PAT can rise while CFO falls for an Indian listed company, giving an example of each with an Ind AS or tax reference.

Q3. Cash prize and action plan [L2]

A distributor with revenue of ₹1,825 crore and DSO of 62 days wants to reach 45 days within a year without losing more than 3% of revenue. Compute the cash released, the interest saving at a 9.5% borrowing cost, and propose a segment-wise credit and collection plan with risks.

Q4. Interview case: supply chain finance [L3]

A hiring manager asks: "We introduced a reverse factoring programme and our DPO went from 55 to 90 days. Is our working capital really better?" Answer covering cash flow classification, how analysts and auditors view it, disclosure, covenant implications and the true economic effect, using illustrative numbers.
