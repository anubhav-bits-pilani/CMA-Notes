Roadmap topic: FP&A & Valuation Case Mock Interviews: Articulating WACC/DCF assumptions, working capital drivers, and PVM variance narratives concisely

Overview

FP&A, corporate finance and valuation interviews in India — at listed conglomerates, consumer and pharma companies, GCCs of US and European multinationals, KPO research houses and boutique valuation firms — converge on a common set of cases. A valuation round asks you to defend a WACC and DCF; an FP&A round asks you to explain why cash fell when profit rose, to decompose a revenue or EBITDA variance into price, volume, mix, FX and cost, and to work a business-partnering case such as a pricing decision or a capex request. The differentiator is rarely the technique: most candidates know CAPM and the Gordon growth formula. It is the ability to source and justify each assumption and to communicate a conclusion with numbers in under two minutes.

For a CMA, much of this is familiar territory in new clothing. Sales price, mix and quantity variances from standard costing are the PVM bridge; CVP and relevant costing are the pricing and capex cases; MIS reporting is FP&A commentary. Interviewers in India value that grounding, but they expect market-facing vocabulary (constant currency, CCC, TV share of EV, Blume-adjusted beta, driver trees) and current Indian data points (G-sec yields, rating spreads, Sec 115BAA rates, MSME payment rules).

The learning sequence: begin with the valuation inputs (s01 WACC defence, s02 DCF defence), then working capital and cash conversion (s03), which links valuation to operations. Next, PVM narratives (s04) and the business-partnering cases (s05) form the FP&A core, supported by frameworks (s06) that give structure to any open question. Finish with the answer structure (s07), which is how everything else gets delivered. The five concepts — working capital days, price/volume, mix, Blume beta and Kd, and the EBITDA bridge — are the formulas you should be able to write and apply from memory.

Practise each subtopic as a mock with a timer and recording. Score technical accuracy, structure, time management and communication; redo the weakest. Build a one-page "assumption sources" sheet for your portfolio DCF (date, source, value) so that every challenge — "why 7% ERP?", "why 5% terminal growth?" — has a sourced answer.

Related concepts to study

WACC/DCF, variance analysis and MIS commentary

→ Week 16: WACC/DCF build; → Week 19: variance analysis; → Week 20: MIS commentary

This topic is the interview-facing layer on top of three earlier builds. Week 16 produced your WACC and DCF — revisit it and annotate every input with a source and date, and run the WACC × g sensitivity so you know your value range by heart. Week 19's variance workbook gives the PVM mechanics; check that your formulas use actual quantities for price and budget weighted-average price for volume, and that the variances reconcile. Week 20's MIS commentary is the written equivalent of the two-minute answer: headline, drivers, actions. In mock interviews, refer to these artefacts by name and number — "my Week 16 DCF has terminal value at 71% of EV" — which turns abstract answers into portfolio evidence.

Capital budgeting in business-partnering interviews

→ Week 21: capital budgeting cases (NPV of a capex request) in business-partnering interviews

The capex request review is the most common business-partnering case, and it relies on Week 21's capital budgeting work. Interviewers look for incremental after-tax cash flows (including working capital build and tax depreciation at WDV rates), a hurdle rate tied to WACC plus a project risk premium, NPV and IRR with sensitivity to volume, price and delay, and recognition of real options (phasing, abandonment). They also test behavioural judgement: challenging an optimistic plant head's payback without rejecting a strategic project, and proposing post-investment reviews. Bring a one-page capex review template from Week 21 to mock interviews and practise walking through it in three minutes.

Rolling forecasts and zero-based budgeting

Rolling forecast and zero-based budgeting discussion points (→ Week 17–18)

Interviewers ask "how would you improve our planning process?" Two talking points from Weeks 17–18 recur. Rolling forecasts (typically 12–18 months, updated monthly or quarterly) shift focus from the annual budget to a continuously updated outlook driven by a small set of drivers; discuss forecast accuracy metrics (MAPE, bias), driver-based models and how they reduce sandbagging. Zero-based budgeting requires every cost to be justified from zero each cycle, used by Indian FMCG and consumer companies to reset overheads; discuss its cost (effort, managerial time) and where it fits (discretionary spend, SG&A) versus where it does not (committed costs). Have one example of each with a quantified outcome.

Unit economics for consumer and tech roles

Unit economics (CAC, LTV, contribution per order) for consumer/tech companies and GCC roles

Consumer internet, D2C, quick-commerce and SaaS companies — and GCCs supporting such businesses — evaluate performance on unit economics. CAC (customer acquisition cost) = sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers acquired; LTV (lifetime value) = average contribution per customer per period × expected lifetime (or ÷ churn rate), discounted; a commonly cited benchmark is LTV:CAC of 3× or more with CAC payback within 12–18 months. Contribution per order = AOV × gross margin − delivery, packaging, payment gateway and discounts. These map directly onto driver trees and PVM: price is AOV, volume is orders, mix is category share. Expect cases like "contribution per order is negative — what would you fix first?"

Common pitfalls

Answers without a headline number

Long, unstructured answers without a headline number. Candidates narrate their analysis in the order they did it, reaching the conclusion after three minutes. This happens because analysts are trained to show work and fear being wrong in a short statement. The correct approach is answer-first: one sentence with the key number and conclusion, then three ranked drivers with numbers, then next steps — under two minutes. To detect it in review, time your recordings and check whether a listener stopping after 15 seconds would know the answer; if not, rewrite the opening.

Mixing budget and actual quantities

Mixing budget and actual quantities in variance formulas so variances do not sum to total. A common error is computing price variance on budget quantities or volume at actual prices, which leaves an unexplained residual or double counts. It happens when formulas are written from memory without the reconciliation check. The correct convention: price on actual quantities; mix on actual total quantity at budget mix and budget prices (or margins); volume on the change in total quantity at the budget weighted-average price (or margin). Always include a check row (price + mix + volume − total = 0) in Excel and Power BI; a non-zero check reveals the error immediately.

Unsourced assumptions

Choosing assumptions (e.g. ERP, terminal growth) without being able to justify the source. Candidates use 8% ERP or 6% terminal growth because a template or tutorial did, and collapse when asked "why?". This is common because sourcing takes time and interviews rarely give notice. The correct treatment is to keep a dated source list: 10-year G-sec yield (RBI/FBIL), ERP (Damodaran India total ERP, implied ERP on the Nifty, Big 4 surveys), beta (peer median, Blume-adjusted, index and frequency stated), rating spread (FBIL matrix, rating agency data), terminal growth (long-run inflation target plus real growth, below nominal GDP). In review, challenge each input with "source and date?" — any input without both is a gap.

Variances without actions

Describing variances without the action or recommendation that follows. Candidates correctly explain that mix was adverse by ₹8 crore and stop. Analysts default to explanation because it feels safer than recommending to a business head. FP&A is valued for decisions: every material variance should end with an action (price pass-through, scheme redesign, cost programme), an owner and a timing, plus the risk if no action is taken. In review, check that each commentary paragraph or interview answer closes with "I recommend…" or "next step…", and that the action is quantified where possible.

Practice Questions

Q1. Valuation defence panel [L3]

You are presenting a DCF of an NSE-listed Indian specialty chemicals company: FY27–FY31 FCFF ₹220, 260, 300, 340, 370 crore; WACC 12.2% (Rf 6.6%, raw beta 1.15, ERP 7.0%, D/V 15%, pre-tax Kd 8.3%, tax 25.168%); terminal growth 5.5%; mid-year convention; net debt ₹450 crore; 50 crore shares; current price ₹128. (a) Recompute the WACC using a Blume-adjusted beta and state the difference. (b) Compute EV, equity value per share and TV share under both WACCs. (c) Build a WACC × g sensitivity grid (±1% in 0.5% steps). (d) Draft a two-minute investment committee answer and list the three challenges you expect, with your sourced responses.

💡 Hint: Discount year-t cash flows at t − 0.5 periods and keep the TV discounting consistent with your stated convention.

Q2. FP&A variance and cash case [L3]

A paint company's FY26 results versus budget (₹ crore): revenue 4,096 vs 4,100; EBITDA 566 vs 660; PAT 330 vs 400; CFO 290 vs 450. Product data: decorative BQ 9.0 lakh KL at ₹35,000/KL (BCM ₹13,000), actual 9.6 lakh KL at ₹34,500 (actual CM ₹12,000); industrial BQ 2.0 lakh KL at ₹47,500 (BCM ₹12,000), actual 1.6 lakh KL at ₹49,000 (actual CM ₹11,500). Working capital days: DSO 32 → 41 (budget 32), DIO 70 → 78, DPO 60 → 55. Opex was ₹20 crore over budget. (a) Compute revenue price, mix and volume variances and reconcile. (b) Build the EBITDA bridge. (c) Explain the CFO shortfall in days and rupees. (d) Deliver the headline, three drivers and recommendation in under two minutes.

Q3. Business-partnering role play [L2]

The sales head of a Pune-based industrial pumps company proposes (i) a 6% price cut on the mid-range series (contribution margin 35%, monthly volume 4,000 units at ₹1.2 lakh) and (ii) 15 additional sales engineers at ₹14 lakh fully loaded CTC each. The CFO asks you to evaluate both. Compute the break-even volume increase for the price cut, the break-even revenue per engineer, propose alternatives, and structure your recommendation using a 30-60-90 day monitoring plan and three KPIs.

Q4. Unit economics and KPI case for a GCC [L2]

A Bengaluru GCC supports a US D2C brand: AOV USD 48, gross margin 55%, shipping and packaging USD 9 per order, payment fees 3% of AOV, discounts 8% of AOV, CAC USD 70, 2.4 orders per customer per year, annual churn 45%. Compute contribution per order, annual contribution per customer, a simple undiscounted LTV and LTV:CAC; build a driver tree for contribution per order; and recommend two levers with quantified impact.
