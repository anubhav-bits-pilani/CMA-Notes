Roadmap topic: Capstone Case Execution (Timed 2-Day Simulation): Download raw financial statements of an unfamiliar NSE-listed company and build a clean 3-statement + DCF model from scratch

Overview

This topic is the integration test for the whole curriculum. You take a company you have never studied, download its raw public filings and, within two working days, produce a balanced, checked, driver-based three-statement model with a DCF valuation and a short written commentary. Each component has been practised separately: Ind AS presentation and restatement (Phases 1–2), ratio and forensic analysis (Weeks 6–7), comps (Week 8), Excel technique (Weeks 9–10), three-statement integration (Weeks 13–15), WACC and DCF (Week 16) and MIS commentary (Phase 5). The capstone tests whether you can combine them under time pressure, which is how equity research, valuation, corporate development and FP&A modelling tests are set in practice.

For the target roles the capstone has two uses. It is a rehearsal for the take-home or in-office case tests that most Indian equity research houses, Big 4 deal advisory teams and corporate FP&A teams set. It is also the flagship portfolio piece (Week 23 t3), and its numbers feed the executive deck (t4). Interviewers will open the file. What they look for (a clean structure, visible checks, defensible drivers, a sensible WACC and a terminal value that does not dominate) is exactly what this topic covers.

The subtopics form a sequence. s01 selects a company that can be modelled in two days with the standard industrial template, excluding financials. s02 sources the data from primary filings, with aggregators for cross-checks. s03 restates five years of history so that drivers are calibrated on recurring economics. s04 fixes the 17-tab architecture, and s05 chooses revenue and cost drivers. s06 builds the DCF with Indian WACC inputs, two terminal value methods and mid-year discounting. s07 wraps the whole sequence in a 16-hour time plan with checkpoints. The six key concepts are the formulas and controls that must be right in every capstone: FCFF/FCFE (c01), terminal value (c02), the equity bridge (c03), CAPM/WACC with the 115BAA rate (c04), the balance and cash checks (c05) and interest circularity (c06).

Related concepts to study

Weeks 13–15: three-statement integration and schedules

→ Week 13–15: 3-statement integration and schedule design

The capstone reuses the Weeks 13–15 architecture: schedules (PPE, WC, debt, tax, equity) feeding statements, cash as the only BS line sourced from the CFS, and roll-forward checks on every schedule. Revisit the indirect-method CFS logic in particular: each BS line's movement must appear once in the CFS with the correct sign, which is the mechanism behind the balance check (c05). The schedule designs you built then, with opening balance, additions, deductions and closing balance on fixed rows, should be in your template before Day 1. The capstone adds two schedules that the earlier weeks may have treated lightly: a full Ind AS 116 Leases tab and a Tax tab that handles the 115BAA switch. If either is missing from your template, build it in advance rather than during the timed window.

Week 16 WACC/DCF and Week 8 peer comps

→ Week 16: WACC and DCF; → Week 8: peer comps for a cross-check valuation

Week 16 provides the theory behind s06 and c01–c04: CAPM assumptions, beta estimation and relevering, the FCFF/FCFE distinction and terminal value logic. Week 8 provides the cross-check: a peer set of 5–8 NSE companies with EV/EBITDA, P/E and EV/Sales on a consistent lease basis and fiscal year. In the capstone the comps serve three purposes. They supply the exit multiple for TV (c02), they supply bottom-up betas for WACC (c04), and they give an independent valuation range for the football field in the t4 deck. If your DCF-implied EV/EBITDA is far outside the peer range, either your drivers or your WACC need a second look, or you need a clear reason (higher growth, better returns) why the company deserves a premium.

Weeks 6–7 ratio and forensic checks on assumptions

→ Week 6–7: ratio analysis and forensic checks to validate assumptions against history

Every forecast driver should be benchmarked against its own five-year history and against peers. Ratio analysis from Weeks 6–7 (margins, asset turnover, receivable/inventory/payable days, ROCE, cash conversion) gives the reference ranges, and the forensic checks (CFO/EBITDA conversion, accrual ratios, Beneish-style indicators, related party transactions, auditor remarks) tell you whether history itself is reliable. Practical use in the capstone: place a historical ratio block next to each forecast driver and flag any forecast value outside the historical min–max range. If CFO has persistently lagged EBITDA, a forecast that assumes 90% conversion needs a stated reason, such as a receivables clean-up.

Industry KPIs for the chosen company

Industry KPIs relevant to the chosen company (e.g. EBITDA/tonne, ARPU, same-store sales growth)

Industry KPIs translate the model into the language analysts and management use, and they are the strongest sanity checks. Cement and steel are judged on EBITDA/tonne and capacity utilisation. Telecoms and subscription businesses are judged on ARPU and churn. Retail and QSR are judged on same-store sales growth (SSSG), revenue per square foot and store additions. Hospitals use ARPOB and occupancy, and hotels use RevPAR. Compute the KPI for history (from investor decks) and forecast (from your drivers) in adjacent rows. A forecast EBITDA/tonne of ₹1,400 when the company's five-year range is ₹750–1,100 and peers are at ₹900–1,200 is an assumption you must defend explicitly. KPIs also give the deck its action titles (t4).

Scenario manager with CHOOSE or INDEX switches

Scenario manager design (base/bull/bear) with CHOOSE or INDEX switches

A scenario manager keeps all scenario-dependent drivers in stacked blocks on the Inputs sheet (base, bull, bear) and selects the live set through one switch cell. =CHOOSE($D$4,E12,E13,E14) is simple for three scenarios. =INDEX(E12:E14,$D$4) scales better and is easier to audit, because the whole block is referenced once. The live row is then the only one referenced by calculations. Design rules: one switch for the whole model, scenario names displayed next to the switch, every driver that differs across scenarios placed in the block (not only revenue growth), and a scenario output table, built with a data table or by manual paste, showing value per share under each case. Test every scenario end to end, and reset to base before sending (t2 pitfall).

Common pitfalls

Too much time on data entry

Spending too long on data entry, leaving no time for checks and commentary

The mistake: spending 5–6 hours typing every line of five years of statements and notes, then rushing the DCF and skipping commentary. Why it happens: data entry feels productive and safe, and Indian annual reports are long, with lakh/crore unit changes. Correct treatment: cap historical entry at about 1.75 hours (s07). Enter only the lines the model uses, map minor items to "other", use XBRL or Power Query for PDF tables, and log time per block. Detection in review: compare the time sheet with the plan. In the model, look for a detailed Historicals tab next to a thin DCF with no sensitivity, or a Cover without a check status.

Excessive terminal growth or terminal value share

Terminal growth above long-run nominal GDP or terminal value above ~80% of EV without justification

The mistake: a Gordon g of 7–9%, or a TV that is 85–90% of EV. Why it happens: India's high current nominal growth tempts analysts to extrapolate it forever, and short explicit periods combined with back-ended growth push value into TV. Correct treatment: keep g at or below a defensible long-run nominal GDP figure (4–6% for most practitioners), make the final explicit year steady state, extend the explicit period for growth companies and report TV % of EV and implied multiples (c02). Detection: a review row computing TV % of EV with a flag above 75–80%, and implied g from the exit multiple.

Double-counting leases

Double-counting leases: subtracting lease liabilities and also deducting rent from FCFF

The mistake: EBITDA or FCFF is reduced by rent (pre-116 or EBITDAaL basis) while the equity bridge also deducts lease liabilities. The lease is then charged twice, once as a cash flow and once as debt. Why it happens: mixing company-reported EBITDA (post-116) with a broker's EBITDAaL, or "conservatively" deducting everything. Correct treatment: choose one basis (s03, c03). Post-116: no rent in FCFF, lease liabilities in net debt, lease capex deducted, post-116 multiples. Pre-116: rent in FCFF, no lease liabilities, pre-116 multiples. Detection: a basis label on the Cover, plus a check that the bridge's lease line is zero when the basis flag is "pre-116".

Plugs to force the balance sheet to balance

Hard-coding plugs to force the balance sheet to balance

The mistake: typing the imbalance into "other current liabilities", or making cash a balancing figure (cash = total liabilities + equity − other assets), so the check always reads zero. Why it happens: time pressure at Checkpoint 2 and fear of submitting an unbalanced model. Correct treatment: diagnose systematically (first non-zero period, constant versus growing, sign test, BS-versus-CFS movement block, c05). A model submitted with a disclosed, located imbalance is more credible than a plugged one. Detection: Go To Special → Constants on forecast columns of the BS (t2 s04), a cash line that does not link to the CFS, or a balance check that stays at zero when you deliberately break an input (flex test, t2 c04).

Practice Questions

Q1. End-to-end mini capstone [L3]

Kaveri Building Products Ltd (NSE mid-cap, tiles and sanitaryware) reports FY26 consolidated figures (₹ crore): revenue 2,416 (tiles 1,690 at 78 million m² sold; sanitaryware 726), EBITDA 338 including a ₹21 crore insurance claim, D&A 92, other income 46 (of which treasury income 39), finance cost 41 (of which lease interest 9), PBT 251, borrowings 420, lease liabilities 98, cash and liquid investments 365, NCI nil, 15.8 crore shares, CMP ₹520. Tiles capacity is 96 million m², adding 12 million m² from 1 October of FY28 at ₹260 crore capex spread over FY27–FY28. (a) Normalise FY26 EBITDA. (b) Specify revenue and cost drivers for FY27–FY31 with justified values. (c) Using Rf 6.6%, bottom-up β 0.95 at target D/E 0.20, ERP 7.0%, Kd 9.0%, t 25.17%, compute WACC. (d) Describe how you would compute TV both ways with a peer EV/EBITDA of 16× and g of 5%, and the checks you would apply to the result. (e) List the bridge items from EV to value per share.

💡 Hint: Fix the lease basis before you touch EBITDA, WACC weights or the bridge.

Q2. Reviewer's red-flag memo [L2]

A peer's capstone shows: TV 88% of EV; Gordon g 7.5%; FCFF deducting ₹64 crore of rent while the bridge deducts ₹310 crore lease liabilities; "Other current liabilities" forecast as a hard-coded ₹17.3 crore in FY28–FY36; time sheet showing 6.5 hours on historicals and 40 minutes on commentary. Write a structured review memo identifying each issue, its valuation or credibility impact, and the fix.

Q3. Scenario and KPI design [L3]

For a cement mid-cap, design a base/bull/bear scenario block (at least six drivers: volume growth, utilisation cap, realisation growth, power and fuel cost per tonne, capex per tonne of new capacity, commissioning delay) and a KPI panel (EBITDA/tonne, utilisation, net debt/EBITDA, ROCE) that sits on the Cover. Specify the switch formulas and how a reviewer can confirm all three scenarios flow end to end.

Q4. Time plan under disruption [L2]

You lose three hours on Day 1 because the company changed its segment reporting in FY25. Using the default plan, show a revised schedule for the remaining 13 hours and state which three simplifications you would make and which three items you would never cut.

Q5. Defending the valuation in interview [L3]

Your DCF gives ₹612 per share against a CMP of ₹520. Trading comps give ₹470–560. Prepare answers to: (a) "What does your DCF imply for EV/EBITDA, and why is it above peers?"; (b) "What happens to value if WACC is 100 bps higher?"; (c) "Why did you use a bottom-up beta?"; (d) "Is circularity on in your model, and how would I turn it off?"
