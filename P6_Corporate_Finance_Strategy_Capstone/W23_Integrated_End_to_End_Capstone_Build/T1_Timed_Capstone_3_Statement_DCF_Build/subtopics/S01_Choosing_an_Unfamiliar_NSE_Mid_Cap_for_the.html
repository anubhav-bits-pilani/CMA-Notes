Subtopic note

Choosing an Unfamiliar NSE Mid-Cap for the Capstone Build

Scope: Company selection: unfamiliar NSE-listed mid-cap with clean segment data, avoiding banks/NBFCs/insurers (different model structure)

Why it matters

The capstone is meant to simulate the first two days of an equity research, corporate development or FP&A analyst assignment: someone hands you a company you have never looked at and expects a balanced, defensible model by the end of the second day. The company you choose decides how much of that time goes into genuine analysis and how much into wrestling with messy disclosures. Pick badly and you spend Day 1 untangling three demergers and a changed segment definition. Pick well and the data is clean enough to finish the schedules, leaving Day 2 for the DCF, checks and commentary. The choice also matters for the portfolio. A recruiter or interviewer will ask why you picked the company. "It had clean segment data, a capital-intensive model with visible drivers, and no acquisitions in five years" shows judgement. "I knew it already" defeats the point of the exercise.

Core notes

"Unfamiliar" — why it is a deliberate constraint

If you choose a company you have tracked, analysed at work or audited, you will be tempted to fill gaps from memory, and you will not test the skill being assessed: reading an annual report cold, building drivers from disclosures and defending assumptions with evidence. Choosing a company you have audited or advised also creates a confidentiality risk, because what you "remember" may be non-public information (see Week 23 t3 on confidentiality). The rule is that you should have no prior working knowledge of the company's numbers. Knowing its sector is fine and actually helps.

"NSE-listed" — why listing matters

A listed company gives you a complete, dated, public data set:

  • audited annual reports with Ind AS financial statements in Schedule III Division II format, notes, segment information (Ind AS 108) and the Board's report;
  • quarterly results under SEBI LODR Reg 33 for testing recent run-rates;
  • investor presentations and earnings call transcripts for volume, capacity and pricing KPIs;
  • a market price, so a DCF can be compared with the current market price (CMP) and with trading multiples.

NSE listing also gives you a beta to regress against the Nifty 50 (or Nifty 500) and a peer set for comps (Week 8).

"Mid-cap" — the sweet spot

SEBI's categorisation (circular of October 2017, refreshed half-yearly by AMFI) ranks companies by full market capitalisation: ranks 1–100 are large-cap, 101–250 mid-cap and 251 onwards small-cap. Mid-caps suit the capstone because:

Feature Large-cap Mid-cap Small-cap
Business complexity Many segments, acquisitions, overseas subsidiaries Usually 1–3 segments Often single product
Disclosure quality Very high Generally adequate, investor decks common Patchy, few decks or calls
Analyst coverage Heavy, so consensus anchors you Moderate Thin or none
Model size in 2 days Hard to finish Feasible Feasible but data gaps

Since the AMFI list changes every six months, record the date and rank of the list you used on the Cover sheet.

"Clean segment data" — what clean means

Revenue builds need segment-level detail. Under Ind AS 108, segments follow what the chief operating decision maker (CODM) reviews, and the standard requires segment revenue, segment results and (if reported to the CODM) segment assets and liabilities. Check for:

  1. Stable segment definitions across five years. A re-segmentation means restating history using the comparatives the company recasts, and these are often given only for one prior year.
  2. Volume and price disclosures, either in the notes or, more often, in the investor presentation: tonnes sold, capacity, utilisation, realisation per unit, store count.
  3. Few corporate actions. Avoid companies with a major acquisition, demerger or scheme of arrangement in the window. Business combinations under common control (Ind AS 103 Appendix C) restate comparatives as though the entities had always been combined, and that complicates any trend analysis.
  4. Standalone ≈ consolidated, or at least subsidiaries that are small. Always model the consolidated statements (that is what the market values), but a large gap between standalone and consolidated means you have to understand each subsidiary.
  5. Clean audit opinion. A qualified opinion, an emphasis of matter on going concern, or a CARO report full of adverse remarks points to data you cannot rely on.

Why banks, NBFCs and insurers are excluded

The standard industrial model (operating cash flows funded by debt and equity, FCFF discounted at WACC) breaks down for financials:

  • Debt is raw material, not financing. For a bank, deposits and borrowings fund the loan book that produces revenue. Separating "operating" from "financing" cash flows, and so computing FCFF or net debt, is meaningless.
  • Regulatory capital drives growth. Growth is constrained by capital adequacy (CRAR, Tier 1) rather than by capex. The correct models are FCFE, dividend discount, residual income or P/B versus ROE.
  • Different formats. Banks still prepare financial statements under RBI formats (Third Schedule to the Banking Regulation Act) and Indian GAAP, as Ind AS implementation for banks has been deferred. NBFCs that apply Ind AS use Schedule III Division III, and insurers follow IRDAI formats. None of these fits the Division II template you built in Weeks 13–15.
  • Different line items. The business is driven by net interest margin, credit cost, ECL staging under Ind AS 109, AUM growth and the solvency ratio, none of which appear in a manufacturing model.

For the same reasons, avoid holding companies, investment companies, and real estate developers whose revenue follows Ind AS 115 completion patterns. These are not forbidden, but they need special treatment.

A practical screening workflow

  1. Open a screener (Screener.in, Tijori, Trendlyne) and filter on market cap ₹5,000–₹40,000 crore, five years of listing, and sector not in Banks/NBFC/Insurance/Finance.
  2. Filter for capital-intensive or volume-driven sectors with clear KPIs: cement, building materials, chemicals, auto ancillaries, consumer durables, QSR/retail, hospitals, logistics.
  3. Remove companies that you or your employer have worked with.
  4. Shortlist three. For each, spend 15 minutes on the latest annual report: segment note, auditor's report, notes on exceptional items and business combinations, and whether the investor deck gives volumes.
  5. Score and pick:
Criterion (weight) Company A Company B Company C
Segment stability 5 yrs (25%) 5 3 4
KPI disclosure — volume/capacity (25%) 4 5 2
No major M&A / restructuring (20%) 5 2 4
Leases/exceptional items manageable (15%) 4 4 3
Peer set available on NSE (15%) 4 5 3
Weighted score 4.5 3.85 3.2

Record the scorecard on the Cover or a "Selection" note in your dossier. It is evidence of structured judgement.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Conglomerate mid-caps. A company with one dominant segment and a small "others" segment is fine. Model "others" as a simple growth line. A company with three equally sized, unrelated segments doubles the work.
  • Recent IPOs. These have fewer than five years of audited history, though the DRHP restated financials can fill the gap. Note that DRHP numbers are "restated" under SEBI ICDR, and their classifications may differ from later annual reports.
  • Holding-company discounts. If listed subsidiaries make up a large part of value, a sum-of-the-parts valuation is needed. Avoid these unless you budget for it.
  • Cyclicals at a peak. Commodity chemicals or steel at peak margins make the base year misleading. They are still acceptable if you normalise margins (s03).
  • Fiscal year differences. Some MNC subsidiaries listed in India once used calendar years. Since the Companies Act 2013, Sec 2(41) requires a financial year ending 31 March unless the Tribunal approves otherwise, so check the transition years.
  • Demerger in year 4 of 5. Usually a reason to reject, unless the company published restated comparatives.

Key takeaways

  • Choose a company you have no prior working knowledge of, both to test the skill and to protect confidentiality.
  • Mid-caps (AMFI ranks 101–250) balance disclosure quality with a model size you can finish in two days.
  • Clean data means stable Ind AS 108 segments, disclosed volume/price KPIs, few corporate actions and a clean audit report.
  • Exclude banks, NBFCs and insurers: debt is operating, regulatory capital drives growth and formats differ (RBI, Division III, IRDAI).
  • Use a weighted scorecard on a shortlist of three and keep it as portfolio evidence.

Practice Questions

Q1. Mid-cap boundary [L1]

Explain how SEBI/AMFI determine whether a company is mid-cap, how often the list changes, and what you would record on your model's Cover sheet so a reviewer can verify the classification at your valuation date.

Q2. Scoring a shortlist [L2]

You shortlist three companies. Alpha Chemicals: segments unchanged for five years, volumes disclosed quarterly, one ₹180 crore acquisition in FY24, lease liabilities 2% of total liabilities. Beta Retail: 410 stores, same-store sales growth disclosed, lease liabilities 38% of total liabilities, re-segmented in FY25. Gamma Auto Components: three segments, capacity and utilisation in the investor deck, no M&A, emphasis of matter on a customs dispute. Build a weighted scorecard using the criteria and weights in these notes, assign and justify scores of 1–5, and recommend one company.

💡 Hint: State a reason for every score; the recommendation must follow from the weighted total, not from preference.

Q3. Why not an NBFC [L2]

A peer argues that an NSE-listed gold-loan NBFC is a good capstone choice because its segment data is clean. Write a 200-word reply explaining which parts of the Week 13–16 model template fail for an NBFC, and which valuation approach and statement format would apply instead.

Q4. Confidentiality conflict [L3]

Two years ago, as an internal auditor, you reviewed the inventory controls of a listed tiles manufacturer that now scores highest on your shortlist. Discuss whether you can select it, referring to the "unfamiliar" requirement, your professional duty of confidentiality and how a portfolio reviewer would perceive the choice.

Q5. Standalone versus consolidated gap [L2]

A candidate company reports FY26 standalone revenue of ₹3,150 crore and consolidated revenue of ₹4,020 crore, with a 51% owned subsidiary contributing most of the difference. List the extra schedules and valuation adjustments your model would need, and say whether this changes your selection decision.
