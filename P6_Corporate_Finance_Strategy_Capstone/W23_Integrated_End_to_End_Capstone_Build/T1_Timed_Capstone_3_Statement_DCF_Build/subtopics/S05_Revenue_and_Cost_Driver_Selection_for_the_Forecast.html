Subtopic note

Revenue and Cost Driver Selection for the Forecast

Scope: Driver selection: volume × realisation, capacity × utilisation, or segment growth; cost as % of revenue vs per-unit drivers

Why it matters

The DCF is only as credible as the revenue and margin forecast behind it, and the forecast is only as credible as its drivers. "Revenue grows 12%" is an assertion. "Volumes grow 7% as utilisation rises from 72% to 80% on existing capacity, and realisation grows 4.5% with blended price hikes" is a hypothesis that can be tested against history, guidance and peers. Driver choice also determines what the sensitivity tables and the FP&A commentary can say. A model driven by utilisation can answer "what if the new line is six months late?". A model driven by top-down growth cannot. In FP&A roles, the same driver tree becomes the budget model and the variance analysis framework (Weeks 18–20).

Core notes

Choosing a revenue driver: the decision rule

Use the most granular driver that (a) the company actually discloses for at least three years, (b) management itself talks about, and (c) you can forecast with evidence. Then pick from three families.

1. Volume × realisation

\[ \text{Revenue}_t = \text{Volume}_t \times \text{Realisation}_t \]

where volume is physical units sold (tonnes, units, litres) and realisation is net revenue per unit (after discounts and before GST). Best for commodity-like or unit-sold businesses: cement, steel, paper, two-wheelers, tiles, FMCG by tonnage. Forecast volume from industry demand × market share or from capacity (family 2), and realisation from price trends, mix and input-cost pass-through.

Calibration: realisation = reported revenue ÷ disclosed volume. If the implied realisation jumps erratically, the volume or revenue definition is inconsistent (e.g. traded goods included in revenue but not in volume).

2. Capacity × utilisation

\[ \text{Volume}_t = \text{Effective capacity}_t \times \text{Utilisation}_t, \qquad \text{Effective capacity}_t = \text{Opening capacity} + \text{New capacity} \times \frac{\text{Months operational}}{12} \]

Use this where supply is the binding constraint and capacity additions are lumpy: cement (MTPA), chemicals (KTPA), hospitals (beds × occupancy × ARPOB), hotels (rooms × occupancy × ADR). It links naturally to the PPE/capex schedule: new capacity requires growth capex, usually with a lag through CWIP. Utilisation ramps up after commissioning (e.g. 50% → 70% → 85% over three years), and you should cap it at a realistic level (about 85–90% for cement) rather than 100%.

Worked illustration: opening capacity of 12.0 MTPA, with a 3.0 MTPA line commissioned on 1 October of FY28:

FY27 FY28 FY29
Nameplate capacity (MTPA, year-end) 12.0 15.0 15.0
Effective capacity (MTPA) 12.0 \(12.0 + 3.0 \times 6/12 = 13.5\) 15.0
Utilisation 78% 74% 77%
Volume (MT mn) 9.36 9.99 11.55
Realisation (₹/tonne) 5,350 5,500 5,650
Revenue (₹ crore) 5,008 5,494 6,526

Utilisation dips in FY28 because new capacity ramps up slowly, which is realistic and should be explained in commentary.

3. Segment growth

\[ \text{Revenue}_t = \sum_{s} \text{Revenue}_{s,t-1} \times \left(1 + g_{s,t}\right) \]

Use this where no reliable volume data exist (diversified engineering, IT services, specialty chemicals with many SKUs), or for minor segments. Anchor \(g_{s,t}\) to evidence: segment history, industry growth, order book cover (order book ÷ trailing revenue), deal wins. Fade growth towards nominal GDP by the end of the explicit period.

Retail and consumer variants include stores × revenue per store (with same-store sales growth plus new stores × ramp-up) and subscribers × ARPU.

Choosing cost drivers

Cost line Typical driver Why
Raw materials Per unit (₹/tonne × volume) or % revenue if pass-through pricing Per-unit when input prices are independent of selling price; % revenue when contracts pass through costs
Power and fuel Per unit (kWh/tonne × ₹/kWh) Physical consumption × tariff; links to fuel price scenario
Freight Per unit × lead distance Volume-linked; diesel sensitivity
Employee costs Headcount × cost per head, or prior year × (1 + wage inflation) + step for new plant Semi-fixed; not proportional to revenue
Other expenses Split into variable (% revenue) and fixed (inflation-indexed) Avoids overstating operating leverage
Selling and advertising % revenue Discretionary, managed to a ratio

Cost as % of revenue is fast and keeps margins stable. That is its weakness: it assumes all costs vary with revenue, ignoring operating leverage, and it lets price increases flow through to costs automatically. Per-unit drivers separate price from volume. When realisation rises 5% but RM per tonne rises 8%, the margin squeeze shows up, and that is exactly the insight an FP&A reader wants. Use per-unit drivers for the two or three largest cost lines (usually RM, power and freight together make up 50–70% of costs in manufacturing), and % of revenue or fixed-plus-inflation for the rest.

Excel implementation

  • Put drivers on Inputs by scenario, and select with =CHOOSE(Scenario, base_row, bull_row, bear_row) or =INDEX(driver_block, Scenario, ) (topic related item on scenario manager design).
  • Compute the historical implied driver on the Historicals tab (e.g. realisation, RM/tonne) and show it next to the forecast driver so a reviewer sees continuity.
  • Add a sanity row under each driver: implied EBITDA/tonne or margin, with conditional formatting when it moves outside the five-year range.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Disclosure gaps. If volumes are disclosed only in some years, back-solve them from deck realisation or use segment growth with a utilisation cross-check, and footnote the method.
  • Mix effects. Blended realisation changes when product or geography mix shifts. If the mix is moving (premiumisation, exports), model sub-segments separately rather than burying mix in a price assumption.
  • Commodity pass-through lags. Pass-through contracts often reset quarterly, so margins compress in rising input cost years and expand when costs fall. An annual % of revenue driver hides this. Note it in risks.
  • Utilisation above 100%. Debottlenecking can lift effective capacity above nameplate. Model it as a capacity increase, not as utilisation above 100%.
  • Driver overload. Twenty drivers for a 2% revenue segment waste the timebox. Granularity should be proportional to value contribution.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the most granular driver that is disclosed, used by management and forecastable with evidence.
  • Volume × realisation suits unit-sold businesses. Capacity × utilisation suits capacity-constrained ones and links revenue to capex.
  • Segment growth is the fallback. Anchor it to evidence and fade it towards nominal GDP.
  • Use per-unit drivers for the largest cost lines to separate price and volume effects. Use % of revenue for discretionary costs.
  • Show historical implied drivers next to forecasts and add sanity rows (EBITDA/unit, margin) to catch implausible combinations.

Practice Questions

Q1. Pick the driver [L1]

For each company type, choose and justify a revenue driver and the two most important cost drivers: (a) a multi-specialty hospital chain; (b) a speciality chemicals maker with 300 SKUs; (c) a two-wheeler OEM; (d) a QSR chain with 450 stores.

Q2. Capacity build [L2]

A cement company has 18.0 MTPA capacity at the start of FY27. A 4.0 MTPA plant will be commissioned on 1 January of FY28 and a 2.5 MTPA grinding unit on 1 July of FY29. Utilisation of existing capacity is 76% in FY27 rising 1 percentage point a year; new capacity runs at 45%, 65% and 80% in its first three full or part years. Realisation is ₹5,400/tonne in FY27, growing 3% a year. Build the FY27–FY30 revenue schedule.

💡 Hint: Compute effective capacity separately for each block before you apply its own utilisation.

Q3. Percentage versus per-unit costs [L2]

FY26: volume 2.40 lakh tonnes, realisation ₹68,000/tonne, RM cost ₹41,500/tonne. For FY27 you expect volume +6%, realisation +4% and RM cost per tonne +7%. Compute FY27 gross margin under (a) RM at the FY26 % of revenue and (b) per-unit RM, and explain which an FP&A reader should see and why.

Q4. Excel scenario selector [L2]

Write CHOOSE-based and INDEX-based formulas that pick the FY27–FY31 volume growth rate from a three-row block (base, bull, bear) on Inputs, given a scenario cell containing 1, 2 or 3. Explain which version is easier to audit and extend to a fourth scenario.

Q5. Defending a driver in interview [L3]

An interviewer says: "Your model uses volume × realisation, but the company doesn't disclose volumes for its exports business, which is 30% of revenue. Defend your approach." Prepare a structured two-minute response including your fallback method and how you tested it.
