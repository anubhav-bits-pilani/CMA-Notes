Subtopic note

Standardising Five Years of Historicals Before Forecasting

Scope: Historical restatement: standardise 5 years, reclassify exceptional items, other income, Ind AS 116 lease effects, discontinued operations

Why it matters

Forecast drivers are calibrated on history: revenue growth, EBITDA margin, capex intensity, working capital days. If the history mixes recurring and one-off items, or switches lease treatment partway through, every driver inherits the distortion and the DCF compounds it into perpetuity. A ₹40 crore insurance claim credited to other expenses in FY24 can inflate the "normal" margin by 150 bps. An analyst who forecasts on that margin overvalues the company by several hundred crore. Restating history is also where your Ind AS and audit background gives you an edge over generalist modellers: you can read the notes and see what is recurring.

Core notes

Step 1 — standardise five years

"Standardise" means one layout, one unit, one sign convention and one basis across all five years:

  • Same basis: consolidated statements throughout, all under Ind AS (any company you pick will have been on Ind AS for the whole window; the Phase I–II transition ran FY17–FY18).
  • Same line items: map each year's Schedule III line items to your model's standard chart of lines. Build a mapping table (reported line → model line) rather than retyping. Schedule III Division II line items are stable, but companies add lines under Ind AS 1.85, for example separate "Share of profit of associates".
  • Same unit: ₹ crore, one decimal place.
  • Same sign convention: expenses positive in the IS with subtraction in the formulas, or expenses negative throughout. Choose one and state it on the Cover (Week 23 t2 pitfall on signs).
  • Latest comparatives: take each year from the next year's report so reclassified comparatives flow through.

Lay out the Historicals tab as reported → adjustments → normalised, with every adjustment on its own row and a source reference. Never overwrite reported numbers.

Step 2 — reclassify exceptional items

Schedule III Division II shows "Exceptional items" as a separate line between profit before exceptional items and PBT. Ind AS itself does not define "exceptional". Ind AS 1.97–98 requires separate disclosure of material items of income and expense (write-downs of inventory or PPE, restructuring, disposals, litigation settlements). Companies use the exceptional line inconsistently, so:

  1. Read the exceptional items note for each year.
  2. Decide whether each item is genuinely non-recurring. A "restructuring cost" that appears in four of five years is recurring and belongs in EBITDA.
  3. Look for one-offs outside the exceptional line: insurance claims in other income, inventory write-downs in COGS, VRS costs in employee benefits. Pull them into an "Adjustments" row.
  4. Compute adjusted EBITDA and adjusted PAT, with the tax effect of each adjustment at the marginal rate (25.17% under Sec 115BAA unless the item is a capital gain taxed differently).

EBITDA (before exceptional) 362.0 −28.0 (insurance claim credited in other expenses) 334.0

Step 3 — separate other income

Other income in Indian companies routinely includes interest on deposits and treasury investments, dividends, fair value gains on mutual funds (Ind AS 109 FVTPL), profit on sale of assets, forex gains, export incentives, government grants and scrap sales. For the model:

  • Treasury income (interest, dividends, MF gains) is non-operating. It goes below EBITDA, and the corresponding investments are added to equity value as non-operating assets (c03). Never include it in operating margins.
  • Operating-type items (scrap sales, export incentives such as RoDTEP, and some grants) arguably belong in operating income. Classify them consistently with how peers are measured.
  • One-off gains (profit on sale of land) belong in adjustments.

Double-count check: if MF investments are added to equity value, MF gains must not also be in FCFF.

Step 4 — Ind AS 116 lease effects

Since FY20, Ind AS 116 has brought most leases onto the balance sheet: an ROU asset and a lease liability, with depreciation and interest replacing rent. Consequences for history:

  • EBITDA jumps in FY20: rent leaves operating costs. FY19 and earlier are not comparable unless you adjust, though with a five-year window ending FY26 every year is post-Ind AS 116.
  • CFS reclassification: principal repayment of lease liabilities goes in financing activities. Interest goes in financing or operating depending on the company's Ind AS 7 policy, so EBITDA-to-CFO conversion looks better than under Ind AS 17.
  • Short-term and low-value leases (recognition exemptions) are still expensed as rent.

Choose a model basis and apply it end to end:

Basis EBITDA FCFF Net debt Multiple
Post-Ind AS 116 (reported) Excludes rent Before lease payments (leases treated as debt); new ROU additions deducted as "lease capex" Include lease liabilities Post-116 EV/EBITDA
Pre-Ind AS 116 (rent-adjusted, "EBITDAaL") After deducting lease payments Rent already deducted through EBITDA Exclude lease liabilities Pre-116 multiple

In the post-116 basis you must also forecast new ROU additions (as a form of capex) or accept that FCFF is overstated for a growing lessee such as a retailer. Never mix the two bases. That is the topic's lease double-counting pitfall.

Step 5 — discontinued operations

Under Ind AS 105, a discontinued operation (a separate major line of business or geographical area that has been disposed of or is held for sale) is presented as a single post-tax amount in the IS, and comparatives are re-presented. For the model:

  • Use the re-presented comparatives from the latest report so history excludes the discontinued unit.
  • Exclude discontinued profit from forecasts. Sale proceeds received after the valuation date are a non-operating asset, and assets held for sale (net of associated liabilities) are added to equity value at expected net proceeds.
  • If only the CFS is not re-presented (Ind AS 105 permits disclosure of discontinued cash flows in the notes), adjust using the note.

Normalisation beyond the four named items

Within the time budget, also consider forex gains or losses on borrowings and unusual working capital (a year-end advance from a customer). Document these but keep the adjustments limited: each one must be supported by a note, not by a view that the number "looks high".

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Recurring "exceptional" items. If a company books exceptional charges most years, treat their average as a normal cost. Investors increasingly do the same.
  • Government grants and PLI incentives. PLI income tied to incremental sales is operating but time-limited. Model it in a separate row that ends with the scheme.
  • Lease-heavy business models. For retail, QSR and hospitals, the lease basis drives the valuation outcome. State the basis on the Cover and use the same basis for comps (Week 8).
  • Discontinued operation that is a segment you liked. You cannot keep it in the forecast. Value it separately only if it is retained.
  • Tax on adjustments. Exceptional items may carry a different tax effect (capital gains, disallowed impairment). Use the tax note when material instead of applying 25.17% mechanically.

Key takeaways

  • Lay historicals out as reported, then adjustments, then normalised. Never overwrite reported figures.
  • Judge "exceptional" by recurrence and nature, not by the company's label. Search COGS, employee costs and other income for hidden one-offs.
  • Treasury other income goes below EBITDA, and its asset is added to equity value. It is never both in FCFF and in the bridge.
  • Pick one lease basis (post-116 with lease liabilities in net debt, or EBITDAaL without them) and apply it across EBITDA, FCFF, net debt and multiples.
  • Use Ind AS 105 re-presented comparatives and exclude discontinued operations from forecasts.

Practice Questions

Q1. Normalising EBITDA [L2]

FY26 data (₹ crore): reported EBITDA before exceptional items 512.0; other expenses include a ₹22.0 crore one-time CSR spend relating to a prior-year shortfall transferred under Sec 135(6); employee costs include ₹15.5 crore of VRS compensation; other income (below EBITDA) of 61.0 includes ₹38.0 crore interest on fixed deposits and ₹9.0 crore profit on sale of land; exceptional items are −₹31.0 crore (impairment of goodwill). Prepare a reported → adjustments → normalised table for EBITDA and PBT and state the tax effect of each adjustment at 25.17%, flagging any adjustment whose tax treatment differs.

💡 Hint: Decide first which items are operating, then which are recurring; goodwill impairment is not deductible.

Q2. Lease basis choice [L2]

A QSR chain reports FY26 EBITDA of ₹420 crore (post-Ind AS 116), lease liabilities of ₹1,150 crore, depreciation on ROU assets of ₹160 crore and interest on lease liabilities of ₹95 crore. Total cash lease payments were ₹238 crore. Compute pre-116 EBITDA (EBITDAaL) and explain how net debt, FCFF and the comparable EV/EBITDA multiple must change under each basis.

Q3. Recurring exceptional items [L3]

Over five years a company reports exceptional "restructuring costs" of ₹12, ₹18, ₹9, ₹21 and ₹15 crore. Management describes each as non-recurring. Write a short memo arguing whether to normalise them out of EBITDA, and propose the forecast treatment.

Q4. Discontinued operation mid-window [L2]

In FY25 a company classified its packaging division as held for sale. The division had FY24 revenue of ₹310 crore and EBITDA of ₹44 crore. The FY25 report re-presents FY24, but your FY22–FY23 data come from older reports. Explain how you would make the five-year history consistent and how the expected ₹260 crore sale proceeds (receivable in FY27) enter the valuation.

Q5. Mapping table in Excel [L3]

Design an Excel mapping table and the formulas (for example SUMIFS) that convert each year's reported Schedule III line items into your model's standard lines, including a check that total mapped values equal the reported totals for each year.
