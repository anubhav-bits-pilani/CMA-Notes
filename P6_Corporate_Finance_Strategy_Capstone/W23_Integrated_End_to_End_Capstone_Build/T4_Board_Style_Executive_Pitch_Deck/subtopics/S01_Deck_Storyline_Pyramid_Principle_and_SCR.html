Subtopic note

Deck Storyline: Pyramid Principle and SCR

Scope: Storyline: Pyramid Principle (answer first) and Situation–Complication–Resolution

Why it matters

Senior audiences — CFOs, boards, investment committees — have limited time and read decks top-down. If the conclusion is on slide 11, many never reach it, and those who do have spent ten slides wondering where you are going. A storyline built on the Pyramid Principle puts the answer first and organises everything else as support; Situation–Complication–Resolution (SCR) frames why the answer matters now. Together they turn a valuation model into an argument. This is the format of strategy consulting decks, investment bank pitch books, equity research initiations and well-run Indian board packs, and it is what interviewers mean when they ask you to "tell the story" of your model.

Core notes

The Pyramid Principle (answer first)

Developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey, the Pyramid Principle structures communication as a pyramid:

  • Apex — the governing thought: the single answer to the audience's question. For a capstone: "ABC Chemicals is worth ₹1,950–₹2,150 per share, 6–17% above CMP; we recommend a positive view, conditional on the new plant reaching 80% utilisation by FY29."
  • Key line — 3 (sometimes 2–4) supporting arguments: each a complete statement that, if true, supports the apex. E.g. (1) capacity expansion drives 13% revenue CAGR; (2) margins recover to 19% as raw-material costs normalise; (3) DCF and comps converge on the range.
  • Supporting data: the evidence beneath each argument — charts, schedules, sensitivities.

Rules:

  • Answer first: the top of the pyramid appears first — in the executive summary and the deck's opening.
  • Vertical logic (question–answer dialogue): each statement raises a question ("why?" or "how?") that the level below answers.
  • Horizontal logic: arguments at the same level are either deductive (a chain: premise, premise, therefore) or inductive (independent reasons of the same kind). Boards prefer inductive groupings — each argument stands alone, so one failing does not collapse the whole.
  • MECE: supporting points should be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive — no overlap, no material gap.
  • Summarising: each group's summary statement must be a conclusion, not a topic label ("Three drivers" is a label; "Growth is capacity-led, not price-led" is a conclusion).

Situation–Complication–Resolution

SCR is the introduction that creates the audience's need for the answer:

  • Situation — what the audience already knows and accepts (stable context). "ABC Chemicals is a ₹4,100 crore specialty chemicals mid-cap with 60% export revenue."
  • Complication — what has changed or what creates tension, raising a question. "Margins fell from 22% to 15% in FY24–FY25 on Chinese pricing pressure while a ₹650 crore capex programme is under way; the stock has de-rated 30% from its peak."
  • Question (implicit) — "Is the stock now undervalued, and what should management do?"
  • Resolution — the answer, i.e. the apex of the pyramid.

SCR order can be varied: Resolution–Situation–Complication (answer first, then context) suits time-pressed boards; the classic S–C–R order suits a narrative presentation. In a board deck, the executive summary typically leads with the resolution and the next slides give situation and complication.

Building the storyline — steps

  1. Write the audience's question in one sentence.
  2. Write the answer in one or two sentences (the apex). If you cannot, the analysis is not finished.
  3. List every finding from the model; group them into three MECE arguments; discard or appendix the rest.
  4. Write the SCR introduction.
  5. Draft one action title per slide (s03); read titles alone in sequence — they should form the story.
  6. Only then build charts.

This "ghost deck" approach — titles and placeholder boxes first — prevents the common failure of building 30 charts and then searching for a story.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • The answer is "hold" or "inconclusive". Still answer first: "Fairly valued at ₹1,800–₹1,950 versus ₹1,842 CMP; upside depends on plant ramp-up, which we cannot yet verify." A clear neutral view is still an answer.
  • Hostile or sceptical audience. If the recommendation is controversial (e.g. recommending the board defer a pet capex project), consider leading with the shared situation and complication to build agreement before the resolution — but keep the executive summary explicit.
  • Deductive chains in valuation. Valuations are partly deductive (drivers → cash flows → value). Present the drivers as independent supporting arguments where possible; a deck that hinges on a single chain is fragile.
  • Too many arguments. More than four supporting points usually means they are not grouped properly.
  • Interview setting. In an interview presentation, the panel may interrupt after the executive summary; answer-first ensures they have heard the conclusion.

Key takeaways

  • Pyramid Principle: answer first, three supporting arguments, data beneath; vertical question–answer logic and MECE horizontal groupings.
  • SCR creates the need for the answer: situation (accepted context), complication (change/tension), resolution (answer).
  • Write the question and the answer before building any chart.
  • Map pyramid levels to slides and build a ghost deck of action titles first.
  • Neutral or conditional views still go first and are stated explicitly.

Practice Questions

Q1. Write the pyramid [L2]

For an NSE-listed quick-service restaurant chain with FY25 revenue of ₹2,350 crore (same-store sales growth 3% versus 9% two years earlier), store count 520 growing to 800 by FY30, EBITDA margin (pre-Ind AS 116) of 12%, and a DCF value of ₹610–₹690 per share versus CMP ₹575, write the apex statement, three MECE supporting arguments and two data points under each argument.

Q2. SCR introduction [L1]

Write a Situation, Complication and Resolution (one or two sentences each) for a capstone on a cement company whose EBITDA per tonne fell from ₹1,150 to ₹820 over FY23–FY25 while it is mid-way through expanding capacity from 18 to 24 MTPA, and whose DCF value is 14% above CMP.

Q3. Diagnose a broken storyline [L2]

A deck's titles in sequence are: "Agenda", "Company overview", "Industry overview", "Historical financials", "Assumptions", "Projections", "WACC", "DCF", "Sensitivity", "Comparable companies", "Conclusion". Explain why this violates the Pyramid Principle, and rewrite the sequence as action titles for a company of your choice with illustrative numbers.

💡 Hint: Where does the reader first learn the answer in the current sequence?

Q4. MECE test [L2]

Assess whether these three supporting arguments are MECE and rewrite them if not: (1) "Revenue grows on new capacity"; (2) "Volume growth from the new plant and export markets"; (3) "Valuation is attractive". Explain the overlap and gap you find.
