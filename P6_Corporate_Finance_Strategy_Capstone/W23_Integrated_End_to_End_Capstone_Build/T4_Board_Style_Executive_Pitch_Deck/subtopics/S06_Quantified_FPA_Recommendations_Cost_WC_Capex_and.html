Subtopic note

Quantified FP&A Recommendations: Cost, WC, Capex and Pricing

Scope: FP&A recommendations: cost levers, WC release, capex phasing, pricing, with quantified impact

Why it matters

A valuation answers "what is it worth?"; FP&A recommendations answer "what should management do to make it worth more?". This is what distinguishes an FP&A or corporate finance deck from a pure equity research note, and what CFOs look for in candidates: the ability to translate a model into operational levers with quantified impact on EBITDA, cash flow and value. A recommendation without a number is an opinion; a recommendation with EBITDA, cash, ROCE and value-per-share impact, an owner and a KPI is a decision proposal.

Core notes

The quantification standard

Every recommendation should state:

Element Example
Lever Reduce receivable days from 78 to 65
Mechanism Tighten credit terms for distributor channel; early-payment discount of 1% for payment within 15 days
EBITDA impact −₹4 crore p.a. (discount cost)
Cash impact +₹146 crore one-time release
Value impact +₹14 per share (PV of cash release net of discount cost)
Owner / timeline Head of Sales and Treasury; 2 quarters
KPI DSO monthly; % of receivables > 60 days

Value impact is computed by running the change through the model (scenario or overlay) rather than by back-of-envelope, so that tax, WC and terminal effects are captured. The model should have a recommendations switch or overlay inputs so each lever can be toggled independently and in combination.

Cost levers

Identify costs where the company is out of line with history or peers, using the ratio benchmark (Week 6–8) and the cost build:

  • Raw material: procurement savings, alternative sourcing, formula optimisation, hedging; quantify as per-unit saving × volume.
  • Power and fuel: captive/renewable power (common in cement, chemicals), waste heat recovery; quantify as ₹/unit saving × volume less incremental capex.
  • Employee cost: productivity (revenue per employee), automation, span-of-control; often sensitive — frame as cost growth below revenue growth rather than cuts.
  • Freight and logistics: lead distance reduction, rail versus road mix.
  • Other expenses: discretionary spend (advertising as % of revenue against peers), overheads.

Example: raw material at 58% of revenue versus peer median of 55%; closing half the gap on FY27E revenue of ₹5,000 crore saves 1.5% × 5,000 = ₹75 crore of EBITDA p.a.; after 25.17% tax, ₹56 crore of post-tax cash flow p.a.

Working-capital release

Compare cash conversion cycle (CCC) components with history and peers:

\[ \text{CCC} = \text{DSO} + \text{DIO} - \text{DPO} \]

The one-time cash release from reducing a component is the change in days times the daily base (revenue for receivables, COGS for inventory and payables) — see c03. Recurring benefit: interest saved on released cash at the cost of debt, and higher FCFF in the year of release. Mechanisms: receivables (credit policy, collections, channel financing, TReDS for MSME counterparties), inventory (SKU rationalisation, safety-stock policy, S&OP), payables (term renegotiation — note the MSMED Act 45-day limit for micro and small suppliers and the Sec 43B(h) Income-tax Act disallowance of payments delayed beyond it, so stretching MSME payables is not a legitimate lever).

Capex phasing

Re-timing capex alters FCFF, funding needs and value:

  • Defer non-critical maintenance or growth capex where utilisation is low.
  • Phase a large project into modules aligned with demand (e.g. two 1 MTPA lines a year apart instead of one 2 MTPA line).
  • Quantify: peak net debt and leverage (net debt/EBITDA) under each phasing; NPV impact from delaying both capex outflows and the resulting EBITDA; covenant headroom.

Example: phasing ₹2,400 crore capex 30/40/30 over three years instead of 50/50 over two reduces peak net debt by ₹480 crore and keeps net debt/EBITDA below 2.0×, at a cost of ₹45 crore of NPV from delayed volume. The board's decision is a trade-off between value and balance-sheet risk — state both.

Pricing

Price is usually the most powerful lever because it flows almost fully to EBITDA, but is constrained by competition and elasticity:

\[ \Delta \text{EBITDA} \approx \Delta P \times Q_1 - (Q_0 - Q_1) \times \text{Contribution per unit} \]

where \( Q_0 \) is volume before and \( Q_1 \) volume after the price change. Quantify a 1% price increase with an assumed elasticity, show the breakeven volume loss, and support with evidence (peer pricing, mix, premiumisation). Example: revenue ₹5,000 crore, contribution margin 35%. A 2% price rise adds ₹100 crore if volume is unchanged; the breakeven volume loss is roughly \( 2\% \div (35\% + 2\%) \approx 5.4\% \).

Prioritising and presenting

Rank recommendations on a 2 × 2 of impact (value per share) versus ease/risk, show total impact (avoiding double-counting overlaps, e.g. pricing and mix), and present in one slide with a summary table plus a bridge from base value to value-with-actions. State that the base-case valuation excludes the recommendations unless explicitly included.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Overlap and double-counting. Raw-material savings and pricing actions may interact (pass-through clauses); WC release and supplier price may trade off (longer payment terms for higher prices). Run combined scenarios.
  • One-time versus recurring. WC release is mainly a one-time cash inflow; cost savings are recurring. Do not capitalise a one-time release at a multiple.
  • Implementation cost and timing. Include one-time costs (restructuring, systems) and ramp-up periods.
  • External constraints. Price controls (e.g. under the Drugs Price Control Order for pharmaceuticals), regulated tariffs, MSME payment rules and labour laws limit some levers.
  • Credibility for an outsider. In a capstone on public data, you do not know internal costs; frame recommendations as "opportunities identified from peer benchmarks", stating assumptions.

Key takeaways

  • Each recommendation states lever, mechanism, EBITDA, cash and value impact, owner, timeline and KPI.
  • Run recommendations through the model via switches or overlays to capture tax, WC and terminal effects.
  • Cost levers come from peer and history benchmarks; WC release from CCC component gaps; capex phasing trades value against peak leverage; pricing is powerful but needs elasticity evidence.
  • Separate one-time from recurring benefits and avoid double-counting interacting levers.
  • Respect legal constraints such as MSME payment timelines and Sec 43B(h).

Practice Questions

Q1. Quantify a cost lever [L2]

A chemicals company has FY27E revenue of ₹4,800 crore, power and fuel at 9.5% of revenue versus a peer median of 7.8%. A 25 MW solar captive project costing ₹130 crore would reduce power cost by ₹1.90 per kWh on 4.2 crore kWh per year. Compute the annual EBITDA saving, post-tax cash saving at 25.17%, simple payback, and state what model inputs you would change to compute value-per-share impact.

Q2. WC release recommendation table [L2]

FY25 revenue ₹3,600 crore; COGS ₹2,340 crore; DSO 72; DIO 64 (on COGS); DPO 48 (on COGS). Peer medians: DSO 58, DIO 55, DPO 52. 30% of payables are to MSME suppliers already paid within 45 days. Build the recommendation table (lever, mechanism, cash impact, owner, KPI) for each component, adjusting the payables lever for the MSME constraint.

💡 Hint: Apply the DPO lever only to the non-MSME share of the payables base.

Q3. Capex phasing trade-off [L3]

A ₹1,800 crore expansion is planned 60/40 over FY26–FY27, adding ₹420 crore of EBITDA from FY28. An alternative phases it 35/35/30 over FY26–FY28 with EBITDA of ₹250 crore in FY28 and ₹420 crore from FY29. Opening net debt is ₹900 crore and FY26–FY28 pre-capex FCFF is ₹550 crore per year. Describe how you would quantify, in the model, peak net debt, net debt/EBITDA and NPV under both plans, and draft the slide's action title and three bullets presenting the trade-off.

Q4. Pricing lever with elasticity [L2]

A consumer company with revenue of ₹2,200 crore and a contribution margin of 42% is considering a 3% price increase. Management estimates a price elasticity of −0.8. Estimate the change in volume, revenue and contribution, compute the breakeven volume loss, and state two pieces of evidence you would cite to support the recommendation.
