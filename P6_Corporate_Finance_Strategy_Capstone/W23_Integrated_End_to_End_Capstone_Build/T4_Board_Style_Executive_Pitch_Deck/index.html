Roadmap topic: Executive Presentation Deck (10–12 Slides): Translating your 3-statement model, DCF valuation, and FP&A recommendations into a board-style pitch deck

Overview

A model is an engine; a deck is the vehicle that takes its conclusions to decision-makers. Boards, investment committees and CFOs do not read 17-tab workbooks — they read 10–12 slides, often in a pre-read before the meeting, and decide in minutes whether the analysis is credible and what to do about it. The board-style pitch deck converts the capstone's 3-statement model, DCF valuation and FP&A analysis into a recommendation-led story: what we think the company is worth, why, how confident we are, and which operational levers management should pull. For candidates moving into FP&A, equity research, valuation or corporate development roles in India, the deck is the most visible proof that you can communicate as well as compute.

Board-style means a specific discipline. Each slide carries one message stated in an action title; the executive summary gives the answer up front; numbers are consistent with the model to the last decimal shown; units are ₹ crore unless stated; every number has a source; and detail lives in the appendix. This mirrors the format of Indian board packs and investor presentations filed with stock exchanges under SEBI LODR, and of investment bank and Big 4 valuation reports. A deck that dumps model tabs into slides, uses descriptive titles, or ends without a recommendation fails regardless of the quality of the underlying model.

The learning sequence: first build the storyline (s01) using the Pyramid Principle and Situation–Complication–Resolution, and draft the executive summary (c05) before any slide. Lay out the slide flow (s02) and write action titles (s03) for every slide — reading the titles alone should tell the story. Then design the charts (s04): revenue/EBITDA bridges, margin trends, EV bridge waterfall, football field (c01) and sensitivity heat maps. Build the valuation section with triangulation (s05), the implied multiples check (c02) and upside/downside (c04). Quantify FP&A recommendations (s06), including the WC release opportunity (c03). Finally apply formatting discipline (s07): consistent units, sources, footnotes, 3–5 bullets per slide and an appendix.

The deck becomes the flagship item in the Week 23 t3 portfolio and the basis for valuation case interviews in Week 24. Build it so that you can present it in 10 minutes and defend every number for 30.

Related concepts to study

Week 16 DCF/comps outputs and Week 20 board-pack style

→ Week 16: DCF and comps outputs; → Week 20: MIS/board-pack commentary style

Week 16 produced the valuation building blocks the deck presents: FCFF projections, the WACC build (risk-free rate from the 10-year G-sec, beta, ERP, post-tax cost of debt at 25.17%), terminal value by Gordon growth and exit multiple, and trading comps with EV/EBITDA and P/E medians. The deck's valuation slides are a curated view of those outputs — not new analysis. Week 20's board-pack commentary style supplies the writing discipline: variance explanations that state cause, impact and action; numbers in ₹ crore with comparatives; and an explicit "ask" of the board. Combining the two gives a deck that is analytically rigorous and written in the register that CFOs and audit committees expect.

Week 24 t4: deck as basis for valuation case interviews

→ Week 24 t4: deck content is the basis for valuation case interviews

Valuation and FP&A interviews frequently include a case: "Walk me through your valuation", "What would change your recommendation?", "Why is your terminal growth 5%?", "How does a 1% change in WACC affect value?". Your deck is the prepared material for this. Every slide should anticipate two follow-up questions, with the answers either in the appendix or in your head. The sensitivity heat map answers "what if"; the implied multiples check answers "is this reasonable"; the football field answers "how does this compare to the market". Rehearsing the deck under time pressure in Week 23 is the preparation for Week 24's mock cases.

Data-visualisation principles

Data-visualisation principles: preattentive attributes, avoiding chart clutter

Preattentive attributes — colour hue, intensity, size, position, length, orientation, enclosure — are processed by the eye in a fraction of a second before conscious attention. Good executive charts use one of them deliberately to point at the insight: grey for all bars except the one that matters in a strong accent colour; a single highlighted line among muted peers. Clutter — gridlines, 3D effects, legends that could be direct labels, redundant axis labels, excessive decimals, borders — consumes attention without adding information (Tufte's "data-ink ratio"). For a board deck: remove gridlines, label data directly, use one accent colour, round to the precision that matters (₹ crore with no decimals for large figures), and make the chart title the insight.

Paste-link and refresh discipline

Linking Excel charts to PowerPoint (paste-link) and refresh discipline

PowerPoint can embed Excel charts in three ways: paste as picture (static, safest for distribution), embed (a copy of the workbook inside the pptx — bloats size and can expose the whole model), or paste-link (the chart stays linked to the source workbook and updates via File > Info > Edit Links to Files, or Refresh Data). Paste-link keeps deck and model in sync during iteration, but links break when files are moved or renamed and may point to local paths. Discipline: during build, use paste-link from a dedicated "Deck_Outputs" sheet in the model; before final release, update all links, check every number against the model, then break links or convert to pictures and export to PDF. Record the model version on the deck's cover or footer.

Common pitfalls

Dumping model tabs into slides

Dumping model tabs into slides instead of insight-led charts

The mistake is pasting a screenshot of the income statement or DCF tab with 15 rows × 10 columns of numbers onto a slide. People do it because the model is their proudest work and selecting feels like losing detail. The audience cannot read it at presentation size and cannot see what matters. Correct treatment: decide the one message of the slide, choose the chart that shows it (bridge, trend line, bar), and show only the numbers that support it; move the full tables to the appendix. Detect it in review: any slide with more than about 30 numbers, or font size below 10 pt, is a dump.

Descriptive rather than insight titles

Titles that describe the chart ("Revenue 2020–2030") rather than the insight

A title such as "Revenue 2020–2030" or "DCF valuation" names the topic but leaves the reader to work out the point. People write them because they label the chart as they build it and never revisit. Correct treatment: an action title that states the conclusion in one sentence — "Capacity expansion drives revenue to ₹6,900 crore by FY31, a 12% CAGR". Detect it by reading only the titles in sequence (PowerPoint Outline view): if they do not form a coherent argument ending in the recommendation, rewrite.

Deck and model numbers diverge

Inconsistent numbers between deck and model after last-minute model changes

You change WACC or fix a lease error the night before; the model updates, but the executive summary text, football field and three charts still show old numbers. This happens because deck numbers are typed manually or charts are pasted as pictures early. Correct treatment: drive every deck number from a single "Deck_Outputs" sheet in the model; paste-link charts during build; freeze the model before final deck edits; run a tie-out checklist listing every number in the deck against its model cell. Detect it with a final read-through comparing the deck's value per share, EV, upside and key drivers to the model's output sheet, and by checking that the version number matches.

No recommendation, or one the valuation does not support

No clear recommendation or a recommendation unsupported by the valuation

Some decks end with "Valuation summary" and no view; others recommend "Buy" when the DCF shows 3% upside within a ±15% sensitivity range, or recommend a capex plan the model shows destroys value. People avoid commitment because they fear being wrong, or they anchor on a prior opinion. Correct treatment: state the recommendation first, tie it to the valuation range versus CMP (e.g. upside above 15% with the full range above CMP supports a positive view), and acknowledge the conditions under which it would change. Detect it by asking: does the executive summary recommendation follow logically from the football field and sensitivity slides?

Practice Questions

Q1. Build the deck skeleton for a capstone [L3]

Your capstone on an NSE-listed cement company (FY25 revenue ₹5,200 crore; EBITDA ₹910 crore; capacity 18 MTPA expanding to 24 MTPA by FY28; net debt ₹1,350 crore including lease liabilities of ₹120 crore; 11.0 crore diluted shares; CMP ₹1,460) produced a DCF base case EV of ₹19,800 crore (range ₹17,600–₹22,400 crore). Trading comps at peer median EV/EBITDA of 15–18× on FY26E EBITDA of ₹1,080 crore; precedent transactions at ₹950–₹1,150 crore of EV per MTPA applied to current capacity of 18 MTPA; 52-week range ₹1,210–₹1,620. (a) Compute the value per share range for each method. (b) Write the executive summary (recommendation, range, three drivers, two to three risks). (c) Write action titles for 12 slides in the s02 sequence. (d) Identify which slide each of the four topic pitfalls is most likely to appear on and how you would prevent it.

💡 Hint: Convert each EV range to equity value using the same net debt before dividing by diluted shares.

Q2. Reviewing a peer's deck [L2]

A peer's 14-slide deck for an IT services company has titles such as "Company overview", "Revenue", "EBITDA margin", "DCF", "WACC", "Sensitivity", "Comps"; the DCF slide is a screenshot of the model tab; units alternate between ₹ crore and ₹ million; the executive summary states "The company has strong fundamentals"; and the deck value per share of ₹1,612 differs from the model's ₹1,588. Write a review note with at least eight specific findings, each linked to a subtopic or pitfall of this topic, and the correction.

Q3. FP&A recommendation slide with WC and capex [L2]

The cement company in Q1 has FY25 revenue of ₹5,200 crore, COGS of ₹3,380 crore, receivable days 38, inventory days 52 (on COGS), payable days 61 (on COGS). Peer medians: receivable 30, inventory 44, payable 65. The ₹2,400 crore expansion capex is planned as 50/50 over FY26–FY27; management is considering phasing it 30/40/30 over FY26–FY28 with a 6-month delay in 2 MTPA of capacity. (a) Quantify the WC release opportunity by component. (b) Describe the model runs needed to quantify the value and funding impact of capex phasing. (c) Draft the slide's action title and three bullets with quantified impact.

Q4. Upside, implied multiples and recommendation [L3]

Your DCF gives intrinsic value per share of ₹1,705 versus CMP ₹1,460. DCF-implied EV/EBITDA on FY26E is 18.3× against a peer median of 16.0× (interquartile range 14.5–17.5×); implied P/E is 34× against a peer median of 28×. Terminal value is 79% of EV with terminal growth of 6.0%. (a) Compute upside. (b) Evaluate whether the recommendation should be positive, neutral or conditional, citing the implied multiples check and terminal value share. (c) Write the executive summary recommendation sentence and the two risks you would highlight.

💡 Hint: Consider what the implied multiple premium tells you about the DCF assumptions before relying on the upside figure.

Q5. Ten-minute presentation plan [L3]

Plan a 10-minute presentation of your 12-slide deck to a mock board, allocating time per slide, identifying which slides you would skip or merge if cut to 6 minutes, listing the three most likely board questions with the appendix slide that answers each, and specifying your last-minute model-to-deck tie-out procedure.
