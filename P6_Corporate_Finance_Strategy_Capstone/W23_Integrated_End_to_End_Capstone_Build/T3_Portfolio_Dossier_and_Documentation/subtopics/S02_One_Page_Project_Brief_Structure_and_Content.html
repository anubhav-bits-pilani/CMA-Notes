Subtopic note

One-Page Project Brief: Structure and Content

Scope: One-page project brief: problem, data sources, approach, tools, key outputs, insights, time taken

Why it matters

Most reviewers will read the brief and nothing else. A one-page brief is the equivalent of the executive summary in a board paper or the summary of audit findings in a management letter: it must stand on its own, state the result, and give enough detail on basis and method that the reader trusts the result. It also disciplines you — if you cannot state the problem and the insight of a project in one page, the project itself is probably unfocused. Briefs become CV bullets (Week 24), LinkedIn Featured descriptions and the opening of interview answers.

Core notes

Layout

One A4 page in PDF (portrait), 10–11 pt body text, with a header block (project number, title, date, version, your name) and seven sections in a fixed order. Fixed order across all six briefs lets a reader compare projects quickly. One small screenshot or chart is allowed if it carries the key output.

1. Problem

Two or three sentences framing a business question, not a learning exercise. "Is Company X fairly valued at ₹1,842 per share given its capacity expansion plan?" is a problem; "To practise DCF modelling" is not. State the decision-maker (investor, CFO, board) and the decision.

2. Data sources

A compact list with dates: "FY21–FY25 annual reports (audited, consolidated), Q1–Q2 FY27 results under SEBI LODR Reg 33, Q2 FY27 earnings call transcript, NSE price data as at 14-Nov-2026, RBI 10-year G-sec yield as at 14-Nov-2026." State whether data is public, synthetic, or calibrated synthetic. Name aggregators used only for cross-checks.

3. Approach

Four to six steps in plain language describing the method — e.g. restated five years of historicals for exceptional items and Ind AS 116; built revenue as capacity × utilisation × realisation; integrated three statements with debt and tax schedules; FCFF DCF with WACC from CAPM; triangulated with trading comps. Mention key judgements (e.g. "treated lease liabilities as debt because EBITDA is post-Ind AS 116").

4. Tools

List tools with what they were used for: "Excel (model, data tables), Power Query (import of 5-year XBRL extracts), Power BI (dashboard), PowerPoint (deck)". Tools are a supporting line, not the headline.

5. Key outputs

The tangible deliverables: "17-tab integrated model with checks sheet; 12-slide board deck; 4-page Power BI report; 5-minute video." Link to each.

6. Insights

Three bullets, each quantified, each a "so what". For example:

  • DCF base case of ₹2,060 per share (range ₹1,780–₹2,390) implies 12% upside to CMP; EV is 68% terminal value.
  • Valuation is most sensitive to utilisation of the new plant: each 5 pp change moves value by ₹140 per share.
  • Receivable days rose from 62 to 78 over FY22–FY25; normalising to 65 days releases ₹96 crore of cash.

7. Time taken

Hours by phase, e.g. "Data and historicals 6 h; schedules 5 h; statements and DCF 4 h; checks 1.5 h; deck 3 h; total 19.5 h". This is credible evidence of speed and planning, important for roles with modelling tests, and honest about scope.

Writing rules

  • Answer first: the first line under the title should give the headline result (Pyramid Principle, covered in Week 23 t4).
  • Use the same units as the model (₹ crore) and state them once.
  • Every figure in the brief must exist in the working file — keep a hidden or appendix "brief tie-out" table in the workbook listing each brief figure with its cell reference.
  • Avoid jargon stacks; one technical term per sentence is enough.
  • Keep a template (Word or Google Docs) and export each brief to PDF with the naming convention P4_Brief_DCF_v1.0.pdf.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Too much to fit. If a project needs more than a page, the brief is still one page; push detail into the README or an appendix PDF.
  • Negative or inconclusive results. A valuation showing the stock is overvalued, or a forecast with poor accuracy, is still a valid insight — state it plainly and explain the driver. Interviewers respect honest findings more than forced optimism.
  • Time taken that looks slow. If P1 took 30 hours, break it down and note what is reusable (templates, mapping tables). Do not understate time; you may be asked to repeat a task under test conditions.
  • Dated valuations. A brief quoting upside to CMP becomes stale. State the valuation date prominently and avoid implying current investment advice; add "for educational purposes; not investment advice" to valuation briefs.
  • SEBI context. Publishing a buy/sell recommendation on a listed security publicly can edge into research-analyst territory under the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations, 2014. Frame outputs as an analytical exercise with a disclaimer rather than a recommendation to the public.

Key takeaways

  • One page, seven fixed sections: problem, data sources, approach, tools, key outputs, insights, time taken.
  • Open with the answer; frame the problem as a business decision.
  • Date every data source and state public versus synthetic.
  • Insights must be quantified "so what" statements; tools are supporting detail.
  • Tie every brief figure to a cell in the working file.
  • Add a valuation date and an educational-purpose disclaimer to valuation briefs.

Practice Questions

Q1. Rewrite a weak brief [L2]

A brief reads: "Project: Power BI. Problem: learn DAX. Data: Kaggle and annual reports. Approach: built dashboard. Tools: Power BI, DAX, Power Query, Excel. Outputs: dashboard. Insights: dashboard shows sales trend." The underlying project analyses five years of segment revenue and EBIT for an NSE-listed consumer durables company (FY25 revenue ₹8,450 crore; cooling products segment 41% of revenue, EBIT margin falling from 11.2% to 7.9% over FY21–FY25). Rewrite all seven sections to the standard in these notes.

Q2. Time-taken breakdown [L1]

Your P5 MIS project took 22 hours in total: 4 hours designing the chart of accounts and phasing, 7 hours building the budget model, 5 hours on the rolling forecast, 3 hours on variance analysis, and 3 hours on commentary and formatting. Draft the time-taken section and add one sentence explaining what you would reuse to cut the time in a second iteration.

Q3. Brief tie-out table [L2]

Design the tie-out table you would add to the capstone workbook to support the brief. Specify columns, list at least six brief figures (value per share, range, upside %, terminal value share, sensitivity, WC release), and write the Excel formula you would use to flag any brief figure that differs from its model source by more than ₹0.5 crore or 0.1%.

💡 Hint: Compare an absolute and a relative tolerance and return a text flag.

Q4. Inconclusive result framing [L3]

Your P6 capital budgeting project for a ₹420 crore greenfield plant shows a base-case NPV of ₹8 crore at a 12.5% WACC, an IRR of 12.9%, and NPV turning negative if utilisation is 3 pp lower than base. Write the Problem and Insights sections so that they are honest, decision-useful, and do not oversell the project.

Q5. Regulatory framing of a valuation brief [L2]

Explain why a public portfolio brief stating "BUY ABC Chemicals, target ₹2,060" may be problematic in India, and redraft the headline and disclaimer so that the brief remains informative about your valuation work.
