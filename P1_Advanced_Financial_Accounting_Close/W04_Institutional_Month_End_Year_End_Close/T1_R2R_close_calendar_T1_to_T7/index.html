Roadmap topic: R2R Close Calendar Design: T+1 to T+7 close checklist sequencing sub-ledger cut-offs (AP, AR, Inventory, Payroll, Fixed Assets) before GL lock

Overview

Record-to-Report (R2R) is the end-to-end process that turns transactions into a locked trial balance, financial statements and a management reporting pack. The close calendar is its operating plan: which task happens on which working day after period-end (T0), who does it, what it depends on, and what exit criterion must be met before the next stage starts. A well-designed calendar sequences sub-ledger cut-offs — AR, AP, inventory, payroll and fixed assets — before the GL lock, so that the GL is complete when it is locked and every later change is a governed adjustment rather than a scramble of manual top-sides.

For the target roles this is foundational. A financial controller in an Indian listed company owns the calendar and must deliver statutory results within SEBI LODR Reg 33 timelines, with IFC evidence that will stand up to Sec 143(3)(i) testing and an audit trail that satisfies Rule 3(1) of the Companies (Accounts) Rules. An FP&A analyst depends on the calendar for when actuals become reliable; flash numbers, rolling forecasts and board packs are scheduled around it. In interviews for both roles, "walk me through your month-end close" is a standard question, and the strong answer is dependency-driven and quantified, not a list of activities.

The topic builds in a logical sequence. Start with the pre-close window (s01), which prevents most delays before they happen. Then learn the sub-ledger sequence (s02) and how it expands into a seven-day plan (s03), anchored by the critical-path concept (c01). Cut-off controls (s04) protect the boundary of the period. ERP period controls and the soft/hard-close distinction (s05, c05) make the calendar enforceable and proportionate. Governance — RACI, maker-checker, JE thresholds and IFC (s06, c03) — defines who may do what. The regulatory clock (s07, c04) and audit-trail rules (s08) fix the external constraints. KPIs (c02) close the loop by measuring speed and quality.

Related concepts to study

Revenue cut-off and subsequent events

Revenue cut-off → w1_t4; subsequent events → w1_t2

Week 1 Topic 4 covered Ind AS 115 performance obligations, control transfer and the revenue waterfall. In the close calendar, that waterfall is a T+1 input: billing, unbilled revenue and contract liabilities must be final before the GL lock, and the cut-off controls in s04 apply the Week 1 rules at the period boundary. Week 1 Topic 2 covered Ind AS 10 events after the reporting period. The calendar must include a subsequent-events review that runs until the date the board approves the financial statements, and the post-close adjustment governance in s05 classifies findings as adjusting (book, possibly reopening the period) or non-adjusting (disclose). For quarters, the review window runs to the Reg 33 board date.

Global fast-close practice and tooling

Global fast-close practice (~5 working days) and tooling: BlackLine, SAP S/4HANA Group Reporting, Oracle ARCS

Benchmarking studies of large multinationals commonly report median monthly closes of about five to six working days, with top performers faster. They achieve this through continuous accounting (daily reconciliations, rolling IC matching), automation and pre-close estimates rather than by skipping controls. BlackLine provides task management, account reconciliation, transaction matching and IC hubs; SAP S/4HANA Group Reporting performs consolidation on the universal journal with IC reconciliation and eliminations; Oracle Account Reconciliation Cloud Service (ARCS) manages balance sheet reconciliation certification and transaction matching. Indian groups increasingly use these tools; the Project 1 Excel workbook mirrors their logic so you understand what the tools automate.

SOX 404 vs India IFC

US SOX 404 vs India IFC; ICAI Guidance Note on Audit of Internal Financial Controls over Financial Reporting

SOX 404(a) requires management of US-listed issuers to assess internal control over financial reporting (ICFR); 404(b) requires auditor attestation for accelerated filers, performed under PCAOB AS 2201 as an integrated audit. India's regime splits differently: directors of listed companies state in the Sec 134(5)(e) responsibility statement that IFC are adequate and operating effectively, and auditors of most companies report under Sec 143(3)(i) on IFC with reference to financial statements, following the ICAI Guidance Note on Audit of Internal Financial Controls over Financial Reporting. The Indian scope covers more companies (including large unlisted ones) but has less prescriptive management documentation. Indian subsidiaries of US groups often run both, using the same risk-control matrices.

Excel/Smartsheet close tracker

Excel/Smartsheet close tracker with dependencies, owners, status and due-date conditional formatting

The close tracker is a structured table with task ID, description, T+n day, owner, reviewer, predecessors, planned date (WORKDAY from period-end with a holiday table), actual completion, status and evidence link. A "Blocked" column checks whether any predecessor is incomplete; conditional formatting colours tasks overdue (red), due today (amber) and complete (green). A summary pivot or dashboard shows completion by day and by owner, and the critical path. Smartsheet adds native Gantt views, dependencies and automated reminders. The tracker becomes the dashboard tab of the Project 1 workbook and is the evidence base for close KPIs (c02).

Common pitfalls

Locking the GL before inventory costing or FA capitalisation

Locking GL before inventory costing or FA capitalisation, forcing manual top-sides

The mistake: the GL (or the materials/assets periods) is locked on a fixed day to meet a speed target while variance settlement, overhead absorption or CWIP capitalisation has not run. People make it because speed is measured and visible, while the resulting manual entries are not. The correct treatment is to make costing and capitalisation explicit predecessors of the lock (s02), and to pull capitalisation evidence (completion certificates) into pre-close. Detect it in review by reconciling the inventory and FA sub-ledgers to the GL each month — recurring differences, or manual journals to inventory/asset accounts after the lock, are the signature of this pitfall.

Calendars without dependencies or owners

Calendars without dependencies or owners, hiding slippage

The mistake: a checklist of tasks with dates but no predecessors and no single accountable owner. It happens because checklists are inherited from audit-style "to-do" lists. Without dependencies, a delayed IC confirmation does not visibly move downstream tasks, so slippage surfaces only on T+6. Without owners, tasks fall between teams. The fix: a tracker with predecessors, a RACI (s06) with exactly one A per task, daily stand-ups and float computation (c01). Detect by checking that every task has an owner and predecessor field populated, and by comparing planned versus actual completion per gate.

Treating recurring auditor adjustments as close work

Treating recurring auditor adjustments as close work instead of root-causing them

The mistake: the same audit adjustments (unrecorded rebate accruals, FX revaluation of a missed account, deferred revenue errors) recur each quarter, and the team treats them as a standard close task. It happens because booking the entry is quicker than fixing the process. The correct approach is to log each post-close and audit adjustment with a root cause (template gap, cut-off failure, system configuration) and fix it upstream — adding the item to the accrual template, mapping the account to the FX run. Detect by trending post-close and top-side entries by root cause (c02); repeats are a control deficiency to be reported in the IFC assessment.

Ignoring Reg 33 and board dates

Ignoring Reg 33 and board dates when sequencing year-end tasks

The mistake: year-end tasks are scheduled on internal convenience (actuarial valuation in week 4, impairment test in week 5) without working backwards from the board date and the 60-day Reg 33 limit. It happens when the controller plans the close and the company secretary plans the board calendar separately. The correct approach is a joint calendar built backwards from the board meeting (s07): Reg 29 intimation, audit committee, final audit sign-off, draft FS and component audits. Detect by checking that every year-end workstream has a latest-finish date derived from the board date and that float exists for audit issues.

Practice Questions

Q1. Designing a T+7 calendar for a listed manufacturer [L3]

Sahyadri Components Ltd (NSE-listed, FY ending 31 March) has two plants, one Indian subsidiary and one subsidiary in Singapore. Current close is T+11. Facts: billing cut-off is not defined; GRNs are often posted on invoice date; inventory variance settlement takes one day after all GRNs; CWIP of ₹46 crore includes a line commissioned on 18 March; IC recharges to Singapore are disputed quarterly; the tax team starts only after consolidation; the board meeting for annual results is scheduled for 24 May. Required: (a) design a pre-close plan for T−3 to T0; (b) design a T+1 to T+7 day plan with gates; (c) identify the critical path and two dependencies you would break with estimates; (d) list cut-off controls for billing and GRNs; (e) prepare a backward calendar from the board date including Reg 29 intimation and trading window dates.

Q2. Governance and materiality case [L3]

Overall audit materiality is ₹7.5 crore, performance materiality ₹5.25 crore and clearly trivial ₹37.5 lakh. In the March close: (i) 23 manual JEs above ₹1 crore were posted, 5 by the financial controller without second approval; (ii) the GL was reopened four times after the lock; (iii) 2 JEs of ₹9.8 lakh each were posted by the same user on the same day to the same account, just under a ₹10 lakh approval limit; (iv) the billing software's edit log was disabled for 3 days during an upgrade. Required: evaluate each issue against s05, s06 and s08 requirements; propose JE approval thresholds aligned with materiality; state the IFC and Rule 11(g) reporting implications; and design a monthly manual JE review.

💡 Hint: Treat each issue separately as design or operating deficiency before aggregating.

Q3. Cut-off and soft/hard close integration [L2]

A company's May (soft close) and June (Q1 hard close) data: power accrual run-rate ₹38 lakh per month vs June meter-based actual for the quarter of ₹1.22 crore; export shipment CIF of USD 3,20,000 loaded on board on 29 June (freight SSP USD 18,000, voyage 18 days, 1 day elapsed); a stock count on 4 July shows 9,600 units with receipts of 1,800 units and issues of 2,100 units between 1 and 4 July; book stock at 30 June is 9,950 units. Rate on 29 June ₹85.40/USD. Required: compute the power true-up, the revenue to be recognised at 30 June, the rolled-back inventory quantity and the difference to investigate; then explain how each would be treated in a soft close versus a hard close.

Q4. Close KPI dashboard and root-cause review [L2]

Over four quarters, a group reports: days to close 9, 8, 7, 7; post-close entries 6, 9, 12, 14 (₹ crore 1.1, 2.0, 3.6, 4.2); recs on time 88%, 90%, 86%, 82%; top-side entries 5, 8, 11, 15; materiality ₹8 crore. Required: compute post-close entries as a percentage of materiality each quarter, interpret the trend, identify which pitfalls of this topic are likely present, and propose a revised KPI set and targets.
