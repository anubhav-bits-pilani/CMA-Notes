Subtopic note

IC Settlement, Netting, FX Differences and Dispute Escalation

Scope: Settlement and netting, FX differences on IC balances, dispute timelines and escalation

Why it matters

Matched IC balances that are never settled become a liquidity, tax and regulatory problem: cash is trapped in the wrong entity, FX exposure accumulates, cross-border receivables breach FEMA realisation timelines, and old IC balances become unreconcilable when staff change. FX differences are the second-largest source of IC breaks after cut-off, and in consolidation they behave counter-intuitively — the balances eliminate but the exchange differences do not. Disputes, if left open, are carried into consolidation as unexplained differences and erode confidence in the close. An IC protocol therefore needs settlement rules, a single FX rate policy and a hard dispute clock.

Core notes

1. Settlement

  • Settlement cycle. Group policy should fix a settlement frequency (e.g., monthly, by T+15 of the following month) for trade and recharge balances. Loans settle per agreement.
  • Gross settlement — each invoice paid individually — is simplest for audit trail and for GST/TDS matching, but generates many payments.
  • Bilateral netting — A and B offset mutual receivables/payables and settle the net. Requires a netting agreement and both sides' confirmation of the gross amounts first.
  • Multilateral netting — a group netting centre (often a treasury entity) computes each entity's net position against all others and settles one amount per entity per currency per cycle.

Indian constraints.

  • Accounting: netting in the books is only permitted where Ind AS 32.42 conditions are met (legally enforceable right to set off and intention to settle net or simultaneously). Without a netting agreement, the gross receivable and payable stay gross in the standalone balance sheet.
  • FEMA: cross-border netting is restricted. Set-off of export receivables against import payables with the same overseas counterparty is permitted only within the conditions of the RBI Master Direction on Export of Goods and Services (through the AD bank, with both transactions reported in EDPMS/IDPMS). Indian entities generally cannot participate in an offshore multilateral netting centre without specific approval. Treat any cross-border netting as a treasury/legal sign-off item.
  • GST/TDS: netting does not change the tax point — GST is due on each supply and TDS on each payment/credit, so the underlying gross documents must exist.

2. FX differences on IC balances

Under Ind AS 21, an IC receivable or payable denominated in foreign currency is a monetary item, retranslated at the closing rate in the standalone books of the entity for which it is foreign currency (Ind AS 21.23(a)), with exchange differences in profit or loss (Ind AS 21.28).

Key consequences:

  • Only one side has FX exposure if the balance is denominated in one party's functional currency. A USD-denominated balance between a US parent (functional USD) and Indian subsidiary (functional INR) creates FX in India only.
  • On consolidation the balances eliminate, but the exchange difference stays in consolidated P&L (Ind AS 21.45), because the group has genuinely been exposed to a currency movement. The exception is a monetary item forming part of the net investment in a foreign operation — settlement neither planned nor likely — where the difference goes to OCI (FCTR) in the consolidated FS (Ind AS 21.15, 21.32).
  • Same rate policy. Both entities must translate the IC balance using the same group rate source and timestamp (e.g., RBI/FBIL reference rate at month-end, or group treasury rate). Different rates produce a residual difference that is pure noise (see topic pitfall).
  • Matching in transaction currency removes FX from the matching problem; the remaining INR difference is then explained by rate differences.

Illustration. Indian subsidiary I owes US parent P USD 5,00,000 (invoice in USD). Booked when rate was ₹83.20. Month-end group rate ₹84.10.

Entity Transaction ccy Functional ccy balance Entry
P (USD functional) USD 5,00,000 receivable USD 5,00,000 None
I (INR functional) USD 5,00,000 payable ₹4,20,50,000 at 84.10 Dr FX loss ₹4,50,000 / Cr IC payable ₹4,50,000

If I had used its bank's TT selling rate of ₹84.35 instead of the group rate, consolidation (translating I's payable into USD at the group rate) would show a USD residual of roughly USD 1,486 — an unexplained break caused only by rate inconsistency.

3. Dispute timelines and escalation

A dispute is any IC item where both sides acknowledge the transaction but disagree on amount, existence or terms (price, quantity, GST, TDS, mark-up). A typical protocol:

Day Action Owner
T+2 Exception report circulated; receiving entity raises dispute in the IC tool with reason code Entity accountants
T+3 Bilateral call; agree adjust/credit note or confirm dispute Entity controllers
T+4 Unresolved items escalated to regional/group IC lead with documentation Group IC lead
T+5 Group controller decides; default rule applies (e.g., seller's invoice prevails, buyer posts and raises credit-note request) Group financial controller
Next month Ageing > 30 days reported to CFO; > 60 days in audit committee pack where material CFO

Principles:

  • Default rule to make consolidation deterministic: pending resolution, the provider's/lender's figure is booked by both sides and the dispute becomes a credit-note request, not an open break.
  • No parking: disputes stay in the IC accounts with the dispute tag; they are not moved to suspense (topic pitfall).
  • Root-cause codes (pricing master out of date, missing PO, GST rate disagreement, TDS deducted on gross vs net) feed a monthly IC quality KPI.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Long-outstanding cross-border IC receivables in an Indian entity can breach FEMA realisation periods (see s04); settlement priority should be driven by regulatory deadlines, not just amount.
  • Write-off of IC balances between Indian group companies has tax consequences (deductibility under Sec 36(1)(vii) depends on prior income recognition) and GST consequences (credit note requirements under Sec 34 CGST Act); never "write off" a dispute in one entity alone.
  • Netting and TDS: where B deducts TDS on a payment to A and then nets, A's receivable reduces by cash + TDS credit. Matching must track the TDS leg or A appears short-paid.
  • Hedged IC exposures: a forecast IC transaction may qualify as a hedged item in consolidated FS only if denominated in a currency other than the entity's functional currency and the FX risk affects consolidated profit or loss (Ind AS 109 6.3.6). Coordinate with treasury before designating.
  • Translation of the whole sub's TB (Ind AS 21.39) is separate from transactional revaluation of the IC balance in the sub's own books; do not confuse the two when explaining differences.

Key takeaways

  • Settle IC balances on a fixed cycle; net only under a documented agreement, and remember Ind AS 32.42 and FEMA constraints.
  • IC foreign-currency balances are monetary items; the exchange difference survives elimination in consolidated P&L unless the item is part of a net investment.
  • One group rate source and timestamp for both entities removes artificial FX breaks.
  • Disputes follow a dated escalation ladder with a default "who prevails" rule; they are never parked in suspense.
  • TDS and GST legs must be tracked separately through settlement and netting.

Practice Questions

Q1. FX entry on an IC loan [L1]

Indian subsidiary S (functional currency INR) borrowed EUR 20,00,000 from its German parent on 1 January at ₹90.40. At 31 March the group closing rate is ₹92.15. Interest at 6% p.a. is payable annually and accrued monthly. Pass the 31 March entries in S's books for revaluation and interest accrual, stating the rates you use for each.

💡 Hint: Accrued interest is itself a monetary item once recognised.

Q2. Consolidated effect of an IC exchange difference [L2]

Using Q1, explain what happens to the loan balance and to the exchange difference in the consolidated financial statements of the German parent (functional currency EUR). How would your answer change if the board minutes state that the loan will not be repaid in the foreseeable future?

Q3. Rate inconsistency break [L2]

Entity A (India) records a USD 3,20,000 receivable from entity B (Singapore, functional currency SGD) at the RBI reference rate of ₹84.05; B records the payable at SGD 4,32,000 using its own bank rate. The group consolidates in INR using a group rate of ₹84.05 per USD and ₹62.40 per SGD. Compute the difference that appears in the IC matching report and explain how a group rate policy would eliminate it.

Q4. Designing a dispute escalation policy [L3]

Draft an IC dispute policy for a 12-entity group (8 Indian, 4 overseas) closing on T+6. Include reason codes, escalation days, the default "who prevails" rule, treatment of disputes at quarter-end for Reg 33 results, and the KPI you would report to the CFO.

Q5. Netting request from treasury [L3]

Group treasury proposes that the Indian subsidiary settle its monthly USD import payables to the Dubai trading company net of its USD export receivables from the same company. List the FEMA, Ind AS 32, GST and TDS questions you would raise before agreeing, and the documentation you would require.
