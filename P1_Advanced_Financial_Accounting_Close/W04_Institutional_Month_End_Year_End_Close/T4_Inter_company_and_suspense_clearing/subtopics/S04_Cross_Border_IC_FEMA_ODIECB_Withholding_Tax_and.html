Subtopic note

Cross-Border IC: FEMA, ODI/ECB, Withholding Tax and GST

Scope: Cross-border IC: FEMA/RBI realisation and ODI/ECB rules, withholding tax and GST on cross-charges

Why it matters

For an Indian entity, an IC balance with a foreign group company is simultaneously an accounting balance, a FEMA-regulated receivable or payable, a TDS event and usually a GST event. A balance that looks harmless in the TB — say, a USD receivable from the US parent that has been "netted informally" for 14 months — can mean an overdue export in the AD bank's EDPMS, a missed Annual Performance Report, a reverse-charge GST liability never paid, or a Sec 195 default with interest and disallowance under Sec 40(a)(i). CARO 2020 and the tax audit both pick these up. The close team therefore needs a cross-border IC checklist that runs alongside the matching process.

Core notes

1. FEMA realisation and repatriation (current account IC)

Flow Rule (RBI Master Directions) Close-process control
Export of goods/services to an overseas group company Full export value to be realised and repatriated within the prescribed period from the date of export — nine months as the standard period (RBI has granted time-bound extensions at times; check the current Master Direction on Export of Goods and Services). Extension or write-off through the AD bank within limits. Age every foreign IC receivable against the realisation deadline, not just the invoice due date; track EDPMS shipping bill status / softex for software exports
Evidence of realisation e-BRC (bank realisation certificate) for goods; FIRC/FIRA for inward remittances Match each receipt to invoice and e-BRC (links to w4_t2 s01 FIRC/BRC matching)
Import of goods/services from group company Remittance generally within six months of shipment for goods (unless deferred payment arrangements apply); services remitted per agreement with Sec 195 compliance Track IDPMS bill of entry closure; payables aged against the remittance window
Set-off of export receivables against import payables Permitted only within AD-bank conditions of the Master Direction (same counterparty, reported transactions) No informal netting in books (see s02)

Because the consequence of non-realisation is a FEMA contravention (compounding under Sec 15 FEMA 1999), long-outstanding IC receivables must be escalated by regulatory deadline as well as by ageing bucket.

2. Capital account IC: ODI and ECB

Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) — governed by the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules 2022, Regulations 2022 and Directions.

  • Loans by the Indian parent to its overseas subsidiary, guarantees issued on its behalf and equity infusions are financial commitments; total financial commitment is capped (generally 400% of the Indian entity's net worth as per last audited balance sheet).
  • Loans must be backed by a documented agreement with an interest rate on arm's-length basis; interest and repayment flows are reported.
  • Annual Performance Report (APR) for each foreign entity, based on its audited accounts, is due by 31 December each year; delays attract late submission fees. Restructuring, write-off of loans and disinvestment have specific conditions and reporting.
  • Close control: reconcile the IC loan/investment balances in the TB to the ODI reporting (UIN-wise) every quarter.

External Commercial Borrowings (ECB) — loans from a foreign parent or group company to the Indian entity.

  • The foreign equity holder / group company is a recognised lender; the ECB framework prescribes eligible borrowers, minimum average maturity, all-in-cost ceilings and end-use restrictions (verify against the current RBI ECB Master Direction, which has been under revision).
  • Loan Registration Number (LRN) obtained via Form ECB before drawdown; monthly ECB 2 return filed through the AD bank within seven days of month-end.
  • Interest accrual, FX revaluation (monetary item under Ind AS 21) and TDS on interest are monthly close entries; the IC lender's figures must reconcile to the ECB 2 return.

3. Withholding tax on cross-border IC

  • Sec 195 (Income-tax Act 1961; renumbered under the 2025 Act): TDS on any sum chargeable to tax paid or credited to a non-resident — the earlier of credit in books or payment. A month-end IC accrual credited to the foreign parent's account is a credit that triggers TDS.
  • Rate: the lower of domestic rate (plus surcharge and cess) and DTAA rate, subject to Tax Residency Certificate, Form 10F and no-PE declaration. Fees for technical services/royalties typically 10–15% under treaties; "make available" clauses in some treaties (e.g., India–USA, India–UK) can take management services out of FTS.
  • Grossing up (Sec 195A) where the Indian entity bears the tax — the IC expense is then higher than the invoice.
  • Forms 15CA/15CB before remittance.
  • Sec 40(a)(i) disallows 100% of the expense if TDS is not deducted/paid on payments to non-residents (compare 30% for residents under Sec 40(a)(ia)).
  • Inbound: TDS deducted by a foreign group company on payments to the Indian entity creates a foreign tax credit claim (Rule 128, Form 67), not a 26AS credit — track it in a separate receivable/expense analysis.

4. GST on cross-border cross-charges

  • Import of services from a related person, even without consideration, is a supply (Schedule I para 4, CGST Act) when in the course of business; the Indian recipient pays IGST under reverse charge (Sec 5(3) IGST Act) and claims ITC if eligible — so the IC accrual should always be accompanied by an RCM liability entry.
  • Export of services by the Indian entity to its foreign group company qualifies as zero-rated only if all Sec 2(6) IGST Act conditions are met: supplier in India, recipient outside India, place of supply outside India, payment received in convertible foreign exchange (or INR where RBI permits), and supplier and recipient are not merely establishments of a distinct person (Explanation 1 to Sec 8). A subsidiary and its foreign parent are separate legal persons, so the last condition is usually met; a branch–HO charge is not.
  • Intermediary services have place of supply at the supplier's location (Sec 13(8)(b) IGST Act), so they are not exports — a common trap for Indian sales-support subsidiaries.
  • LUT must be in place for exports without payment of IGST; otherwise pay IGST and claim refund.

Illustration — monthly cross-border IC close entries

Indian subsidiary receives a March management fee invoice of USD 50,000 from its UK parent (treaty FTS rate 15%, parent bears tax, no grossing up). Rate ₹84.00.

Entry Dr ₹ Cr ₹
Management fee expense 42,00,000
IC payable – UK parent (USD 42,500) 35,70,000
TDS payable Sec 195 (15% of ₹42,00,000) 6,30,000
IGST input (RCM, if eligible) 18% 7,56,000
IGST payable – RCM 7,56,000

The UK parent's IC receivable is USD 50,000; the Indian payable is USD 42,500 plus USD 7,500 TDS. The IC matching tool must tag the USD 7,500 as a withholding leg so the pair still matches (c01).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Secondment of employees from a foreign parent: reimbursement of salary can be a supply of manpower services subject to RCM (the Supreme Court in Northern Operating Systems, 2022, treated the overseas entity as service provider) and can attract Sec 195 TDS depending on characterisation.
  • Free-of-cost services received from a foreign group entity — the open-market-value rules (Rule 28 CGST Rules) apply to RCM valuation; where the recipient is eligible for full ITC, the invoice value may be deemed the open market value.
  • Export receivable written off — requires AD bank/RBI conditions, GST refund implications (refunded IGST may need to be repaid if export proceeds are not realised) and tax deductibility analysis.
  • Interest on ECB — check the applicable TDS section (concessional rates under Sec 194LC apply only to borrowings within its specified time window).
  • CARO 2020: clause 3(vii)(a) statutory dues (including Sec 195 TDS and RCM GST) and clause 3(ix) loan defaults; outstanding IC loan defaults can surface here.

Key takeaways

  • Foreign IC receivables carry a FEMA realisation clock; age them against regulatory deadlines and match to e-BRC/FIRC.
  • IC loans to overseas subsidiaries are ODI financial commitments (APR by 31 December); loans from foreign parents are ECBs (LRN, monthly ECB 2).
  • A month-end credit of an IC charge to a non-resident triggers Sec 195 TDS; non-deduction disallows the whole expense.
  • Cross-border services from group entities attract RCM IGST even without consideration; exports to group entities need Sec 2(6) conditions and LUT.
  • Matching tools must recognise withholding and RCM legs so the IC identity still holds.

Practice Questions

Q1. Ageing against FEMA deadlines [L2]

An Indian software subsidiary has the following unpaid invoices to its US parent at 31 March: USD 1,20,000 dated 15 May (prior year), USD 2,40,000 dated 30 September, USD 3,10,000 dated 28 February. Assuming a nine-month realisation period, classify each by ageing bucket and by FEMA status, and state the escalation action for each.

Q2. Journal entries for an IC loan from a foreign parent [L2]

On 1 October an Indian company draws EUR 10,00,000 ECB from its Dutch parent at 7% p.a., interest payable half-yearly. Rates: 1 October ₹91.20; 31 March ₹93.00; average for the half-year ₹92.10. Treaty WHT rate on interest is 10% and the lender bears the tax. Prepare the 31 March entries (interest accrual, TDS, revaluation) and list the regulatory reports the close team must reconcile to.

💡 Hint: Decide which rate applies to the accrued interest and whether it is revalued at closing.

Q3. Export or intermediary? [L3]

An Indian subsidiary identifies Indian customers for its Singapore sister company, which contracts and invoices the customers directly; the Indian entity is paid cost + 10% in USD. Analyse whether the Indian entity's service is a zero-rated export of service, citing the relevant IGST Act provisions, and describe the impact on the IC recharge invoice and GST returns.

Q4. Cross-border IC checklist [L3]

Draft a month-end cross-border IC checklist (maximum 12 steps) for an Indian group with a UK parent, a Dubai sister entity and a Singapore subsidiary, covering FEMA, ODI/ECB reporting, Sec 195, RCM and documentation evidence.

Q5. Interview: Sec 40(a)(i) vs Sec 40(a)(ia) [L1]

Explain the difference in disallowance consequences between failing to deduct TDS on an IC service fee paid to a resident group company and to a non-resident group company, and when the disallowed amount can be claimed later.
