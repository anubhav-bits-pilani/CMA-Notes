Subtopic note

Tax Interplay: TDS on Year-End Provisions, Sec 40(a)(ia) and Sec 43B

Scope: Tax interplay: TDS on year-end provisions (Sec 40(a)(ia) 30% disallowance), Sec 43B payment basis

Why it matters

Every year-end accrual has a tax shadow. When a company books a provision for professional fees, contract services or rent at 31 March, the Income-tax Act may treat that book entry as a "credit" to the payee that triggers TDS — and if TDS is not deducted and deposited in time, 30% of the expense is disallowed. Separately, certain expenses (bonus, leave encashment, statutory dues, interest to banks, payments to MSMEs) are deductible only when actually paid. Both rules create differences between book profit and taxable income, affect current and deferred tax (Week 3), and appear in tax audit reporting (Form 3CD). The close team must flag TDS-relevant provisions at T+5 and track 43B items through to payment.

The references below are to the Income-tax Act, 1961, which governs FY 2025-26 (assessment year 2026-27). The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies from 1 April 2026 (tax year 2026-27) with renumbered provisions; the substantive rules are largely carried forward, but map each section before applying it to tax year 2026-27 onwards.

Core notes

TDS on year-end provisions

TDS provisions for most non-salary payments (e.g., Sec 194C contracts, 194J professional/technical fees, 194I rent, 194H commission, 194A interest) apply at the time of credit of the income to the account of the payee or payment, whichever is earlier. The Explanation to these sections provides that a credit to a "suspense account" or "any other account, by whatever name called" in the payer's books is deemed to be a credit to the payee's account.

Consequences for year-end provisions:

  • Where the payee is identifiable and the amount is ascertainable (e.g., March audit fees, March legal fees for a named firm, March security services), the provision is a credit triggering TDS. Deduct TDS at the applicable rate on the provision and deposit by 30 April (the due date for tax deducted in March under Rule 30).
  • Where the payee is not identifiable or the amount is a general estimate (e.g., a lump-sum provision for "miscellaneous expenses"), courts have held in several cases that TDS does not apply because there is no identifiable payee; but this is a fact-specific, litigated area.
  • Reversal on day 1 of the next year does not undo the TDS obligation if the credit had already occurred at year-end; many companies therefore deduct on provisions and, when the invoice arrives, adjust TDS against the invoice.
  • GST component: where GST is shown separately in the invoice, TDS is on the amount excluding GST (CBDT Circular 23/2017); provisions should be computed excluding GST.

Sec 40(a)(ia) — 30% disallowance

If tax is deductible on a sum payable to a resident (interest, commission, brokerage, rent, royalty, fees for professional/technical services, contract payments) and:

  • it has not been deducted, or
  • after deduction, it has not been paid on or before the due date of filing the return under Sec 139(1),

then 30% of that sum is disallowed in computing business income. The disallowed amount is allowed in the year in which the tax is subsequently deducted and paid. A proviso relieves the payer if the resident payee has included the sum in its return and paid tax on it (certified by an accountant in Form 26A), treating the payer as not in default.

For non-residents, Sec 40(a)(i) disallows 100% of the expense until TDS is deducted and paid.

Illustration: March provisions include ₹2.4 crore of professional fees to identifiable firms. TDS at 10% (₹24 lakh) is not deducted. Disallowance = 30% × ₹2.4 crore = ₹72 lakh in FY 2025-26; allowed in the year TDS is deducted and paid. Tax effect at 25.168% (Sec 115BAA rate including surcharge and cess) ≈ ₹18.1 lakh of current tax, reversing later — a deferred tax asset if recovery is expected. Interest under Sec 201(1A) applies on the TDS default as well.

Sec 43B — deduction on actual payment

Sec 43B allows certain deductions only in the year of actual payment, irrespective of the method of accounting:

Clause Item Relief if paid by return due date?
(a) Taxes, duties, cess, fees (e.g., GST not claimed as ITC, property tax, electricity duty) Yes
(b) Employer's contribution to PF, superannuation, gratuity fund or other employee welfare fund Yes
(c) Bonus or commission to employees Yes
(d), (da) Interest on loans from public financial institutions, state financial corporations, state industrial investment corporations, NBFCs (as specified) Yes
(e) Interest on loans/advances from scheduled banks and co-operative banks Yes
(f) Leave encashment Yes
(g) Payments to Indian Railways for use of railway assets Yes
(h) Sums payable to micro or small enterprises beyond the time limit in Sec 15 of the MSMED Act, 2006 (inserted by Finance Act 2023, from AY 2024-25) No — deduction only in the year of actual payment

Key interactions:

  • Employee contributions deducted from salary (PF/ESI) are income of the employer under Sec 2(24)(x) and deductible under Sec 36(1)(va) only if deposited by the due date under the relevant welfare law — the Sec 43B grace to the return due date does not apply (Supreme Court, Checkmate Services, 2022).
  • For 43B(h), the MSMED Act Sec 15 limit is 15 days from acceptance, or up to 45 days where agreed in writing. A year-end payable to a micro/small supplier that is not paid within that limit is disallowed in the current year and allowed when paid. AP ageing must therefore carry the MSME status and acceptance date of each vendor invoice.
  • Converting interest into a new loan or debenture is not "actual payment" (Explanation to Sec 43B).

Illustration: at 31 March 2026, provisions include bonus ₹6.3 crore (paid in July 2026, before the 31 October return due date → allowed in FY 2025-26), leave encashment provision ₹4.1 crore (paid as employees avail; ₹0.6 crore paid by the due date → ₹3.5 crore disallowed), and MSME payables of ₹2.2 crore beyond 45 days unpaid at 31 March (disallowed; allowed in FY 2026-27 when paid).

Close integration

  • T+5 tax step: the accrual schedule carries a TDS flag (section, rate, payee PAN) and a 43B flag for each line (s02). The tax team computes TDS on identifiable provisions for deposit by 30 April and lists 43B items.
  • Deferred tax: 40(a)(ia) and 43B disallowances are temporary differences creating deferred tax assets, subject to Ind AS 12 recognition criteria (Week 3).
  • Tax audit: Form 3CD clauses report 40(a) and 43B disallowances and MSME-related amounts; reconciling the accrual schedule to these clauses avoids year-end surprises.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Provisions reversed and never invoiced: if TDS was deducted on a provision that later lapses, the TDS deposited cannot simply be reclaimed; adjust against future payments to the same payee or pursue refund procedures.
  • Lower-deduction certificates: if the payee holds a valid certificate, TDS on the provision follows the certificate rate (Topic 2 s07).
  • Ind AS vs tax timing of bonus: a bonus accrued in Q4 but paid after the return due date is disallowed for that year and allowed on payment.
  • MSME status changes: suppliers may register as MSMEs mid-year; status is determined at the time of the transaction — maintain dated evidence of Udyam registration.
  • 2025 Act transition: provisions booked at 31 March 2026 fall under the 1961 Act; payments made after 1 April 2026 and deduction allowances in tax year 2026-27 follow the 2025 Act — check transitional provisions.

Key takeaways

  • A year-end provision credited to an identifiable payee generally triggers TDS; deduct and deposit by 30 April.
  • Sec 40(a)(ia): 30% disallowance for TDS failures on resident payments, allowed when TDS is paid; Sec 40(a)(i): 100% for non-residents.
  • Sec 43B: listed items deductible on payment, with relief for payment by the return due date — except Sec 43B(h) MSME payments.
  • AP ageing must carry MSME status and acceptance dates to apply Sec 43B(h).
  • Both regimes create temporary differences and must be flagged in the accrual schedule at T+5.

Practice Questions

Q1. TDS on provisions [L2]

At 31 March, the company books provisions: statutory audit fee ₹40 lakh (firm identified), legal fees ₹18 lakh (two named firms), security services ₹22 lakh (one vendor), and "general provision for expenses" ₹15 lakh (no identified payees). TDS rates: professional fees 10%, contracts 2%. Determine the TDS to deduct, the deposit due date and the Sec 40(a)(ia) disallowance if no TDS is deducted on any provision.

💡 Hint: Separate identifiable-payee provisions from the general provision before applying TDS.

Q2. Sec 43B classification [L2]

Classify each item for FY 2025-26 (return due date 31 October 2026): (a) bonus ₹3.2 crore paid on 15 September 2026; (b) employee PF contribution for March deposited on 20 April 2026 (due date 15 April); (c) employer PF contribution for March deposited on 20 April 2026; (d) interest due to a scheduled bank ₹1.1 crore converted into a term loan; (e) ₹48 lakh owed to a micro enterprise for goods accepted on 1 March 2026 with no written agreement, paid on 30 April 2026.

Q3. Deferred tax effect [L2]

Disallowances for FY 2025-26: Sec 40(a)(ia) ₹72 lakh, Sec 43B leave encashment ₹3.5 crore, Sec 43B(h) MSME ₹2.2 crore. Tax rate 25.168%. Compute the deferred tax asset, state the recognition condition under Ind AS 12 and describe how each reverses.

Q4. Process design [L3]

Design the T+5 "tax interplay" step in the close calendar: data fields required in the accrual schedule and AP ageing, owners, outputs (TDS deposit list for 30 April, 43B tracker, MSME 43B(h) list), controls, and the reconciliation to Form 3CD clauses. Include how you would adapt the step for tax year 2026-27 under the Income-tax Act, 2025.
