Key concept

Accrual Amount: Expected Period Cost Less Amount Invoiced

Concept: Accrual = Expected period cost (contract rate × usage, or trailing average) − Amount already invoiced for the period

Statement

\[ A_p = \max\left(0,\; E_p - I_p\right) \]

\[ E_p = \begin{cases} r \times u_p + F_p & \text{(contract rate × usage, plus fixed charges)} \\[4pt] \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} C_{p-k} \times \text{adj}_p & \text{(trailing average, adjusted)} \end{cases} \]

where \(A_p\) is the accrual for period \(p\); \(E_p\) is the expected cost of goods/services received in \(p\); \(I_p\) is the amount already invoiced and booked for the same period's services; \(r\) is the contract rate, \(u_p\) the usage in the period and \(F_p\) fixed charges; \(C_{p-k}\) are actual costs of the previous \(n\) periods; and \(\text{adj}_p\) is an adjustment factor for known changes (working days, tariff revisions, volumes). If \(I_p > E_p\), the excess is not a negative accrual but a potential prepayment or an indicator that \(E_p\) is understated — investigate.

Explanation

The formula captures two disciplines. First, accrue for services received, measured as precisely as the data allows: contract rate × usage where usage is measurable at period-end (meters, man-days, trips); trailing average only where it is not, and adjusted for known drivers. Second, deduct what has already been invoiced for the same period, so partial or interim billing does not produce double expense. The period dimension matters: invoices for prior periods are not deducted from the current period's accrual (they clear prior accruals).

Common errors the formula avoids: accruing a full month when half has been invoiced; using PO value outstanding rather than usage (topic pitfall 1); using a trailing average when a tariff change is known; and accruing negative amounts that net against other lines.

The trailing average is a fallback with known weaknesses: it lags price and volume changes, hides trends, and can perpetuate errors. A 3-month average is common for utilities without meter data; 6 months smooths seasonality poorly in seasonal businesses — consider same-month-last-year adjusted for growth.

Illustration

March electricity for a plant: meter reading not available for one feeder. Feeder A: 2,60,000 kWh × ₹8.40 = ₹21.84 lakh; feeder B: trailing 3-month average ₹11.2 lakh × (26 production days in March ÷ 24 average) = ₹12.13 lakh; fixed demand charge ₹2.5 lakh. \(E_p\) = ₹36.47 lakh. The DISCOM issued a provisional bill for 1–15 March of ₹14.0 lakh, already booked. Accrual \(A_p\) = 36.47 − 14.0 = ₹22.47 lakh.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute an accrual [L1]

Security services: contract rate ₹1,050 per guard-day; 48 guards for 31 days and 6 additional guards for 10 days in March. Vendor has invoiced ₹7,20,000 for 1–15 March. Compute the March accrual.

Q2. Trailing average adjustment [L2]

Courier costs for December–February were ₹4.8 lakh, ₹5.1 lakh and ₹4.5 lakh. In March, shipment volumes were 30% higher than the three-month average and the courier rate increased by 5% from 1 March. No March invoice has been received. Compute the March accrual and explain why an unadjusted trailing average would be misleading.

💡 Hint: Apply both a volume and a price adjustment to the average.

Q3. Negative accrual judgement [L2]

For a monthly AMC of ₹2.4 lakh, the vendor invoiced ₹4.8 lakh in March covering March and April. Explain why the accrual formula gives a negative result, the correct accounting for the excess, and the control that should detect it.
