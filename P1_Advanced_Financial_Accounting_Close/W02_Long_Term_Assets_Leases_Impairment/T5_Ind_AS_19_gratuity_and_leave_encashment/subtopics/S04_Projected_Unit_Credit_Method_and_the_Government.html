Subtopic note

Projected Unit Credit Method and the Government Bond Discount Rate

Scope: PUCM measurement (19.67); discount rate by reference to government bond yields (Ind AS 19.83, vs IAS 19 high-quality corporate bonds)

Why it matters

The projected unit credit method (PUCM) is the only method Ind AS 19 permits for measuring defined benefit obligations, and the discount rate is the assumption that moves the DBO most. Understanding the mechanics lets you rebuild a single-employee valuation, explain why current service cost rises over an employee's career, and evaluate whether the actuary's rate is appropriate. Ind AS 19 also contains one of the most-cited Ind AS carve-outs from IFRS: the discount rate is based on government bond yields, not high-quality corporate bond yields, which affects comparability with IFRS reporters and the US GAAP packs of Indian subsidiaries.

Core notes

PUCM (Ind AS 19.67–69)

An entity uses the projected unit credit method to determine the present value of its defined benefit obligations, the related current service cost and, where applicable, past service cost (19.67). PUCM (also called "accrued benefit method pro-rated on service" or "benefit/years of service method"):

  • sees each period of service as giving rise to an additional unit of benefit entitlement; and
  • measures each unit separately to build up the final obligation (19.68).

The entity discounts the whole of a post-employment benefit obligation, even if part is expected to be settled within 12 months (19.69).

Mechanics for a single employee:

  1. Project the benefit at the expected exit date using projected final salary (salary escalation) and total service at exit.
  2. Attribute the projected benefit to periods of service using the plan's benefit formula (19.70), which for gratuity is linear in service. If later years would give a materially higher level of benefit (back-end loading), attribute on a straight-line basis (19.70(b)).
  3. Discount the attributed benefit to the reporting date, weighting by the probability of exit at each future date (attrition, mortality, retirement).

\[ \text{DBO} = \sum_{k} q_k \times \frac{B_k \times \frac{S_0}{S_k}}{(1+r)^{t_k}} \]

where \(k\) indexes possible exit years, \(q_k\) the probability of exit in year \(k\), \(B_k\) the projected benefit on exit in year \(k\) (based on projected salary and total service \(S_k\)), \(S_0/S_k\) the fraction of service already rendered (attribution), \(r\) the discount rate and \(t_k\) the time to exit.

Current service cost is the present value of the benefit unit earned in the year:

\[ \text{CSC} = \sum_{k} q_k \times \frac{B_k \times \frac{1}{S_k}}{(1+r)^{t_k}} \]

Worked single-employee example

An employee aged 55 has 15 years of service and current monthly wages ₹60,000. Retirement at 58 (exit with certainty in 3 years; ignore attrition and mortality). Salary escalation 8%; discount rate 7%. Benefit cap not binding.

  • Projected wages at exit: \(60{,}000 \times 1.08^{3} = \text{₹}75{,}583\).
  • Total service at exit: 18 years. Projected benefit \(B = 75{,}583 \times 15/26 \times 18 = \text{₹}7{,}84{,}899\).
  • Attributed to past service (15/18): ₹6,54,083. DBO = \(6{,}54{,}083 \div 1.07^{3} = \text{₹}5{,}33{,}926\).
  • CSC for the coming year (1/18 of B, discounted): \(43{,}606 \div 1.07^{3} = \text{₹}35{,}596\) (if CSC is measured at the start of the year with the discount period to exit).
  • Interest cost for the year: \(5{,}33{,}926 \times 7\% = \text{₹}37{,}375\).

Note the "salary-related" back-loading: PUCM charges more per year as the employee ages because each unit is discounted over a shorter period and benefits are based on projected final salary.

Attribution and the benefit cap (19.70–74)

Benefit is attributed to periods of service until the date when further service will lead to no material amount of further benefits, other than from further salary increases (19.70(b), 19.73). For gratuity with a ₹20 lakh cap, an employee whose projected benefit hits the cap at year 25 has the benefit attributed over 25 years, not the full career. Actuarial software handles this; ensure the actuary has applied the current ceiling.

Actuarial assumptions (19.75–80)

Assumptions must be unbiased (neither imprudent nor excessively conservative) and mutually compatible (for example, salary escalation and discount rate both reflecting the same inflation expectation) (19.75–78). Financial assumptions are based on market expectations at the end of the reporting period for the period over which obligations are to be settled (19.80).

Discount rate (Ind AS 19.83–86)

Ind AS 19.83. The rate used to discount post-employment benefit obligations (both funded and unfunded) shall be determined by reference to market yields at the end of the reporting period on government bonds. However, subsidiaries, associates, joint ventures and branches domiciled outside India shall discount using the rate determined by reference to market yields on high-quality corporate bonds at the end of the reporting period, in case such subsidiaries, associates, JVs and branches are required to do so under their local laws; where there is no deep market in such bonds, market yields on government bonds of that currency are used. The currency and term of the bonds shall be consistent with the currency and estimated term of the obligations.

IAS 19.83. Uses market yields on high-quality corporate bonds; only in countries with no deep market in such bonds are government bond yields used.

Feature Ind AS 19 IAS 19
Primary reference Government bond yields High-quality corporate bond yields
Fallback Corporate bonds for foreign operations where local law requires Government bonds where no deep corporate market
Effect Lower rate → higher DBO and higher service cost Higher rate → lower DBO

The rationale for the carve-out was the absence of a deep market for high-quality corporate bonds in India when Ind AS was framed, and consistency of practice.

Term (19.84–86). The discount rate reflects the estimated timing of benefit payments. In practice, a single weighted-average rate is derived from the zero-coupon G-sec yield curve matching the plan's duration (or the cash flows are discounted at each tenor's spot rate). Where there is no deep market in bonds with sufficiently long maturity, extrapolate current market rates along the yield curve (19.86).

Not reflected. The discount rate reflects time value only; it does not reflect investment risk, actuarial risk or the entity's own credit risk (19.84). Expected return on plan assets is not used — the same discount rate determines interest income on assets (s05).

Practical rate setting

Step Practice
Duration From actuary's maturity profile (weighted average duration, e.g. 7.5 years)
Yield source FBIL/CCIL G-sec zero-coupon yield curve or par yields at 31 March
Rate Interpolate yield at the duration; round to nearest 5 bp; document
Consistency Same approach every year; changes in approach disclosed
Quarterly Update for material yield movements in interim results (Ind AS 34)

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Very short-duration plans (high attrition). A 4-year duration uses short-tenor yields; using the 10-year benchmark overstates the rate in an upward-sloping curve.
  • Foreign subsidiaries. A UK subsidiary of an Indian parent may use AA corporate bond yields if local law requires; in consolidation, Ind AS 19.83 permits that.
  • Back-loaded plans. Enhanced company gratuity schemes (e.g., 30 days per year after 15 years) are attributed straight-line.
  • Census-date valuations. If the census is at 31 December, roll forward to 31 March but update the discount rate to 31 March.
  • Using the 10-year G-sec par yield for every plan. Common shortcut; acceptable only if duration and curve shape make the difference immaterial.

Key takeaways

  • PUCM attributes benefit to each year of service, projects final salary and discounts each unit using probability-weighted exit dates (19.67–69).
  • Attribution follows the benefit formula, straight-line where back-loaded, stopping where further service adds no material benefit (19.70–73).
  • Ind AS 19.83 uses government bond yields; IAS 19 uses high-quality corporate bond yields — a notified carve-out.
  • Rate term must match the obligation's duration; currency must match the benefit currency.
  • Assumptions must be unbiased and mutually compatible; inflation in salary escalation and discount rate should agree.

Practice Questions

Q1. Single-employee PUCM [L2]

An employee aged 50 with 10 years of service earns monthly wages of ₹80,000. Retirement age 58; ignore attrition and mortality. Salary escalation 7%, discount rate 6.8%. Benefit cap ₹20 lakh. Required: compute the projected benefit, the DBO at 31 March 2026, the CSC for FY27 and the interest cost for FY27.

💡 Hint: Check whether the cap binds on the projected benefit before attributing to past service.

Q2. Choosing the discount rate [L2]

A gratuity plan's weighted average duration is 6.2 years. At 31 March 2026, zero-coupon G-sec yields are: 5 years 6.42%, 6 years 6.50%, 7 years 6.58%, 10 years 6.71%. The actuary proposes 6.71% "being the benchmark 10-year yield". Required: recommend a rate, estimate the DBO difference using the rule of thumb that DBO changes by duration × rate change, for a DBO of ₹60 crore, and draft the communication to the actuary.

Q3. Ind AS vs IFRS discount rate [L2]

An Indian subsidiary of a European group reports to its parent under IFRS. Its gratuity DBO under Ind AS (G-sec rate 6.7%) is ₹40 crore with a duration of 9 years; AAA corporate bond yields are about 7.4%. Required: explain the difference in principle, estimate the IFRS DBO, and explain whether the parent may adopt the G-sec rate under IAS 19.

Q4. Back-loaded benefit attribution [L3]

A company's enhanced gratuity scheme pays 15 days' wages per year for the first 10 years and 30 days per year for each year thereafter, uncapped. Required: explain how PUCM attribution applies under 19.70(b), illustrate the attribution for an employee projected to exit after 20 years, and discuss the effect on CSC in early versus later years.
