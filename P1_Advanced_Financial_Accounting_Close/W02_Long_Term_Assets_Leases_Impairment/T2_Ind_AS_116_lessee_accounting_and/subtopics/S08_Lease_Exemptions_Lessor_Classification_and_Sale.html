Subtopic note

Lease Exemptions, Lessor Classification and Sale-and-Leaseback

Scope: Exemptions (short-term ≤12 months, low-value), lessor finance vs operating classification, sale-and-leaseback

Why it matters

The two lessee exemptions keep thousands of small leases (laptops, guest houses, short project sites) off the balance sheet, but they are frequently misapplied — 11-month leave-and-licence agreements renewed for years are a classic Indian example. Lessors (real estate developers, REITs, equipment financiers, aircraft lessors, NBFCs) still use a dual finance/operating model. Sale-and-leaseback is used by Indian companies to monetise real estate, towers and data centres (often with InvITs/REITs) and has its own rules, including the 2024 amendment on measuring the leaseback liability. All three are tested in interviews and appear in real transactions.

Core notes

Short-term lease exemption (Ind AS 116.5–8, B34)

A short-term lease is a lease that, at the commencement date, has a lease term of 12 months or less. A lease that contains a purchase option is not a short-term lease (Appendix A).

  • The election is made by class of underlying asset (Ind AS 116.8).
  • The lessee recognises payments as an expense on a straight-line basis (or another systematic basis) over the lease term (Ind AS 116.6).
  • The lease term is determined using the normal rules (s03) — including reasonably certain renewals and enforceability with economic penalties. A series of 11-month agreements that the lessee is reasonably certain to keep renewing, or where both parties would incur more than insignificant penalties to not renew, is not short-term.
  • If the lease is modified or the lease term changes, the lessee treats it as a new lease (Ind AS 116.7).

Low-value asset exemption (Ind AS 116.5, B3–B8)

Elected lease-by-lease. The assessment is on an absolute basis — the value of the underlying asset when new, regardless of the age of the asset being leased and of the lessee's size (B3–B4). Conditions (B5):

  • the lessee can benefit from use of the asset on its own or with readily available resources; and
  • the asset is not highly dependent on, or highly interrelated with, other assets.

Ind AS 116 does not set a threshold; the IASB Basis for Conclusions referred to assets of around USD 5,000 or less when new — Indian practice commonly uses ₹3–5 lakh. Typical: laptops, tablets, mobile phones, small office furniture, water dispensers. Not low-value: cars (even small), and a lease of an asset that is subleased (head lease cannot be low-value if the asset is subleased, B7).

Presentation of exempt leases

Expense in P&L (often "rent" within other expenses), cash outflows in operating activities, and disclosure of short-term and low-value lease expense (Ind AS 116.53(c), (d)) and the commitment for short-term leases if the portfolio differs (Ind AS 116.55).

Lessor finance vs operating classification (Ind AS 116.61–66)

A lessor classifies each lease at inception as:

  • Finance lease — transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership of an underlying asset;
  • Operating lease — otherwise.

Indicators (Ind AS 116.63), individually or in combination, normally leading to finance lease:

  1. ownership transfers by the end of the lease term;
  2. a purchase option at a price expected to be sufficiently lower than fair value that exercise is reasonably certain;
  3. the lease term is for the major part of the economic life of the asset;
  4. at inception, the PV of lease payments amounts to at least substantially all of the fair value of the asset;
  5. the asset is of such a specialised nature that only the lessee can use it without major modifications.

Additional indicators (Ind AS 116.64): lessor's losses on cancellation borne by the lessee; gains/losses from fluctuation in residual fair value accrue to the lessee; ability to continue for a secondary period at substantially below-market rent. Land and buildings elements are assessed separately (B55–B57).

Finance lease (lessor): derecognise asset; recognise a net investment in the lease (PV of lease payments plus unguaranteed residual, discounted at the implicit rate); finance income over the term at a constant periodic rate (Ind AS 116.67–76). Manufacturer/dealer lessors recognise selling profit at commencement.

Operating lease (lessor): keep the asset; recognise lease income on a straight-line basis (or another systematic basis) (Ind AS 116.81); depreciate the asset; add initial direct costs to the asset and expense over the term. Fixed escalations are straight-lined — unlike the lessee, a lessor in an operating lease does straight-line 15%-every-3-years escalations (Indian REITs show "straight-lining of rent" adjustments). Modifications of operating leases are treated as new leases from the effective date (Ind AS 116.87).

Subleases: an intermediate lessor classifies the sublease by reference to the ROU asset of the head lease, not the underlying asset (B58).

Sale-and-leaseback (Ind AS 116.98–103)

Step 1 — Is the transfer a sale? Apply Ind AS 115 performance obligation requirements to determine whether control of the asset transfers to the buyer-lessor (Ind AS 116.99). A repurchase option (call) held by the seller-lessee generally prevents a sale (Ind AS 115.B66), unless the asset is readily available in the market and the option is at fair value.

Step 2a — If a sale:

  • the seller-lessee measures the ROU asset arising from the leaseback at the proportion of the previous carrying amount that relates to the right of use retained;
  • recognises only the gain or loss relating to the rights transferred to the buyer-lessor (Ind AS 116.100(a));
  • adjusts for off-market terms: if fair value of consideration ≠ fair value of the asset, or payments are not at market rates — below-market terms are a prepayment of lease payments; above-market terms are additional financing from the buyer (Ind AS 116.101–102);
  • the buyer-lessor applies lessor accounting.

\[ \text{Gain recognised} = (FV - CA) \times \frac{FV - PV_{\text{leaseback}}}{FV} \]

where \( FV \) is fair value of the asset, \( CA \) is its carrying amount and \( PV_{\text{leaseback}} \) is the PV of leaseback payments (at market terms); \( \frac{PV_{\text{leaseback}}}{FV} \) is the proportion retained. ROU = \( CA \times \frac{PV_{\text{leaseback}}}{FV} \).

Step 2b — If not a sale: the seller-lessee continues to recognise the asset and recognises a financial liability under Ind AS 109 for the proceeds (a financing); the buyer-lessor recognises a financial asset (Ind AS 116.103).

2024 amendment (subsequent measurement): In measuring the lease liability arising from a leaseback, the seller-lessee determines "lease payments" or "revised lease payments" in a way that does not result in recognising any gain or loss relating to the right of use it retains (Ind AS 116.102A, notified by the Companies (Indian Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules, 2024, effective for periods beginning on or after 1 April 2024). This matters for leasebacks with variable payments not linked to an index: later remeasurements or partial terminations cannot produce a gain on the retained right of use. It does not prescribe a specific method; entities develop a policy (e.g. treating expected variable payments as part of lease payments).

Worked sale-and-leaseback illustration

Deccan Logistics Ltd sells a warehouse (carrying amount ₹60 crore, fair value ₹100 crore) to a REIT for ₹100 crore on 1 April 2025 and leases it back for 10 years at ₹9 crore per year in arrears (market rent); IBR 9%. Control transfers (sale).

PV of leaseback = 9 × 6.4177 = ₹57.76 crore. Proportion retained = 57.76/100 = 57.76%.

  • ROU = 60 × 57.76% = ₹34.66 crore.
  • Total gain = 100 − 60 = ₹40 crore; gain on rights transferred = 40 × (1 − 0.5776) = ₹16.90 crore.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • 11-month renewals — not short-term if lease term (with economic penalties) exceeds 12 months.
  • Short-term election by class — cannot cherry-pick within a class.
  • Low-value lease of a laptop bundled into a larger IT outsourcing deal with interdependent assets fails B5.
  • Lessor of land with 99-year lease and upfront premium (industrial parks, SEZ developers) — often a finance lease of land; revenue recognition questions under Ind AS 115 vs 116.
  • Sale-and-leaseback with a repurchase option — not a sale; financing.
  • Above-market leaseback rent — part of proceeds is financing, not sale consideration.
  • Related-party REIT/InvIT sponsors — control and Ind AS 110 consolidation analysis may override the sale conclusion at group level.

Key takeaways

  • Short-term (≤12 months, no purchase option) is elected by class; low-value (asset value when new) is elected lease-by-lease.
  • Lease term for the short-term test uses full Ind AS 116 rules, including economic penalties.
  • Lessors still classify leases as finance or operating based on transfer of substantially all risks and rewards; operating lessors straight-line escalations.
  • Sale-and-leaseback: if control transfers, recognise ROU as a proportion of carrying amount and gain only on rights transferred; otherwise treat as financing.
  • The 2024 amendment prevents gains on the retained right of use when measuring the leaseback liability.

Practice Questions

Q1. Short-term or not [L2]

A construction company rents site offices for 10 months (no options), a guest house under rolling 11-month agreements since 2018 (both parties can terminate with 1 month's notice; the company has spent ₹25 lakh on furnishing), and a crane for 12 months with an option to buy at the end at a nominal price. Determine whether each qualifies for the short-term exemption.

Q2. Lessor classification [L2]

An equipment lessor leases a ₹10 crore MRI machine (economic life 10 years) to a hospital for 8 years at ₹1.7 crore per year in arrears; the hospital guarantees a residual value of ₹1 crore; implicit rate 10%. Classify the lease, and if it is a finance lease compute the net investment and first-year finance income.

💡 Hint: Compare the PV of lease payments, including the guaranteed residual, with fair value.

Q3. Sale-and-leaseback with off-market price [L3]

A company sells an office (carrying amount ₹40 crore, fair value ₹70 crore) for ₹80 crore and leases it back for 7 years at ₹10 crore per year in arrears (market rent ₹8.2 crore); IBR 9.5%. Account for the transaction, separating the additional financing, and compute the ROU, lease liability, financial liability and gain recognised.

Q4. Operating lessor straight-lining [L1]

A REIT leases office space for 9 years at ₹1 crore per year in year 1–3, ₹1.15 crore in years 4–6 and ₹1.3225 crore in years 7–9. Compute the annual straight-line lease income and the receivable at the end of year 2.

Q5. Failed sale [L2]

A telecom company sells its towers to an InvIT sponsored by a third party for ₹2,000 crore and leases back tenancy for 20 years, with an option to repurchase the towers after 10 years at a fixed price of ₹1,200 crore. Determine whether a sale has occurred under Ind AS 115 and describe the accounting if it has not.
