Subtopic note

Lease Identification: Identified Asset, Benefits and Direction of Use

Scope: Lease identification (Ind AS 116.9, B9–B31): identified asset, substantive substitution rights, economic benefits, right to direct use — warehousing, data centres, captive power

Why it matters

Whether a contract contains a lease decides whether an asset and liability appear on the balance sheet. Many Indian contracts are not labelled "lease" — third-party logistics with dedicated warehouses, data-centre co-location, captive power purchase agreements, dedicated vessel charters, IT outsourcing with dedicated servers, cell-site sharing, transportation contracts with dedicated trucks. Missing an embedded lease understates assets, debt and EBITDA; wrongly identifying a lease inflates them. Auditors and NFRA examine service contracts for embedded leases, and FP&A teams need to know whether a new outsourcing deal will add lease liabilities to net debt.

Core notes

The definition

A contract is, or contains, a lease if it conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration (Ind AS 116.9). Control requires, throughout the period of use, both (Ind AS 116.B9):

  1. the right to obtain substantially all of the economic benefits from use of the identified asset; and
  2. the right to direct the use of the identified asset.

Assessment is at inception and reassessed only if the terms and conditions change (Ind AS 116.11). The "period of time" can be described in terms of usage (e.g. number of production units).

Step 1 — Is there an identified asset? (B13–B20)

  • An asset is typically identified by being explicitly specified in the contract, or implicitly specified at the time it is made available (B13). A dedicated warehouse built to the customer's design and located at its plant is implicitly specified.
  • A capacity portion is an identified asset if it is physically distinct (e.g. a floor of a building, a specific fibre strand). A capacity portion that is not physically distinct (e.g. 60% of a pipeline's capacity) is not an identified asset unless it represents substantially all of the capacity, so that the customer gets substantially all benefits (B20).
  • Substantive substitution rights (B14–B19): even if specified, there is no identified asset if the supplier has the substantive right to substitute the asset throughout the period of use. A right is substantive only if both:
  • the supplier has the practical ability to substitute alternative assets throughout the period of use (the customer cannot prevent it, and alternatives are readily available or sourced within a reasonable time); and
  • the supplier would benefit economically from substitution (benefits exceed costs).
  • Assessment is based on facts at inception, excluding future events not considered likely (B16). A right to substitute only on a specific date or after an event, or only for repairs, is not substantive (B18). If the customer cannot readily determine whether the right is substantive, it presumes it is not (B19).

Step 2 — Substantially all economic benefits (B21–B23)

  • Benefits include primary output and by-products, and other benefits from use that could be realised in a commercial transaction with a third party (e.g. selling renewable energy certificates from a captive solar plant).
  • Consider benefits within the defined scope of the customer's right — e.g. restrictions to a territory do not prevent "substantially all".
  • Payments of a portion of cash flows (e.g. revenue share) to the supplier do not prevent the customer from obtaining substantially all benefits (B23).

Step 3 — Right to direct the use (B24–B30)

The customer has the right to direct use if either:

  • (a) it has the right to direct how and for what purpose the asset is used throughout the period of use (B25–B27) — relevant decision-making rights include what to produce, when, where and whether to produce, and how much; or
  • (b) the relevant decisions are predetermined and the customer either has the right to operate the asset (or direct others to operate it) without the supplier having the right to change instructions, or the customer designed the asset (or specific aspects) in a way that predetermines how and for what purpose it will be used (B24(b)).

Protective rights of the supplier (e.g. maximum usage limits, safety rules, compliance with law) do not prevent the customer having the right to direct use (B30). Operating and maintenance decisions alone, made by the supplier, do not give the supplier control if the customer decides how and for what purpose (B27).

Sector applications

Warehousing / 3PL. A 3PL provider stores the customer's goods. If the contract specifies a dedicated warehouse and the provider cannot practically substitute (customised racking, location fixed for the customer), and the customer decides what is stored, when goods move in and out and how much space is used, the contract contains a lease. If the provider can move goods among its multi-client facilities at its discretion and benefits economically from doing so, there is no identified asset — a service contract.

Data centres. Co-location contracts for "200 racks in Hall 3, cage C" with no supplier right to relocate may contain a lease of a physically distinct space. Where the supplier can relocate racks among halls at any time and benefits from optimising space, substitution is substantive and there is no lease. Server hosting where the provider decides which servers run the workload is usually a service. Dedicated hardware that only the customer uses, configured to its specifications, may be a lease.

Captive power (group captive, PPAs). A solar plant built for one customer that takes substantially all output: the plant is identified (implicitly specified). If pricing is per unit and the customer takes all output, it obtains substantially all benefits. Direction of use: in a solar plant, decisions about how and for what purpose are largely predetermined by design (it generates when the sun shines); the customer directs use if it designed the plant or its relevant aspects (B24(b)(ii)). If the developer designed and operates it, usually there is no lease (Ind AS 116 Illustrative Example 9 analyses a solar farm). Group captive structures with equity in an SPV also require Ind AS 110/111 analysis.

Other Indian examples: dedicated tank storage at ports, dedicated cell towers or fibre strands (IRUs), vessel time charters (lease of vessel plus crew services) vs voyage charters (service), dedicated buses for employee transport.

Decision flow

Question If No If Yes
Identified asset (explicit/implicit; physically distinct or substantially all capacity)? Not a lease Continue
Supplier substitution right substantive (practical ability + economic benefit)? Continue Not a lease
Customer obtains substantially all economic benefits? Not a lease Continue
Customer directs how and for what purpose (or predetermined + operates/designed)? Not a lease Lease

Accounting consequence

If a lease exists, the lessee separates lease and non-lease components (s02) and applies the recognition model, unless the short-term or low-value exemption applies (s08). Contracts combined under Ind AS 116.B2 (entered at or near the same time with the same counterparty and negotiated as a package) are assessed together.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Substitution for repairs only is not substantive.
  • Supplier's operating decisions (e.g. ship crew, pilot) do not prevent customer control if the customer decides route and timing.
  • Embedded leases in outsourcing: a 7-year IT deal with dedicated servers at the customer's site may contain a lease component with significant ROU.
  • Take-or-pay with substantially all output from a dedicated plant leans towards a lease unless the supplier directs use.
  • Pipeline or fibre capacity shares below "substantially all" and not physically distinct are not leases.
  • Contracts with related parties — terms may not be arm's length; still apply the definition, and consider Ind AS 24 disclosure.
  • Licences of IP are outside Ind AS 116: lessor licences fall under Ind AS 115 (Ind AS 116.3(d)) and lessee rights under licensing agreements for films, patents, copyrights and similar items fall under Ind AS 38 (Ind AS 116.3(e)); a lessee may, but need not, apply Ind AS 116 to other intangible assets (Ind AS 116.4).

Key takeaways

  • A lease = right to control use of an identified asset for a period in exchange for consideration.
  • Identified asset fails if the supplier has a substantive substitution right — practical ability plus economic benefit throughout the period.
  • The customer must obtain substantially all economic benefits and direct how and for what purpose the asset is used.
  • Predetermined-use assets are leases only if the customer operates or designed them.
  • Warehousing, data centre and captive power contracts often contain embedded leases — analyse facts, not labels.

Practice Questions

Q1. 3PL warehouse contract [L2]

FreshMart Ltd enters a 5-year contract with a 3PL provider for storage of chilled goods in a 50,000 sq ft warehouse in Bhiwandi identified by address. The provider may move FreshMart's goods to another of its facilities only with FreshMart's consent. FreshMart decides inventory levels, receipt and dispatch schedules; the provider operates the warehouse and employs staff. Analyse whether the contract contains a lease and identify the lease and non-lease components.

Q2. Co-location with relocation rights [L2]

A fintech company contracts for 40 racks in a Navi Mumbai data centre for 6 years. The provider may relocate racks to any hall at its own cost with 48 hours' notice; halls are identical and the provider routinely re-optimises space to fit new customers. Evaluate whether there is an identified asset.

💡 Hint: Test both limbs of the substitution right — practical ability and economic benefit.

Q3. Captive solar PPA [L3]

An auto-component manufacturer signs a 25-year PPA for 100% of the output of a 20 MW solar plant to be built by a developer on the manufacturer's land. The manufacturer specified panel type, layout and tracking system in the tender and approved the final design; the developer operates and maintains the plant. Analyse lease identification under B24(b), and discuss the accounting consequences if a lease exists, including the measurement of payments linked to units generated.

Q4. Time charter vs voyage charter [L1]

Distinguish the lease analysis of a 3-year time charter of a specified vessel (the charterer decides ports and cargo; the owner provides crew) from a voyage charter for one shipment of cargo on a vessel of the owner's choice.

Q5. Interview case: outsourcing deal [L3]

The CFO asks whether a proposed 8-year logistics outsourcing deal (dedicated trucks branded with the company's logo, routes decided by the company, the vendor can replace trucks only when under maintenance) will increase reported net debt. Prepare a 6–8 sentence response covering the lease analysis and expected financial statement impact.
