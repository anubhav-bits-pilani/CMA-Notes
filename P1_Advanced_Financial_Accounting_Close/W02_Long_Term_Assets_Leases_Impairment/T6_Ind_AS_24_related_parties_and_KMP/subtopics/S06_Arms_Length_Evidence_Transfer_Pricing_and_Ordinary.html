Arm's-Length Evidence: Transfer Pricing and Ordinary Course Tests

Scope: Arm's-length evidence: transfer pricing (Sec 92, domestic TP Sec 92BA), benchmarking methods, ordinary course of business test

Why it matters

"Ordinary course of business and at arm's length" is the phrase that exempts most RPTs from Sec 188 Board and shareholder approval, and the phrase audit committees are asked to confirm every quarter. Ind AS 24.23 permits an arm's-length statement in the financial statements only if it can be substantiated. The Income-tax Act's transfer pricing regime provides the most developed method for substantiating it — and for international and specified domestic transactions, it is mandatory. A finance professional must be able to assemble evidence that satisfies the audit committee, the statutory auditor and the transfer pricing officer, even though each applies a slightly different lens.

Core notes

The three lenses

Lens Test Consequence
Companies Act Sec 188 Ordinary course and arm's length (Explanation (b): as if unrelated, so that there is no conflict of interest) Exempt from Sec 188 approvals; audit committee still approves (Sec 177)
Ind AS 24.23 Arm's-length statement only if substantiated Disclosure wording; otherwise omit the claim
Income-tax Act Sec 92–92F Arm's length price (ALP) computed under prescribed methods Adjustment to taxable income if transaction price outside the arm's length range
SEBI LODR Reg 23 No exemption for material RPTs; audit committee evaluates terms Approval papers must justify pricing

Transfer pricing under the Income-tax Act

Sec 92(1): any income arising from an international transaction shall be computed having regard to the arm's length price. Sec 92(2A) and 92BA extend this to specified domestic transactions (SDTs).

Associated enterprises (Sec 92A): participation in management, control or capital (directly or indirectly), with deeming tests in 92A(2) — for example, holding 26% or more of voting power, advancing loans of 51% or more of the book value of total assets, guaranteeing 10% or more of total borrowings, appointing more than half of the Board, dependence on intangibles or 90%+ of raw-material supply under influenced prices.

International transaction (Sec 92B): transaction between two or more AEs, either or both non-residents, in the nature of purchase, sale or lease of tangible or intangible property, provision of services, lending or borrowing, guarantees, cost-sharing, business restructuring, etc.

Specified domestic transaction (Sec 92BA): after the Finance Act 2017 removed Sec 40A(2)(b) expenditure, SDTs principally cover transactions of undertakings claiming profit-linked deductions (Sec 80-IA(8)/(10) and related provisions, Sec 10AA) and transactions with persons having close connection under the concessional regime of Sec 115BAB (new manufacturing companies), where the aggregate value exceeds ₹20 crore in the year. A domestic group without tax-holiday units or 115BAB companies usually has no SDTs — so domestic RPTs are often not covered by TP law, yet still need arm's-length evidence for Sec 188.

Methods (Sec 92C, Rule 10B):

Method How it works Typical use
Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) Price in comparable uncontrolled transactions, adjusted for differences Commodities, interest rates on loans, guarantee fees, royalties with comparable licences
Resale Price Method (RPM) Resale price to unrelated party less normal gross margin Distributors buying from AEs
Cost Plus Method (CPM) Direct and indirect costs plus normal gross mark-up Contract manufacturing, semi-finished goods
Profit Split Method (PSM) Combined profit split by relative contributions Integrated operations, unique intangibles
Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM) Net profit margin relative to costs, sales or assets compared with comparables Most common in India for services (IT/ITeS captives, mark-up on total cost)
Other method (Rule 10AB) Any method that takes into account the price that would have been charged in comparable uncontrolled transactions (quotes, valuations) Asset transfers, unique transactions

Most appropriate method (Rule 10C): chosen considering the nature of transaction, functions, assets and risks (FAR analysis), availability and reliability of data, and the degree of comparability.

Arm's length range (Rule 10CA): where six or more comparables are available under CUP, RPM, CPM or TNMM, a range from the 35th to 65th percentile of the dataset is used; if the transaction price is within the range it is accepted, otherwise ALP is the median. Where fewer than six comparables (or for PSM/other method), the arithmetic mean is used with a tolerance of 3% (1% for wholesale traders) as notified. Multiple-year data (current year and two preceding years) may be used.

Compliance: Form 3CEB accountant's report (Sec 92E) by the due date (generally 31 October); documentation under Rule 10D for transactions above ₹1 crore (aggregate international transactions); master file and CbCR for large groups (Sec 286). Penalties for failure to maintain documentation, furnish reports or for adjustments. Safe harbour rules (Rules 10TA–10TG) and Advance Pricing Agreements (Sec 92CC) offer certainty.

Ordinary course of business test

Not defined in the Companies Act. Indicators used by practitioners and the ICAI/MCA guidance:

  • the transaction is within the objects in the Memorandum of Association;
  • it is frequent or regular, part of the normal business pattern (not one-off);
  • it is of the type the company enters into with unrelated parties;
  • it is revenue/operational rather than capital or extraordinary (sale of a business unit is not ordinary course);
  • it is in line with industry practice.

Ordinary course alone does not exempt; the transaction must also be at arm's length.

Building the evidence file

  1. Characterise the transaction — nature, volume, terms, functions/risks of each party.
  2. Choose the method — CUP if internal comparables (same product sold to unrelated customers) or external quotes exist; TNMM or cost-plus for services.
  3. Gather comparables — internal (own unrelated transactions), external quotes (at least three), market prices (exchange rates, commodity indices, SBI/MCLR-based rates for loans), databases (Prowess, Capitaline for TNMM).
  4. Adjust for differences — credit terms, volume discounts, delivery terms (Incoterms), warranties, currency.
  5. Conclude — price within range; document reasoning; record reviewer.
  6. Refresh — at least annually or on material change; present to the audit committee with the approval request.

Illustration

A listed tyre company buys carbon black from a promoter-group company at ₹96,000 per tonne, 90-day credit, delivered. Evidence: internal CUP — the promoter company sells identical grade to two unrelated tyre makers at ₹94,500 and ₹97,800 (60-day credit, ex-works); external quotes from unrelated suppliers ₹95,000–₹99,000 delivered, 45-day credit. Adjustment: 30 extra days of credit at 9% cost of funds ≈ 0.74% of price (≈ ₹700 per tonne); freight ≈ ₹1,200 per tonne for ex-works quotes. Adjusted internal CUP range ≈ ₹96,400–₹99,700. The transaction price of ₹96,000 sits just below the adjusted range — favourable to the listed company, so it passes the arm's-length test from the listed company's perspective (the supplier's tax position is a separate question). Document and present to the audit committee.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Favourable to the company is not automatically fine. A price below arm's length paid to a related supplier may still raise issues for the supplier (tax, minority shareholders of the supplier, Sec 188 for the supplier).
  • Loans and guarantees. Interest rate benchmarks (SBI MCLR plus spread, comparable bond yields) and guarantee fee benchmarks (bank guarantee commission rates) are CUPs; interest-free loans to subsidiaries are not at arm's length but may be exempt under Sec 186 and fall within the Ind AS 109 fair-value accounting.
  • Cost allocations within groups. Shared-service charges need a documented allocation key and evidence of benefit.
  • Asset transfers. Use registered valuer reports (Sec 247) as "other method" evidence.
  • TP adjustments and accounting. A TP adjustment affects tax (Ind AS 12, Appendix C uncertainty), not the related-party disclosure amount, which reflects actual transaction values.

Key takeaways

  • Three lenses — Sec 188 (ordinary course and arm's length), Ind AS 24.23 (substantiated statement), Income-tax TP (ALP within range).
  • Sec 92 covers international transactions with AEs; Sec 92BA covers specified domestic transactions (tax-holiday and 115BAB-linked) above ₹20 crore.
  • Six methods: CUP, RPM, CPM, PSM, TNMM and other method; range 35th–65th percentile with six or more comparables, otherwise arithmetic mean ± tolerance.
  • Ordinary course is a facts-based test: MoA objects, frequency, normal business pattern, revenue nature.
  • Build an evidence file per material RPT, refresh annually, and present it with audit-committee approval requests.

Practice Questions

Q1. Choose the method [L1]

For each, recommend the most appropriate TP method and the comparables you would seek: (a) software development services by an Indian captive to its US parent; (b) purchase of finished goods from a Chinese AE for resale in India; (c) a ₹200 crore loan from the Indian parent to its Singapore subsidiary; (d) a corporate guarantee by the Indian parent for its UK subsidiary's bank loan; (e) brand royalty paid to the foreign parent.

Q2. Arm's length range [L2]

An IT captive earns a mark-up on total costs of 14.2%. Eight comparables show mark-ups (current year) of 9.5%, 11.8%, 12.4%, 14.9%, 15.6%, 16.3%, 18.0%, 21.4%. Required: compute the 35th and 65th percentile range per Rule 10CA (using the statutory percentile method), state whether the transaction is at arm's length and, if not, the adjustment on a cost base of ₹420 crore.

💡 Hint: Rule 10CA defines the percentile position by rank, rounding up if not a whole number — apply the rule rather than Excel's PERCENTILE function.

Q3. Domestic RPT evidence without TP [L2]

A listed FMCG company (no tax-holiday units) leases a warehouse from the promoter's LLP at ₹48 lakh a year. Required: explain whether Sec 92BA applies, and design the arm's-length evidence for the audit committee and Sec 188 purposes.

Q4. Ordinary course challenge [L3]

A listed engineering company's audit committee is asked to approve, as "ordinary course and arm's length", the purchase of a commercial building from the promoter for ₹140 crore based on a registered valuer's report of ₹142 crore. The company has never bought commercial property before. Required: evaluate the ordinary course and arm's-length claims, state the approvals required under Sec 188 and Reg 23, and list the additional evidence you would request.
