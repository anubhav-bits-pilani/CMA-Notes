Key concept

Substance Over Form and Parent–Subsidiary Disclosure

Concept: Substance over legal form (24.10); parent–subsidiary relationships disclosed even with no transactions (24.13)

Statement

  1. Substance over form (Ind AS 24.10). In considering each possible related-party relationship, attention is directed to the substance of the relationship and not merely the legal form.
  2. Relationships regardless of transactions (Ind AS 24.13). Relationships between a parent and its subsidiaries shall be disclosed irrespective of whether there have been transactions between them. An entity discloses the name of its parent and, if different, the ultimate controlling party. If neither the parent nor the ultimate controlling party produces consolidated financial statements available for public use, the name of the next most senior parent that does so is also disclosed.

Explanation

Why substance. Related-party relationships are often deliberately structured to sit just outside a legal definition: a company held 49% by the promoter and 2% by his driver, a trust whose trustees are the promoter's employees, a "consultant" who directs the Board, or a supplier funded entirely by the promoter through a third party. Ind AS 24's definitions use control, joint control and significant influence (themselves substance-based under Ind AS 110, 111 and 28), and 24.10 instructs preparers and auditors to look through nominee arrangements, side agreements and informal influence.

Substance cuts both ways: an entity that is formally within a definition but over which there is no actual ability to influence may still be a related party if the definition is met (definitions are not rebuttable merely by assertion), whereas 24.11 lists relationships that are not related merely by their form (common directors, providers of finance, economic dependence).

Indicators that substance may differ from form:

  • shareholders or directors who are employees, relatives or associates of the promoter;
  • financing of a counterparty's equity by the entity or its promoter;
  • the counterparty's address, email domain or bank signatories match those of the group;
  • the counterparty was incorporated shortly before transacting and depends on the entity for most revenue;
  • unusual terms (no security, deferred payment, prices away from market);
  • funds moving onward from the counterparty to promoter entities.

Why disclose parent–subsidiary relationships without transactions. Control relationships affect an entity's position and results even without transactions — through the parent's influence over decisions, the possibility of transactions not on arm's-length terms, or the withholding of transactions (for example, a parent directing a subsidiary not to compete) (24.6–8). Users need to know who controls the entity to interpret its financial statements, its dividend policy and its access to group support. The "next most senior parent" rule ensures users can locate public consolidated statements.

The 24.13 requirement applies to parent–subsidiary relationships; for other related parties (associates, KMP, other entities), disclosure is triggered by transactions or balances (24.18), though KMP compensation is always disclosed (24.17).

Illustration

Narmada Agro Ltd buys 70% of its fertiliser from Swastik Trading Pvt Ltd. Swastik's shareholders are two employees of Narmada's promoter group; its funding came from an unsecured loan from a promoter LLP; its registered office is the promoter's family office; its dealings with Narmada carry 180-day credit when market terms are 45 days.

Although Swastik is not legally controlled by the promoter, the substance indicates the promoter controls it (power through nominee shareholders and funding, exposure to returns, ability to direct). Under 24.9(b)(6), Swastik is a related party of Narmada (entity controlled by a person who controls Narmada). Transactions, balances and terms must be disclosed; Sec 188 and Reg 23 approvals must be considered.

Separately, Narmada's dormant 100% subsidiary Narmada Exports Pte Ltd (no transactions in FY26) is still disclosed as a subsidiary in the relationships section.

Connections

Applied throughout s01 (definition) and s03 (group mapping); the disclosure requirement is part of s07. The substance approach links to Week 3 Topic 5 (control and significant influence), to the topic pitfall on relying only on Companies Act definitions, and to forensic accounting work on fund diversion through undisclosed related parties (a recurring theme in SEBI orders).

Practice Questions

Q1. Look-through assessment [L2]

A listed hotel company pays ₹22 crore a year for housekeeping to a firm whose two partners are the chauffeur and the personal assistant of the company's chairman-promoter. The firm has no other clients and its bank account receives a monthly transfer from the promoter's family office. Required: assess whether the firm is a related party under Ind AS 24, citing 24.10, and list the procedures an auditor would perform.

💡 Hint: Frame the analysis using the Ind AS 110 elements of control applied to the firm.

Q2. Dormant subsidiary and ultimate controlling party [L1]

A company is 100% owned by an unlisted Indian holding company that does not prepare consolidated financial statements (exempted), which in turn is 80% owned by a Mauritius entity that also does not publish consolidated statements; the Mauritius entity is owned by a listed Japanese parent that publishes consolidated IFRS statements. The company has a dormant subsidiary with no transactions. Required: draft the relationship disclosures under 24.13 and 24.16.

Q3. Form looks related, substance does not [L2]

Two listed companies share an independent director. One sells ₹40 crore of goods a year to the other on standard terms. Required: explain under 24.11 whether they are related parties and whether the conclusion would change if the shared director were the controlling promoter of one of them.
