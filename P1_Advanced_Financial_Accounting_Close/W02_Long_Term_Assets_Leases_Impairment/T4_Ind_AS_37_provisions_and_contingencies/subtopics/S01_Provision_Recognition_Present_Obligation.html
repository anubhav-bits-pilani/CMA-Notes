Subtopic note

Provision Recognition: Present Obligation, Probability, Estimate

Scope: Recognition (37.14): present legal/constructive obligation from past event, probable outflow, reliable estimate

Why it matters

Recognition is the gate that decides whether an exposure sits on the balance sheet, in a note, or nowhere. It is the first question in every provisions memo, every audit committee discussion of litigation and every "exceptional items" debate in quarterly results. In FP&A, recognition timing determines which quarter absorbs a cost; in audit, premature or delayed recognition is a classic earnings-management flag. Under Ind AS, the threshold is more permissive than US GAAP (more likely than not rather than likely), so Indian entities typically recognise more litigation provisions than their US-reporting peers.

Core notes

Definitions (Ind AS 37.10)

  • Provision: a liability of uncertain timing or amount.
  • Liability: a present obligation arising from past events, the settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits.
  • Obligating event: an event that creates a legal or constructive obligation that leaves the entity with no realistic alternative to settling it.
  • Legal obligation: derives from a contract (explicit or implicit terms), legislation or other operation of law.
  • Constructive obligation: derives from the entity's own actions where, by an established pattern of past practice, published policies or a sufficiently specific current statement, the entity has indicated to others that it will accept certain responsibilities, and has thereby created a valid expectation on their part that it will discharge them.

Provisions differ from trade payables and accruals (37.11) by the degree of uncertainty: an accrual for unbilled electricity is a liability with little uncertainty, normally reported within trade or other payables; a provision for a lawsuit carries real uncertainty in amount or timing.

The three recognition criteria (37.14)

A provision is recognised when all of the following are met:

  1. The entity has a present obligation (legal or constructive) as a result of a past event.
  2. It is probable that an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits will be required to settle the obligation.
  3. A reliable estimate can be made of the amount of the obligation.

If any criterion fails, no provision is recognised; the item is then assessed as a contingent liability (37.27–30) unless the possibility of outflow is remote.

Criterion 1: present obligation from a past event

Present obligation (37.15–16). In almost all cases it is clear. Where it is not — typically a lawsuit where it is disputed whether events occurred or whether they create an obligation — a past event is deemed to give rise to a present obligation if, taking account of all available evidence (including expert opinion and events after the reporting period), it is more likely than not that a present obligation exists at the reporting date.

Past event (37.17–22). The obligating event must leave no realistic alternative but to settle, either because settlement is enforceable by law or because a constructive obligation has created valid expectations. Key rules:

  • Only obligations arising from past events existing independently of the entity's future actions are provided (37.19). Penalties or clean-up costs for unlawful environmental damage already caused are provided; the cost of fitting smoke filters required by law from next year is not, because the entity could avoid it by changing its operations (for example, closing the plant).
  • An obligation always involves another party, though the identity need not be known — it may be the public at large (37.20).
  • A management or Board decision does not by itself create a constructive obligation at the reporting date unless it has been communicated in a sufficiently specific manner before that date to those affected (37.20).
  • An event that does not give rise to an obligation immediately may do so later because of changes in law or because an act by the entity creates a constructive obligation (37.21). Where details of new legislation are still being finalised, an obligation arises only when the legislation is virtually certain to be enacted as drafted (37.22).

Legal vs constructive in Indian practice. Legal: environmental penalties under the Environment (Protection) Act, statutory warranty under the Consumer Protection Act 2019, contractual warranties, liquidated damages in EPC contracts. Constructive: a retailer's published "no-questions 30-day refund" policy beyond statutory requirement; a company's widely publicised policy of cleaning up contamination in states where law does not require it (Ind AS 37 Illustrative Example 2B); a long-established practice of paying performance-linked incentives to dealers.

Criterion 2: probable outflow

Probable means more likely than not — the probability that the event will occur is greater than the probability that it will not (37.23). Where there are many similar obligations (warranties), the probability is assessed for the class as a whole (37.24): even if the probability of outflow on any one product is small, an outflow for the class may well be probable. See concept c01 for the full threshold ladder.

Criterion 3: reliable estimate

The use of estimates is essential and does not undermine reliability (37.25). Except in extremely rare cases, an entity can determine a range of possible outcomes and make a sufficiently reliable estimate. In the extremely rare case where no reliable estimate is possible, a liability exists that cannot be recognised; it is disclosed as a contingent liability (37.26). Auditors challenge any claim of "no reliable estimate" hard — it is rarely accepted for routine disputes.

Scope boundaries to check before applying 37.14

Ind AS 37 does not apply to executory contracts (unless onerous), financial instruments within Ind AS 109 (including most financial guarantees), and items covered by other standards: income taxes (Ind AS 12, including Appendix C), employee benefits (Ind AS 19), leases (Ind AS 116, except leases that become onerous before commencement and short-term/low-value leases accounted under the exemption), insurance contracts (Ind AS 117) and revenue contracts (Ind AS 115) except where onerous (37.1–5). Levies imposed by government are recognised when the activity that triggers payment under legislation occurs (Ind AS 37 Appendix C, the Indian equivalent of IFRIC 21).

Illustration

A consumer-durables company received a complaint in January 2026 alleging that a batch of water purifiers caused property damage. At 31 March 2026 counsel advises that, on the evidence, the company is more likely than not to be found liable, and that damages would be about ₹1.8 crore. The obligating event is the sale of defective products (past). Outflow is probable (counsel > 50%). A reliable estimate exists. Recognise a provision of ₹1.8 crore (subject to measurement and discounting in s02–s03). Had counsel's view been 30%, the company would disclose a contingent liability; at 3%, nothing.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Events after the reporting period. A court judgment in May 2026 on a claim pending at 31 March 2026 is an adjusting event under Ind AS 10 — it gives evidence of the obligation at the reporting date — and updates both recognition and measurement.
  • Licence conditions and regulatory directions. An NGT or pollution control board order directing restoration creates a legal obligation from the date of the order; a draft notification does not, unless virtually certain to be enacted.
  • Self-insurance. No provision for uninsured future accidents; only for incidents that have occurred (incurred-but-not-reported claims are provided).
  • Staggered obligations. An airline's obligation to overhaul leased aircraft arises as flying hours accumulate under lease return conditions, not at the overhaul date; owned-aircraft overhauls are not provided at all (componentised under Ind AS 16 instead).
  • Constructive obligation from past practice. Repeated ex-gratia payments to retiring staff can create a constructive obligation; the bar is whether employees have a valid expectation, not whether the Board minutes call it discretionary.

Key takeaways

  • All three criteria of 37.14 must be met; failing any one diverts the item to contingent-liability analysis.
  • The obligating event must be past and independent of the entity's future conduct — avoidable future costs are never provided.
  • Constructive obligations need communication to the affected parties before the reporting date.
  • "Probable" in Ind AS 37 means more likely than not, assessed for a class where obligations are similar.
  • Inability to estimate reliably is an extremely rare exception, not a routine escape.
  • Check scope first: income taxes, employee benefits, leases, financial guarantees and revenue contracts have their own standards.

Practice Questions

Q1. Smoke filters and penalties [L1]

A cement company must, under a notified regulation, install emission filters by 30 September 2026 at a cost of ₹14 crore. At 31 March 2026 it has not installed them and has continued operating in breach of an earlier emission norm since January 2026, attracting a penalty of ₹25,000 per day under the regulation. Required: state whether a provision is required at 31 March 2026 for (a) the filter cost and (b) the penalty, and quantify (b) assuming the penalty is probable.

💡 Hint: Ask whether each cost is avoidable by the entity's future conduct.

Q2. Constructive obligation in a retail chain [L2]

A fashion retailer with 180 stores has, for six years, publicly displayed and honoured a policy of accepting returns within 45 days even though law requires none. FY26 sales in March were ₹42 crore; historically 3.5% of March sales are returned in April–May, with resale value recovering 60% of the refunded amount. Required: (a) identify the obligating event and type of obligation; (b) explain how this interacts with Ind AS 115's refund liability and right-of-return asset; (c) state which standard governs measurement.

Q3. Draft legislation and contamination [L2]

A mining company in a state with no clean-up law has contaminated land. In February 2026 the state assembly passed a bill requiring clean-up of historical contamination; Governor's assent is expected in May 2026. Estimated clean-up cost is ₹9 crore. The company has no public policy on clean-up. Required: evaluate the recognition position at 31 March 2026 and explain what evidence an auditor would expect to see.

Q4. Board-approved closure not yet communicated [L2]

On 28 March 2026 the Board of a textile company approved closure of a spinning unit, with severance estimated at ₹3.6 crore. The plan was communicated to employees on 10 April 2026, and the financial statements were approved on 20 May 2026. Required: (a) is a provision recognised at 31 March 2026? (b) what disclosure is required under Ind AS 10? (c) how would your answer change if a supplier contract had been terminated, with a penalty, on 30 March 2026?
