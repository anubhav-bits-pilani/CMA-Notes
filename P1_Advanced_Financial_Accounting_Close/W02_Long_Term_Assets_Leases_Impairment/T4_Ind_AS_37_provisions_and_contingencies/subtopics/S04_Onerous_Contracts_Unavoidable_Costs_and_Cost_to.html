Subtopic note

Onerous Contracts: Unavoidable Costs and Cost to Fulfil

Scope: Onerous contracts (37.66–69): unavoidable cost = lower of cost to fulfil and exit penalty; cost to fulfil = incremental + allocated directly related costs

Why it matters

Commodity spikes, fixed-price EPC bids, take-or-pay purchase agreements, long-term power purchase agreements and fixed-tariff service contracts can all turn loss-making. When they do, Ind AS 37 forces the entire expected loss into the current period rather than letting it bleed through future margins. The 2022 amendment (effective in India for periods beginning on or after 1 April 2022) settled a long-running debate on which costs count, and Indian EPC, telecom and power companies changed their provisions as a result. For FP&A, onerous-contract tests are part of every quarterly contract review; for valuation, a recognised provision is a debt-like item.

Core notes

Definitions and the core rule (37.66–68)

If an entity has a contract that is onerous, the present obligation under the contract is recognised and measured as a provision (37.66). Executory contracts — where neither party has performed, or both have partially performed to an equal extent — are outside Ind AS 37 unless onerous (37.67, 37.3).

An onerous contract is a contract in which the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations under the contract exceed the economic benefits expected to be received under it (37.10).

The unavoidable costs reflect the least net cost of exiting from the contract (37.68):

\[ \text{Unavoidable cost} = \min\left(\text{Cost of fulfilling the contract},\ \text{Compensation or penalties from failure to fulfil}\right) \]

\[ \text{Onerous provision} = \text{Unavoidable cost} - \text{Economic benefits expected under the contract} \]

(The provision is recognised only if the result is positive.)

Cost of fulfilling a contract (37.68A)

The 2022 amendment clarified that the cost of fulfilling comprises costs that relate directly to the contract:

  1. Incremental costs of fulfilling that contract — for example, direct labour and materials; and
  2. An allocation of other costs that relate directly to fulfilling contracts — for example, an allocation of the depreciation charge for an item of PPE used in fulfilling that contract (among others).

General and administrative costs that do not relate directly to contracts are excluded unless explicitly chargeable to the counterparty under the contract. Before the amendment, some entities used an "incremental cost only" approach, which produced lower provisions; that approach is no longer acceptable.

Cost Include in cost to fulfil?
Direct materials, direct labour, sub-contract cost Yes — incremental
Fuel and power consumed on the contract Yes — incremental
Depreciation of machinery used on the contract Yes — allocated directly related
Site supervision and contract management staff Yes — allocated directly related
Insurance of contract assets Yes — allocated directly related
Corporate head-office costs, general marketing No (unless chargeable to customer)
Finance costs of general borrowings No

Economic benefits expected

Expected benefits include the remaining contract price and any other benefits directly arising, for example the sale of by-products or recoveries from the counterparty. They do not include the general benefits of maintaining a customer relationship.

Impairment first (37.69)

Before a separate provision for an onerous contract is established, the entity recognises any impairment loss that has occurred on assets dedicated to that contract under Ind AS 36 (concept c05). The provision covers only the residual excess of unavoidable cost over benefits after the asset has been written down. This avoids double-counting the same loss — once as impairment, once as provision — because depreciation of the dedicated asset is part of the cost to fulfil.

Interaction with Ind AS 115 and Ind AS 116

  • For contracts with customers, Ind AS 115 contains no onerous-contract test; the Ind AS 37 test applies to the whole contract (as explained in the IFRS 15 Basis for Conclusions). Contract assets and capitalised contract costs under Ind AS 115.91–104 are tested for impairment first.
  • After Ind AS 116, most leases are on the balance sheet: a lease that becomes onerous is addressed by impairing the ROU asset (Ind AS 36). Ind AS 37 continues to apply to leases that become onerous before commencement and to short-term or low-value leases expensed under the exemption, and to non-lease service components of a lease contract.

Worked illustration

A packaging company has a fixed-price contract to supply 50 lakh cartons over the next 12 months at ₹18 per carton. Kraft paper prices have risen; the company estimates per-carton costs as follows: paper and ink ₹15.20; direct labour ₹1.80; power ₹0.90; depreciation of the dedicated corrugator line ₹1.10; plant supervision ₹0.40; head-office allocation ₹0.70. Cancellation penalty: ₹1.20 crore.

Cost to fulfil per carton (excluding head-office) = 15.20 + 1.80 + 0.90 + 1.10 + 0.40 = ₹19.40. Total = 50,00,000 × 19.40 = ₹9.70 crore. Benefits = 50,00,000 × 18 = ₹9.00 crore. Net cost to fulfil = ₹0.70 crore. Exit cost = ₹1.20 crore. Unavoidable cost is the lower net route: fulfilling (net ₹0.70 crore).

The dedicated corrugator line (carrying amount ₹0.80 crore, entire remaining life consumed by the contract, no alternative use) is tested for impairment first: its cash flows from the contract are revenue ₹9.00 crore less other costs ₹9.15 crore (excluding depreciation), i.e. negative, so recoverable amount is nil (assuming scrap value nil) and impairment of ₹0.80 crore is recognised. With the asset written down, depreciation ₹0.55 crore (₹1.10 × 50 lakh) falls away from future cost; residual onerous provision = 9.70 − 0.55 − 9.00 = ₹0.15 crore.

Entry Dr (₹ crore) Cr (₹ crore)
Impairment loss (P&L) / Accumulated impairment — corrugator line 0.80 0.80
Other expenses — onerous contract / Provision for onerous contract 0.15 0.15

Total P&L hit ₹0.95 crore, against a contract loss of ₹0.70 crore on a full-cost basis plus the write-off of the line's carrying amount in excess of the depreciation the contract absorbs (0.80 − 0.55 = 0.25). (Verify: 0.70 + 0.25 = 0.95.)

Subsequent measurement

The provision is utilised as the loss-making deliveries occur (the costs are charged against it), unwound if discounted, and remeasured at each reporting date for changes in input prices, volumes and penalties. Release excess amounts to P&L and disclose them separately in the roll-forward.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Contract boundary. Assess the contract as a whole; do not cherry-pick loss-making call-offs within a framework agreement unless they are separate contracts.
  • Exit route realism. The penalty route counts only if the entity can legally exit; a penalty clause that does not release the entity from performance is not an exit.
  • Take-or-pay purchase contracts. Where purchased inputs can be resold at market, compare the contract price with market value; the loss is the excess of contract cost over the benefit (resale value or use value).
  • Portfolio contracts. Ind AS 37 is applied contract by contract; one profitable and one onerous contract with the same customer are not netted unless they are combined as a single contract.
  • Future operating losses vs onerous contract. A loss-making business unit does not have an onerous contract merely because it loses money; a specific contract with unavoidable costs above benefits is required.

Key takeaways

  • Onerous = unavoidable costs exceed expected economic benefits; the unavoidable cost is the lower of net cost to fulfil and exit compensation.
  • Cost to fulfil includes incremental costs plus an allocation of other directly related costs (37.68A, 2022 amendment); exclude general overheads.
  • Test dedicated assets for impairment first (37.69), then provide only the residual.
  • Ind AS 115 contracts and pre-commencement or exempt leases use this test; on-balance-sheet leases use ROU impairment.
  • Remeasure, utilise and release separately in the roll-forward.

Practice Questions

Q1. Fixed-price EPC contract turned loss-making [L2]

An EPC contractor has a remaining fixed price of ₹240 crore on a substation contract. Remaining costs: materials ₹168 crore, sub-contract labour ₹52 crore, depreciation of cranes and equipment used on site ₹9 crore, project-office staff ₹11 crore, allocated corporate costs ₹7 crore. The contract allows termination by the contractor on payment of ₹30 crore. Required: (a) compute the cost to fulfil and the unavoidable cost; (b) determine whether a provision is required and its amount; (c) explain how the answer would have differed under an incremental-cost-only approach.

Q2. Take-or-pay gas purchase [L2]

A fertiliser company is committed to buy 3 mmscmd of gas for 2 more years at ₹1,050 per mmBtu-equivalent unit. Its urea plant has been shut indefinitely after a subsidy change. The gas can be resold at ₹880 per unit, with transport costs of ₹25 per unit. Annual contracted volume is 40 lakh units. Required: compute the onerous provision at 31 March 2026 (ignore discounting), and state the impairment analysis required on the urea plant first.

💡 Hint: Compare the cost of the contract with the best available benefit from the contracted gas, not with what the shut plant would have earned.

Q3. Leased warehouse vacated [L2]

A logistics company vacated a leased warehouse in January 2026 and cannot sub-let it. The lease is on balance sheet under Ind AS 116 (ROU asset ₹6.2 crore; lease liability ₹6.8 crore). The landlord separately bills a fixed maintenance charge of ₹40 lakh a year for the remaining 4 years, which is a non-lease component. Required: explain which elements are dealt with under Ind AS 36 and which under Ind AS 37, and compute the Ind AS 37 provision (discount at 8% pre-tax).

Q4. Interview: why impairment comes first [L1]

Explain to a non-accountant board member why Ind AS 37.69 requires dedicated assets to be impaired before an onerous provision is recognised, and what would go wrong in the financial statements if the order were reversed.
