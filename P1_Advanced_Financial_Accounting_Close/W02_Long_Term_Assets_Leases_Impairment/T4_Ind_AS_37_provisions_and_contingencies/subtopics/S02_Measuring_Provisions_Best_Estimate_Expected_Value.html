Subtopic note

Measuring Provisions: Best Estimate, Expected Value, Most Likely

Scope: Measurement: best estimate (37.36–40), expected value for large populations, most likely outcome for single obligations, risk adjustment

Why it matters

Once recognition is settled, the number is what auditors, analysts and tax officers argue about. Measurement choices drive the quantum of exceptional items, the reversal pattern in later years and the credibility of management estimates. In FP&A, warranty and claims provisions are modelled as rates on volume; in audit, the retrospective review of prior-year estimates against actual settlements (required by SA 540) tests whether the methodology is biased. Knowing when to use expected value versus most likely outcome, and how to reflect risk without creating "cookie-jar" reserves, is core to defending a provision.

Core notes

The best estimate (37.36–37)

The amount recognised as a provision is the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the present obligation at the end of the reporting period (37.36). It is the amount the entity would rationally pay to settle the obligation at the reporting date, or to transfer it to a third party at that time (37.37). Settlement or transfer may be impossible or prohibitively expensive in practice, but the rational-payment notion remains the measurement objective.

Estimates are determined by management judgement, supplemented by experience of similar transactions and, in some cases, independent experts' reports (37.38). The evidence includes events after the reporting period that provide additional evidence (Ind AS 10 adjusting events).

Large populations: expected value (37.39)

Where the provision involves a large population of items, the obligation is estimated by weighting all possible outcomes by their associated probabilities — the expected value:

\[ \text{Provision} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \times C_i \]

where \(p_i\) is the probability of outcome \(i\), \(C_i\) is the cost of outcome \(i\), and \(k\) is the number of outcomes. The provision will therefore differ depending on whether the probability of a loss of a given amount is, say, 60% or 90%.

Illustration (adapted from 37.39). A retailer sells goods with a warranty. If all goods sold in the year had minor defects, repair costs would be ₹1.0 crore; if all had major defects, ₹4.0 crore. Experience: 75% of goods have no defects, 20% minor, 5% major.

\[ \text{Expected value} = 0.75 \times 0 + 0.20 \times 1.0 + 0.05 \times 4.0 = \text{₹}0.40 \text{ crore} \]

Where there is a continuous range of possible outcomes and each point in that range is as likely as any other, the midpoint of the range is used (37.39; concept c02).

Single obligations: most likely outcome (37.40)

Where a single obligation is being measured, the individual most likely outcome may be the best estimate. However, the entity must still consider other possible outcomes: where other outcomes are mostly higher or mostly lower than the most likely outcome, the best estimate will be a higher or lower amount. Ind AS 37.40's example: an entity must rectify a serious fault in a major plant constructed for a customer; the most likely outcome may be that the repair succeeds at the first attempt for ₹1,000, but a provision is made for a larger amount if there is a significant chance that further attempts will be necessary.

Practical approach for a single lawsuit:

Outcome Probability Payout (₹ crore)
Settle out of court 50% 6.0
Lose at trial 30% 11.0
Win 20% 0 (legal costs only, separately accrued)

The most likely outcome is ₹6.0 crore; but the other outcome with an outflow is higher, so the best estimate is skewed upward from ₹6.0 crore. Expected value (₹6.3 crore) is not mandated for a single obligation; it is one input into the judgement. Document why the chosen number is the best estimate.

Risks and uncertainties (37.42–44)

The risks and uncertainties that inevitably surround events are taken into account in reaching the best estimate (37.42). Risk describes variability of outcome; a risk adjustment may increase the amount at which a liability is measured. Caution is needed in making judgements under uncertainty so that expenses or liabilities are not understated, but uncertainty does not justify excessive provisions or deliberate overstatement (37.43). Care is required to avoid double-counting risk — for example, by using a pessimistic cash flow and also a risk-adjusted discount rate (37.47, see s03). Uncertainties about the amount are disclosed (37.44, 37.85(b)).

Two ways to reflect risk, never both for the same risk:

  1. Adjust the cash flows (for example, a higher percentile, or a margin) and discount at a risk-free pre-tax rate.
  2. Use best-estimate cash flows and a discount rate that includes a liability-specific risk premium.

Other measurement rules

  • Pre-tax: provisions are measured before tax; tax consequences are dealt with under Ind AS 12 (37.41).
  • Future events (37.48–50): expected future events that may affect the amount — for example, technology changes that reduce clean-up cost — are reflected where there is sufficient objective evidence they will occur. New legislation is taken into account only when virtually certain to be enacted.
  • Expected disposals (37.51–52): gains from the expected disposal of assets are not taken into account, even if the disposal is closely linked to the event giving rise to the provision.
  • Review at each reporting date (37.59): provisions are reviewed and adjusted to reflect the current best estimate; if an outflow is no longer probable, the provision is reversed.
  • Incremental costs: the estimate includes external costs (lawyers, contractors) and, where they are incremental, internal costs; it excludes general overheads that would be incurred anyway.

Modelling it

In a provisions workbook, keep a separate assumptions block for each class: volume driver, rate, unit cost, probability, timing. For expected-value classes use SUMPRODUCT over the probability and cost columns:

=SUMPRODUCT(Prob_Range, Cost_Range)

For single obligations, show the scenario table, the most likely outcome, and an explicit "adjustment for skew" line with rationale so that the reviewer sees the judgement rather than a buried number.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Group of single obligations. Fifty separate labour disputes with similar facts can be treated as a population (expected value); one high-value arbitration cannot.
  • Bimodal outcomes. Where the outcome is either nil or ₹50 crore (win/lose), expected value at 60% (₹30 crore) is an amount that will never be paid; Ind AS 37.40 points to the most likely outcome (₹50 crore) adjusted for the other outcome — in practice many preparers recognise ₹50 crore and disclose the uncertainty.
  • Legal costs. Whether future legal defence costs are included depends on policy and whether they are part of the obligation; many entities accrue them only as incurred because they arise from future services. Apply consistently.
  • Prior-year hindsight. A material difference between last year's provision and this year's settlement is a change in estimate (Ind AS 8) unless the original estimate ignored information available at the time, in which case it is an error.
  • Risk margin creep. A "general contingency" layered on top of specific provisions is an unidentified obligation and fails recognition.

Key takeaways

  • Measure at the amount the entity would rationally pay to settle or transfer at the reporting date (37.36–37).
  • Large populations → probability-weighted expected value; continuous equally likely range → midpoint.
  • Single obligation → most likely outcome, moved up or down where other outcomes are skewed (37.40).
  • Reflect risk once — in the cash flows or in the discount rate, never both.
  • Measure pre-tax, ignore gains on expected disposals, and reflect only virtually certain legislation.
  • Review every reporting date; uncertainty never licenses excessive provisioning.

Practice Questions

Q1. Expected value for an extended warranty book [L1]

A two-wheeler maker sold 3,60,000 vehicles in FY26. Claims history: 82% no claim, 14% minor claims averaging ₹1,800, 3.5% moderate claims averaging ₹6,500, 0.5% major claims averaging ₹28,000. Required: compute the warranty provision for FY26 sales using expected value and explain why expected value is appropriate here.

Q2. Single arbitration with skewed outcomes [L2]

An infrastructure company faces an arbitration claim. Counsel's assessment: 55% probability of an award of ₹40 crore, 30% probability of ₹70 crore, 15% probability of no award. Required: (a) identify the most likely outcome; (b) compute the expected value; (c) argue for a best estimate under Ind AS 37.40 and draft the disclosure of uncertainty under 37.85.

💡 Hint: Consider whether the outcomes other than the most likely one are predominantly higher or lower.

Q3. Risk adjustment without double-counting [L2]

A company estimates a site-restoration outflow in 6 years. Best-estimate cash flow ₹18 crore; a 90th-percentile cash flow ₹22 crore. The pre-tax risk-free rate is 7.0%; a rate including a liability-specific risk adjustment is 5.9% (note: for a liability, a risk adjustment lowers the discount rate). Required: show the two acceptable measurement approaches and explain why discounting ₹22 crore at 5.9% would be wrong.

Q4. Prior-year estimate settled for less [L2]

At 31 March 2025 a company provided ₹9 crore for a customer claim based on counsel's best estimate. The claim was settled in November 2025 for ₹6.5 crore. Required: (a) the accounting in FY26; (b) whether this is a change in estimate or a prior-period error and the evidence you would examine; (c) how the roll-forward reports the ₹9 crore movement.
