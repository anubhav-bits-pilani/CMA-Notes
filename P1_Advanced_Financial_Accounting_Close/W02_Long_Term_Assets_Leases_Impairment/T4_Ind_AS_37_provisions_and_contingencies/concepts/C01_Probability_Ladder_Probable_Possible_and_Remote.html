Key concept

Probability Ladder: Probable, Possible and Remote Outflows

Concept: Probable = more likely than not (37.23); possible → disclose contingent liability (37.86); remote → no disclosure

Statement

For an obligation arising from a past event, the probability of an outflow of resources determines its accounting:

Assessed likelihood of outflow Accounting
Probable — more likely than not, \(p > 50\%\) (37.23), with a reliable estimate Recognise a provision (37.14)
Possible — not probable but not remote Do not recognise; disclose as a contingent liability (37.86)
Remote No recognition, no disclosure (37.28, 37.86)

\[ \text{Probable} \iff P(\text{outflow}) > P(\text{no outflow}) \iff P(\text{outflow}) > 0.5 \]

Explanation

Ind AS 37.23 defines "probable" for the standard's purposes as "more likely than not to occur", that is, the probability that the event will occur is greater than the probability that it will not. The same threshold is used in 37.15–16 for deciding, in unclear cases such as lawsuits, whether a present obligation exists at all.

"Possible" and "remote" are not numerically defined. In practice "remote" is used where the chance of outflow is slight — many entities and auditors think in terms of single-digit percentages — and "possible" covers the band between remote and more-likely-than-not. The thresholds are applied to the obligation:

  • For similar obligations (warranties, small claims) the probability is assessed for the class as a whole (37.24). Even if each item has a low probability, an outflow for the class is probable, so a provision is made.
  • For a single obligation, the probability relates to that obligation.

Two further routes lead to contingent-liability disclosure even for a present obligation: an outflow that is not probable, and the extremely rare case where the amount cannot be measured reliably (37.26). Assessments are continual: a contingent liability that becomes probable is provided in the period of change (37.30), and the change is a change in estimate, not an error, unless information was ignored.

Probability of outflow is distinct from the measurement of the amount. Once the probable threshold is crossed, the measurement is the best estimate (37.36–40), not "probability × amount" for a single obligation. A 60% chance of losing a ₹10 crore claim produces a provision near ₹10 crore if that is the most likely outcome, not ₹6 crore.

Ind AS vs US GAAP: under ASC 450 "probable" means "likely to occur", generally a higher bar, so a matter assessed at 55–70% may be accrued under Ind AS but only disclosed under US GAAP.

Illustration

A pharmaceutical company has three matters at 31 March 2026:

Matter Counsel's assessment Amount (₹ crore) Accounting
Customer claim for contaminated batch 70% likely to lose; best estimate ₹4.2 crore 6.0 claimed Provision ₹4.2 crore; disclose the excess only if a further outflow is possible
Drug-pricing authority overcharging demand 35% likely to be upheld 18.0 Contingent liability disclosed
Ex-employee wrongful dismissal claim Claim filed after limitation; 2% chance 0.5 Nothing (remote)

Connections

This threshold drives s01 (recognition) and s06 (contingent liabilities and the Schedule III note). It pairs with c04, which sets the higher "virtually certain" threshold for contingent assets, creating deliberate asymmetry between losses and gains. In FP&A and valuation, a probability-weighted view of contingent liabilities is often deducted as a debt-like item in equity bridges even where not provided. The same "probable" language appears in Ind AS 12 Appendix C for income-tax positions (Week 3) but is applied to whether the tax authority will accept a treatment.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify five matters [L1]

Classify each as provision, contingent liability or no disclosure, giving reasons: (a) a ₹2 crore warranty book with 0.4% per-unit claim probability across 5 lakh units; (b) a ₹15 crore competition-law penalty assessed at 45% likely; (c) a ₹0.3 crore consumer claim assessed at 80% likely with best estimate ₹0.2 crore; (d) a ₹50 crore claim assessed at 3%; (e) a claim where counsel says liability is likely but damages cannot yet be quantified within any range.

Q2. Change in assessment after year-end [L2]

At 31 December 2025 (Q3) a GST demand of ₹7 crore was disclosed as a contingent liability. In March 2026 the Appellate Authority upheld the demand; the company plans to appeal to the Tribunal, and counsel now rates the chance of success at 30%. Required: state the treatment at 31 March 2026, the P&L presentation and the disclosure of the change.

💡 Hint: Consider whether the event is an adjusting event and whether the change is an error or a change in estimate.

Q3. Interview: why measurement is not probability × amount [L2]

An interviewer asks: "If there is a 60% chance of losing a ₹10 crore lawsuit, why not provide ₹6 crore?" Answer using Ind AS 37.23, 37.39 and 37.40 and explain when a probability-weighted amount would be appropriate.
