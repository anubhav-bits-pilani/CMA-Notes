Replacement Parts and Derecognition of Replaced Carrying Amount

Scope: Replacement parts (Ind AS 16.13) and derecognition of replaced carrying amount

Why it matters

Replacement of significant parts — kiln linings, engines, roofs, turbine blades, lifts, transformer cores, batteries — is a constant feature of Indian manufacturing and infrastructure. Two errors are common: replacements are expensed as repairs (understating assets, overstating current-year expense), or they are capitalised without writing off the replaced part (double-counting assets and overstating depreciation thereafter). Both distort the fixed-asset register and asset-intensity ratios. Auditors examine large repair ledgers and capital additions in mature plants for exactly these issues. In FP&A, replacement capex modelling depends on a clean component register.

Core notes

The rule

Parts of some items of PPE may require replacement at regular intervals — a furnace may require relining after a specified number of hours of use; aircraft interiors such as seats and galleys may require replacement several times during the airframe's life. Items may also be acquired to make a less frequently recurring replacement, such as replacing the interior walls of a building, or a non-recurring replacement. Under the recognition principle, an entity recognises in the carrying amount of an item of PPE the cost of replacing part of such an item when that cost is incurred if the recognition criteria are met. The carrying amount of the parts that are replaced is derecognised in accordance with the derecognition provisions (Ind AS 16.13).

Derecognition provisions (Ind AS 16.67–72): the carrying amount of an item is derecognised on disposal or when no future economic benefits are expected from use or disposal. If the replaced part was not depreciated separately, the entity may use the cost of the replacement as an indication of what the cost of the replaced part was at the time it was acquired or constructed (Ind AS 16.70).

Distinguishing replacement from repair

Characteristic Capitalise as replacement (16.13) Expense as repair (16.12)
Nature Replaces a significant part or component Labour, consumables, small parts
Benefit Restores/extends life or capacity for a further period Maintains existing condition
Frequency Periodic, large Routine, frequent
Recognition Meets Ind AS 16.7 Fails materiality or future-benefit test
Examples Kiln refractory relining, aircraft engine replacement, new roof Lubrication, minor spares, painting

Note that Ind AS uses one recognition principle for initial and subsequent costs; there is no separate "improvement vs maintenance" test as in old AS 10, which capitalised subsequent expenditure only if it increased future benefits beyond the originally assessed standard of performance.

Mechanics

  1. Identify the replaced part in the fixed-asset register. If it was a separate component, its cost and accumulated depreciation are known.
  2. If not separately recorded, estimate the replaced part's original cost using the replacement cost, deflated to the acquisition date (by a price index or a reasonable rate), and compute accumulated depreciation on it at the parent asset's rate.
  3. Derecognise cost and accumulated depreciation; recognise the net carrying amount (less any scrap proceeds) as a loss in P&L.
  4. Capitalise the replacement cost as a new component with its own life.
  5. Update depreciation of the parent asset going forward (the parent's depreciable base is now lower by the derecognised part).

Worked illustration

Vindhya Cement Ltd's kiln (cost ₹300 crore on 1 April 2020; single unit, life 25 years, nil residual) gets its refractory lining replaced on 1 April 2025 at ₹18 crore. The lining was not separately identified. Cement-industry plant price inflation is 6% p.a. Scrap realised from the old lining: ₹0.4 crore.

Estimated original cost of the lining = \( \frac{18}{1.06^{5}} \) = ₹13.45 crore.

Accumulated depreciation on it to 31 March 2025 = 13.45 × 5 ÷ 25 = ₹2.69 crore.

Carrying amount derecognised = 13.45 − 2.69 = ₹10.76 crore; loss = 10.76 − 0.40 = ₹10.36 crore.

Journal entries (₹ crore):

Account Dr Cr
Accumulated depreciation — kiln 2.69
Bank (scrap) 0.40
Loss on derecognition of PPE 10.36
PPE — kiln 13.45
PPE — kiln lining (new component) 18.00
Bank / Creditors 18.00

Going forward, the kiln's remaining carrying amount is depreciated over its remaining 20 years, and the new lining over its own expected life (say 5 years, ₹3.6 crore per year). The large derecognition loss signals that the lining should have been a component from the start — under a component approach the lining would have been nearly fully depreciated by now.

Using the derecognition proxy carefully

The Ind AS 16.70 proxy is permissive ("may use"). Alternatives include engineering estimates of the original part cost or vendor quotations from the original purchase. The choice should be documented. If the replacement is a technologically superior part with greater capacity, using its full cost as proxy overstates the replaced part's cost; an adjustment for the upgrade element is appropriate.

Presentation and disclosure

  • Derecognised parts appear as "disposals/deductions" in the PPE roll-forward (cost and accumulated depreciation columns) under Schedule III.
  • Gains or losses are in P&L as other income/other expenses, never revenue (Ind AS 16.68).
  • Material write-offs from a component policy change may warrant disclosure as an exceptional item (Ind AS 1.97–98).

Tax interaction

For income-tax, replacement of a part may qualify as current repairs (Sec 31) if it does not bring a new asset into existence — courts (e.g. Ballimal Naval Kishore, SC 1997) focus on whether the expenditure preserves an existing asset. The book derecognition loss is not separately deductible; the tax block continues under Sec 32 unless the asset is sold. This creates temporary differences tracked in the deferred tax computation.

Model implementation

In a fixed-asset model, keep a "derecognition" column in both the gross block and accumulated depreciation roll-forwards. Replacement capex forecasts should reference the component register: replacement year = commissioning year + component life. Write off the old component's residual carrying amount in the replacement year if lives are mis-estimated.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Partial replacement (e.g. 40% of a roof) — derecognise 40% of the roof component's carrying amount.
  • Replacement with insurance recovery — derecognition and insurance compensation are separate events: write off the damaged part, recognise compensation in P&L when receivable (Ind AS 16.65–66), capitalise the replacement.
  • Capital spares used as replacements move from PPE spares to the parent asset (s07); avoid double capitalisation.
  • Replacements within warranty at no cost — derecognise the old part only if it had carrying amount separate from the warranted item; usually no entry.
  • Leased assets: replacements in leased premises are leasehold improvements depreciated over the shorter of life and lease term.
  • Sec 43B or GST implications are irrelevant to the book derecognition but affect the cash cost of the replacement (ITC on parts).

Key takeaways

  • Replacements meeting recognition criteria are capitalised; the replaced part's carrying amount is derecognised to P&L.
  • If the part was not separately depreciated, use the replacement cost as a proxy for its original cost (Ind AS 16.70).
  • Routine repairs and small parts are expensed under Ind AS 16.12.
  • Large derecognition losses usually indicate inadequate componentisation.
  • Derecognition gains/losses are not revenue; they are shown in other income/expenses.

Practice Questions

Q1. Engine replacement on a component basis [L1]

An aircraft engine component (cost ₹40 crore, life 8 years, nil residual) is replaced after 6 years on 1 April 2026 by a new engine costing ₹46 crore. The old engine is sold for ₹4 crore. Pass the entries and compute the FY27 depreciation on the engine component.

Q2. Replacement of an unidentified part [L2]

A thermal plant boiler (cost ₹400 crore on 1 April 2018, life 25 years, nil residual, single unit) has its economiser replaced on 1 April 2026 for ₹28 crore. No component was identified. Inflation in boiler equipment prices is 5% p.a. Estimate the carrying amount to derecognise, pass the entries, and state the revised depreciation for the boiler and the new economiser (life 10 years).

💡 Hint: Deflate the replacement cost to the original acquisition date, then depreciate it at the parent asset's rate for the elapsed years.

Q3. Upgrade element [L2]

A data centre replaces UPS units originally costing ₹6 crore (carrying amount ₹2 crore) with higher-capacity units costing ₹10 crore. Engineers estimate that a like-for-like replacement would cost ₹7 crore. Discuss whether ₹10 crore or ₹7 crore should be the proxy if the original cost were unknown, and pass the entries using known original cost.

Q4. Repairs ledger review [L3]

During the FY26 audit of a steel company, the repairs and maintenance ledger shows ₹140 crore against ₹95 crore last year. Line items include blast furnace relining ₹38 crore, conveyor belt replacements ₹6 crore, routine maintenance contracts ₹70 crore, and roll changes ₹26 crore. Design audit procedures to determine which items should be capitalised under Ind AS 16.13, and describe the adjustments if the relining was expensed.

Q5. Insurance and replacement [L2]

A fire destroys a packaging line component (carrying amount ₹3.5 crore) on 10 January 2026. The insurer admits a claim of ₹4.2 crore on 25 March 2026, paid in April 2026. A replacement costing ₹5 crore is installed on 31 March 2026. Pass the entries for FY26 and explain why the compensation is not netted against the replacement cost.
