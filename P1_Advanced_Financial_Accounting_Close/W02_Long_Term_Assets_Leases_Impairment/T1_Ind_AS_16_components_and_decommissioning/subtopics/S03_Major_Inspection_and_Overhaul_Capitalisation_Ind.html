Subtopic note

Major Inspection and Overhaul Capitalisation (Ind AS 16.14)

Scope: Major inspection/overhaul capitalisation (Ind AS 16.14): aircraft checks, refinery turnarounds, dry-docking; derecognising prior inspection component

Why it matters

Airlines, refiners, shipping companies, power producers and fertiliser plants spend large sums on periodic inspections that are condition-for-continued-operation rather than routine repairs. A refinery turnaround at an Indian PSU refiner can cost several hundred crore; an A320 heavy check costs several crore per aircraft. Whether these are capitalised and depreciated over the cycle or expensed when incurred changes EBITDA volatility, the timing of profits and comparability across peers. Overhaul accounting is a common audit and NFRA focus, and FP&A teams must forecast the overhaul component's depreciation and the periodic capex spike.

Core notes

The rule

A condition of continuing to operate an item of PPE (for example, an aircraft) may be performing regular major inspections for faults, regardless of whether parts are replaced. When each major inspection is performed, its cost is recognised in the carrying amount of the item as a replacement if the recognition criteria are satisfied. Any remaining carrying amount of the cost of the previous inspection (as distinct from physical parts) is derecognised. This occurs regardless of whether the cost of the previous inspection was identified in the transaction in which the item was acquired or constructed. If necessary, the estimated cost of a future similar inspection may be used as an indication of what the cost of the existing inspection component was when the item was acquired or constructed (Ind AS 16.14).

Contrast with day-to-day servicing (Ind AS 16.12): costs of labour, consumables and small parts for routine maintenance ("repairs and maintenance") are recognised in P&L as incurred.

Recognition criteria applied

The inspection is capitalised when (Ind AS 16.7):

  1. it is probable that future economic benefits will flow — the inspection is required for continued operation (regulatory airworthiness, classification society survey, Factories Act/Boiler Act compliance, process safety); and
  2. its cost can be measured reliably.

Examples

Industry Inspection Typical cycle Notes
Aviation C-check / heavy maintenance visit (airframe), engine shop visit C-check 18–24 months; heavy check 6–8 years DGCA airworthiness requirement; leased aircraft under Ind AS 116 have different overlays
Refining/petrochemicals Planned turnaround (shutdown, inspection, catalyst regeneration) 3–5 years Costs include contractor labour, scaffolding, inspection, cleaning
Shipping Dry-docking and special survey 2.5–5 years Classification society requirement (IRS, DNV)
Power Major overhaul of boiler/turbine 4–6 years Plant load factor affects cycle
Fertiliser/chemicals Annual turnaround of ammonia/urea plant 1–2 years Short cycles still qualify if significant

Mechanics: three stages

Stage 1 — Identify the embedded inspection component at acquisition. For a new asset, the price implicitly includes the "first inspection" condition. Estimate it using the expected cost of the next inspection (Ind AS 16.14 proxy) and depreciate it over the period to the first inspection.

Stage 2 — Capitalise each actual inspection. Debit PPE (inspection component) with the actual inspection cost; set its life to the expected interval until the next inspection.

Stage 3 — Derecognise the previous inspection component. Whatever carrying amount remains of the previous component (zero if the inspection occurs exactly on schedule) is written off to P&L as a loss on derecognition (Ind AS 16.67–68). If an inspection happens early, a residual carrying amount remains and must be derecognised.

Worked illustration

Sahyadri Refinery Ltd commissions a crude unit on 1 April 2022 for ₹1,200 crore. Engineers estimate a turnaround every 4 years costing ₹80 crore at current prices. Overall unit life 25 years, nil residual value.

Initial split:

Component Cost (₹ crore) Life Annual depreciation
Embedded turnaround 80 4 years 20.00
Rest of unit 1,120 25 years 44.80

Because of a fault, the turnaround occurs early on 1 April 2025 (after 3 years) at an actual cost of ₹96 crore; the next is expected in 4 years.

Carrying amount of embedded turnaround at 1 April 2025 = 80 − (20 × 3) = ₹20 crore.

Journal entries (₹ crore):

Account Dr Cr
Accumulated depreciation — turnaround (old) 60
Loss on derecognition of PPE (P&L) 20
PPE — turnaround (old) at cost 80
PPE — turnaround 2025 96
Bank / Creditors 96

FY26 depreciation on the new turnaround component = 96 ÷ 4 = ₹24 crore. The loss of ₹20 crore is presented in other expenses (not as a reduction of revenue), per Ind AS 16.68.

Accounting when inspections are not identified at acquisition

If the company acquired the unit without identifying the turnaround component, Ind AS 16.14 still requires derecognition of the implied previous inspection carrying amount. Practice: estimate the original component using the cost of the current inspection, discounted back or deflated by an index, compute its accumulated depreciation over the elapsed period, and derecognise that net amount from the single-unit asset. This keeps the asset from carrying two inspection components simultaneously.

Leased assets and maintenance reserves

For aircraft under leases, lessees often pay maintenance reserves to lessors and bear return conditions. The overhaul on a leased aircraft is capitalised as a leasehold improvement or as part of the ROU (if it is a component the lessee owns economically), or accounted for as a provision for return conditions under Ind AS 37 where the obligation is to the lessor. Indian airlines disclose these as "maintenance reserve" receivables and "provision for redelivery". Treat the lessee's own heavy maintenance obligation carefully — it is not the same as Ind AS 16.14 for owned aircraft.

Presentation and disclosure

  • The inspection component sits within the relevant PPE class (e.g. plant and equipment; aircraft).
  • Depreciation methods and lives (including inspection cycles) are disclosed under Ind AS 16.73.
  • Derecognition losses are disclosed in the PPE roll-forward as "disposals/deductions".

Model implementation

In a 3-statement model, overhaul spend is a capex line with a periodic pattern (e.g. every fourth year). Depreciation of the overhaul component should be modelled as a separate vintage layer, so that a turnaround in FY27 generates depreciation in FY28–FY31 and the old component is fully written off. Use a capex flag =IF(MOD(year-first_year, cycle)=0, cost×(1+inflation)^(year-base), 0).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Turnaround including major replacements: physical parts replaced (e.g. a heat exchanger) are accounted for under Ind AS 16.13 (s04) separately from the inspection service cost.
  • Inspection cost overruns caused by unexpected repairs may be routine repairs and expensed, not capitalised.
  • Extended cycles: if engineers extend the interval from 4 to 5 years, the remaining carrying amount is depreciated over the revised remaining interval — a prospective estimate change (s06).
  • Provision for future overhauls is prohibited — there is no present obligation to perform a future overhaul (Ind AS 37 Illustrative Example 11); the entity could sell or stop operating the asset.
  • Regulatory inspections with no physical work (e.g. statutory boiler inspection with minimal cost) may be immaterial and expensed under the materiality principle.
  • Tax: expenditure on overhauls is often claimed as current repairs (Sec 30/31) — creating a temporary difference with the capitalised book component.

Key takeaways

  • Major inspections required for continued operation are capitalised as a component and depreciated to the next inspection.
  • The remaining carrying amount of the previous inspection is always derecognised, even if it was never separately identified.
  • Use the cost of a similar future inspection as a proxy for the original embedded component.
  • Routine servicing is expensed; future overhauls are never provided for.
  • Early inspections produce a derecognition loss in P&L, not an adjustment to revenue.
  • Model overhauls as periodic capex with separate depreciation vintages.

Practice Questions

Q1. Aircraft heavy check sequence [L2]

Garuda Air Ltd buys an aircraft for ₹520 crore on 1 April 2023. A heavy check is required every 6 years; the cost of a check at purchase-date prices is estimated at ₹42 crore. Airframe life 20 years, residual value ₹52 crore (on the non-check portion). The first heavy check is performed on 1 April 2028 at ₹48 crore. Compute annual depreciation by component from FY24, the carrying amount of the original check component at 1 April 2028, and pass the entries on the check date.

Q2. Dry-docking with no original component identified [L3]

Andaman Shipping Ltd has carried a vessel at a single cost of ₹90 crore (25-year life, nil residual) since 1 April 2021, without identifying a dry-docking component. The first dry-docking occurs on 1 April 2026 at ₹10 crore; the next is expected in 5 years. Management argues no derecognition is needed because no component was recorded. Evaluate the argument, compute an estimate of the implied component to derecognise using the current cost as proxy deflated at 5% p.a., and pass the entries.

💡 Hint: Ind AS 16.14 applies regardless of whether the previous inspection cost was identified at acquisition.

Q3. Turnaround spend analysis [L2]

A fertiliser company's ₹65 crore annual turnaround includes: contractor inspection and cleaning ₹30 crore, replacement of a reformer tube bundle ₹22 crore (previous bundle carrying amount ₹6 crore), catalyst replacement ₹8 crore, and repairs of leaks discovered ₹5 crore. Classify each element, state the accounting and compute the P&L charge in the year of the turnaround.

Q4. Provision for future overhaul [L1]

A power company proposes to create a provision of ₹15 crore each year for a major turbine overhaul due in 4 years. Explain why Ind AS 37 does not permit this and how Ind AS 16 achieves a similar smoothing of cost.

Q5. Model build: refinery turnaround capex [L3]

Build the specification for an Excel block that forecasts turnaround capex (₹120 crore at FY26 prices, every 4 years starting FY27, inflation 5%), its depreciation by vintage and the derecognition of the prior component, feeding the PPE roll-forward for FY26–FY35. List the inputs, the formulas you would use and the checks.
