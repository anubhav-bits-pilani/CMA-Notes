Subtopic note

Component Accounting under Ind AS 16.43 and Schedule II

Scope: Component accounting (Ind AS 16.43) and Schedule II mandatory componentisation; significant parts with different useful lives

Why it matters

Component accounting changes depreciation timing, sometimes materially. An aircraft depreciated as one 20-year unit understates early depreciation if its engines need replacement every 8 years; a hotel building depreciated over 60 years ignores interiors that last 10. Componentisation also decides what happens when a part is replaced: if the part was a separate component, its remaining carrying amount is derecognised; if not, the replacement is often wrongly expensed. Indian companies moved to mandatory componentisation with Schedule II of the Companies Act 2013 (from FY 2015-16), so every fixed asset register should carry a component map. Auditors test whether components are identified for all significant assets; analysts see the effect in depreciation-to-gross-block ratios; modellers need component lives to forecast replacement capex.

Core notes

The rule

Each part of an item of PPE with a cost that is significant in relation to the total cost of the item shall be depreciated separately (Ind AS 16.43). The entity allocates the amount initially recognised to its significant parts (Ind AS 16.44). Key supporting provisions:

  • A significant part may have a useful life and depreciation method that are the same as another significant part of the same item; such parts may be grouped for depreciation (Ind AS 16.45).
  • Parts that are individually not significant may be grouped and depreciated together; if expectations vary, approximation techniques may be used (Ind AS 16.46).
  • An entity may depreciate separately parts that are not significant (Ind AS 16.47) — e.g. a policy threshold that captures more components.
  • Each component's depreciation charge is recognised in P&L unless included in the carrying amount of another asset (Ind AS 16.48).

Schedule II requirement

Schedule II, Part A, Note 4 (as applicable from 1 April 2015) states that useful life of a significant part shall be determined separately where its cost is significant to the total cost of the asset and its useful life differs from that of the remaining asset. This made componentisation mandatory for all companies, including those under Indian GAAP (AS 10 Revised) — the ICAI Guidance Note on Accounting for Depreciation in Companies in the context of Schedule II gives practical guidance. Two conditions together trigger separate depreciation under Schedule II: significance of cost and a different life. Ind AS 16.43 is technically broader (significance alone), though parts with the same life can be grouped, so in practice the result converges.

Determining "significant"

Neither Ind AS 16 nor Schedule II gives a numerical threshold. Common Indian practice:

  • a percentage threshold such as 10% of total item cost combined with an absolute floor (e.g. ₹10 lakh or ₹50 lakh depending on materiality);
  • engineering input on parts with distinctly different replacement cycles;
  • materiality of the depreciation difference: if separate depreciation would not materially change the charge, grouping is acceptable.

Document the policy in the PPE accounting manual and apply it consistently by asset class.

Typical component maps

Asset Typical components Indicative life drivers
Aircraft Airframe, engines (core and life-limited parts), landing gear, interiors, heavy maintenance check Flight hours/cycles
Refinery/process plant Reactor and columns, catalyst, refractory lining, rotating equipment, turnaround component Run-length between turnarounds
Hotel building Structure, façade, MEP (HVAC, lifts, electricals), interiors/furnishings Refurbishment cycle
Ship Hull, engines, dry-docking component Classification society survey cycle
Telecom tower Tower structure, shelter, DG set, batteries Battery replacement every 4–5 years
Thermal power plant Boiler, turbine, generator, coal handling, ash handling Overhaul cycle
Solar plant Modules, inverters, mounting structures Inverters ~10–12 years vs modules ~25 years

Allocating cost to components

Cost is allocated on a rational basis:

  1. Vendor invoice breakdown where available (engines priced separately from airframe).
  2. Replacement cost or engineering estimates where only a lump-sum price exists, scaled to the total.
  3. Relative fair values in a purchased group.
  4. For self-constructed assets, project cost codes mapped to components; directly attributable costs and capitalised borrowing costs are allocated pro rata unless clearly linked to one component.

The decommissioning asset is usually depreciated with the main structure or over the period to which the obligation relates.

Mechanics and example

Konkan Hotels Ltd completes a hotel on 1 April 2025 for ₹240 crore (land excluded). Component study:

Component Cost (₹ crore) Useful life Annual SLM (nil RV)
Structure 150 60 years 2.50
MEP (HVAC, lifts, electricals) 50 15 years 3.33
Interiors and FF&E 40 8 years 5.00
Total 240 10.83

Single-unit depreciation over 60 years would be ₹4.00 crore — the component approach more than doubles the charge and matches the refurbishment pattern. Schedule II gives 60 years for RCC frame buildings and 8 years for furniture and fittings in hotels, so these lives align with Part C.

Journal entry (FY26, ₹ crore):

Account Dr Cr
Depreciation expense 10.83
Accumulated depreciation — Structure 2.50
Accumulated depreciation — MEP 3.33
Accumulated depreciation — Interiors 5.00

Fixed asset register design

Each component is a separate asset record with its own cost, life, residual value, method, capitalisation date and parent asset ID. This enables:

  • automatic derecognition when a component is replaced (s03, s04);
  • Schedule III class-wise roll-forward (components roll up into "Buildings", "Plant and equipment", "Furniture and fixtures");
  • tax block mapping (components of a building may fall into different tax blocks — e.g. building at 10% vs plant at 15%).

In ERP systems (SAP FI-AA, Oracle FA), components are sub-numbers of the main asset. In Tally Prime, maintain them as separate ledgers under the asset group with a register in Excel.

Transition and first-time component splits

Companies that did not componentise before Schedule II had to split existing carrying amounts; under Ind AS 101 first-time adoption, the deemed cost exemption (previous GAAP carrying amount) applies at the item level, but componentisation must still be applied prospectively from transition for depreciation.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Land and buildings are always separate assets (Ind AS 16.58); land is not depreciated except quarries and landfill sites.
  • Leasehold improvements in leased premises are depreciated over the shorter of their life and the lease term — this is a componentisation decision linked to Ind AS 116.
  • Spare parts and servicing equipment meeting the PPE definition are PPE (Ind AS 16.8) and may form their own components (s07).
  • Low-value parts grouped together may use an average life; revisit when replacement patterns diverge.
  • Different methods for components: a component can use units-of-production (e.g. engines on flight hours) while the airframe uses SLM.
  • Overlap with major inspections: the inspection cost embedded in a new asset is identified as a component even if it was not separately invoiced (s03).
  • Materiality judgements must be revisited when a component later becomes significant due to cost increases.

Key takeaways

  • Ind AS 16.43 requires separate depreciation of each significant part; Schedule II requires separate lives where a part is significant and has a different life.
  • Significance is a policy judgement — set a percentage-plus-absolute threshold and engineering criteria.
  • Parts with the same life and method can be grouped; insignificant parts can be grouped with approximations.
  • Allocate cost on a rational basis (invoices, replacement cost, relative fair values) and document it.
  • Component records make replacements and overhauls mechanically correct and drive replacement capex forecasts.
  • Componentisation usually increases early-year depreciation versus single-unit accounting.

Practice Questions

Q1. Component split for a ship [L2]

Coromandel Shipping Ltd acquires a vessel for ₹180 crore on 1 April 2025. Engineers estimate: hull ₹120 crore (25 years), main engines ₹36 crore (12 years), navigation equipment ₹9 crore (6 years), dry-docking component ₹15 crore (5-year survey cycle). Residual value of the hull is ₹12 crore; others nil. Compute FY26 depreciation using components and as a single 25-year unit with the same total residual value, and pass the component-wise entry.

Q2. Setting a significance policy [L3]

Draft a componentisation policy for a diversified manufacturer with gross block of ₹4,000 crore across 3,200 assets. Specify the significance threshold, the role of engineering assessments, grouping rules, documentation requirements and how the policy reconciles Ind AS 16.43 with Schedule II Note 4.

💡 Hint: Consider both a relative threshold and an absolute floor so that small assets do not proliferate into components.

Q3. Lump-sum purchase allocation [L2]

A telecom infrastructure company buys 500 towers from an operator for ₹600 crore. Replacement cost estimates per tower: structure ₹0.85 crore (25 years), shelter ₹0.10 crore (15 years), DG set ₹0.12 crore (8 years), batteries ₹0.08 crore (4 years). Allocate the purchase price to components and compute first-year depreciation for the portfolio.

Q4. Review of a register without components [L2]

An auditor finds that a company's ₹900 crore refinery has been depreciated as one asset over 25 years, while its catalyst (₹60 crore) is replaced every 3 years and expensed. Describe the misstatements, whether correcting them is a change in estimate or an error under Ind AS 8, and the steps to rebuild the register.

Q5. Interview question: component vs single unit [L1]

In an FP&A interview you are asked: "Why does a hotel company's depreciation look high relative to its buildings' 60-year life?" Explain in 5–6 sentences, referring to componentisation and its effect on EBIT and capex forecasting.
