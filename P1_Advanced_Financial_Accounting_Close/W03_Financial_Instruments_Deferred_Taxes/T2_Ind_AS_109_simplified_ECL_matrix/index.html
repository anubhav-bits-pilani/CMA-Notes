Roadmap topic: Ind AS 109 Expected Credit Loss (ECL): Simplified approach provision matrix for trade receivables using historical loss rates and forward-looking overlays

Overview

Ind AS 109 replaced the incurred-loss model with an expected credit loss model: an allowance is recognised from day one for credit losses expected over a horizon, without waiting for a loss event. For trade receivables and contract assets the standard mandates a simplified approach — lifetime ECL from initial recognition, with no stage tracking (5.5.15) — and expressly permits a provision matrix based on historical loss experience adjusted for current and forward-looking information (B5.5.35). For most Indian corporates the provision matrix is the single largest impairment estimate after goodwill and is a standing key audit matter for infrastructure, power, EPC and government-facing businesses.

The skills matter across the target roles. A reporting accountant segments receivables, computes roll rates from ageing reports, applies forward-looking overlays and books the allowance; a technical-accounting specialist writes the policy, the credit-impairment and write-off criteria and the Schedule III and Ind AS 107 disclosures; an FP&A analyst uses the same loss rates and DSO drivers to forecast bad-debt cost and working capital. Auditors test segmentation, data completeness, the link between overlays and macro indicators, and the overlap with specific provisions.

The learning sequence: confirm which balances fall under the simplified approach (s01), design the matrix and its segments (s02), derive historical loss rates by roll-rate analysis (s03), add forward-looking overlays and scenario weighting (s04), layer specific provisions for credit-impaired customers without double counting (s05), handle write-offs, recoveries, related-party and government receivables and time value (s06), present the balances under Schedule III (s07) and contrast the general three-stage approach used for loans and NBFCs (s08). The six key concepts give the formulas (PD × LGD × EAD, roll rates, adjusted loss rates), the measurement principles of 5.5.17, the journal entries, and the 30/90 days-past-due presumptions.

Related concepts to study

US GAAP CECL

US GAAP CECL (ASC 326): lifetime losses from day one on all amortised-cost assets

ASC 326-20 (current expected credit loss) requires lifetime expected losses on day one for all financial assets at amortised cost — trade receivables, loans, held-to-maturity securities, net investment in leases — with no 12-month stage. Ind AS 109's simplified approach for receivables is therefore economically close to CECL, but for loans Ind AS uses the three-stage model where Stage 1 carries only 12-month ECL. CECL allows reversion to historical loss information beyond the reasonable and supportable forecast period and permits a range of methods (loss-rate, roll-rate, vintage, DCF, PD/LGD). Available-for-sale debt securities follow a separate allowance model (ASC 326-30). Indian subsidiaries of US groups often run both: a lifetime CECL number for the US pack and a staged number for statutory accounts.

Tax deduction only on write-off

Tax: deduction only on write-off (Sec 36(1)(vii)) → DTA → w3_t3

The Income-tax Act allows a deduction for bad debts only when actually written off as irrecoverable in the books (Sec 36(1)(vii), subject to the debt having been included in income under Sec 36(2)); after TRF Ltd (SC, 2010) the assessee need not prove irrecoverability once written off. An ECL allowance is a provision and is not deductible; ICDS does not change this. The allowance therefore has a tax base of nil against a carrying amount (as a contra-asset) that reduces receivables, giving a deductible temporary difference and a DTA at 25.168% under 115BAA, subject to the probability test. When the debt is written off, the DTA reverses and current tax falls. Banks and NBFCs have additional provisions under Sec 36(1)(viia).

Ind AS 107 loss-allowance reconciliation

Ind AS 107.35H loss-allowance reconciliation and credit risk disclosures → w3_t6

Ind AS 107.35H requires a reconciliation from opening to closing loss allowance, by class, showing separately for simplified-approach assets the lifetime ECL movement, and for general-approach assets the 12-month, lifetime not credit-impaired and lifetime credit-impaired amounts. Typical lines: opening balance, allowance on new assets, changes in risk parameters, write-offs, recoveries, foreign exchange and closing balance. 107.35F requires disclosure of the credit risk management practices (default definition, write-off policy, how forward-looking information is used); 35M requires credit risk exposure by rating grade, which for receivables under the simplified approach can be a provision-matrix table (35N). Week 3 t6 builds these tables.

RBI IRACP norms vs Ind AS ECL

RBI IRACP norms vs Ind AS ECL for NBFCs (Schedule III Division III)

NBFCs on Ind AS present under Schedule III Division III and measure impairment by ECL, but RBI's Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning (IRACP) norms continue to apply for regulatory purposes. Under the RBI circular of 13 March 2020 on Ind AS implementation by NBFCs, where the Ind AS impairment allowance is lower than the IRACP provision (including standard asset provisioning), the difference is appropriated from net profit after tax to a separate "Impairment Reserve", which cannot be used for dividends or capital without RBI approval. NBFCs disclose a comparison of the two by asset classification. Asset classification (NPA at 90 DPD) and Stage 3 usually align, though upgrade rules differ.

Contract assets and FP&A drivers

Contract assets from w1_t4 included in the matrix; DSO and bad-debt drivers in FP&A forecasts

Contract assets under Ind AS 115 (unbilled revenue where the right to consideration is conditional) are within the simplified approach and should be included in the matrix, usually as a "not yet due" segment or with a loss rate derived from the related customer segment (they carry the same customer credit risk). In FP&A models, the ECL rate feeds a bad-debt expense driver (allowance as a percentage of closing receivables by segment), and DSO drives the receivable balance; a change in collection behaviour (rising DSO) both raises receivables and pushes balances into higher-loss buckets, so forecasts should link the ageing profile to DSO rather than hold the ECL percentage flat.

Common pitfalls

Historical rates with no forward-looking adjustment

Using ageing-based historical rates with no forward-looking adjustment

Teams compute loss rates from past ageing and stop, because finding a statistical link to macro data is hard. Ind AS 109.5.5.17(c) and B5.5.35 require adjustment for current conditions and forecasts. The correct approach is to identify drivers (sector stress, GDP, commodity prices, payer-specific signals), build at least two scenarios with weights, and adjust bucket rates; if the overlay is judged to be nil, document why. In review, look for an overlay working paper, scenario weights approved by management, and back-testing of last year's estimate against actual losses.

Zero ECL on government receivables

Zero ECL on government/PSU receivables ignoring time value of delayed payment

Preparers assume sovereign or PSU counterparties never default, so loss rates are zero. But ECL includes time value: a ₹100 Cr receivable from a state DISCOM collected after 2 years without compensating interest is worth less than ₹100 Cr (5.5.17(b), B5.5.44). Correct treatment: estimate expected collection timing, discount at the original EIR (for a receivable with no financing component, a rate approximating the contract's effective rate), and recognise the shortfall as ECL. Detect it by checking for large old government balances with nil allowance and comparing actual collection days with contract terms.

Double counting specific and matrix provisions

Applying matrix rates to balances already specifically provided (double count)

When a customer is admitted to NCLT, a 100% specific provision is booked — but the balance still sits in the >365 days bucket and attracts the matrix rate again. The fix is to remove specifically assessed balances from the matrix population before applying bucket rates, and to remove the same customers from the historical data used to compute roll rates (or adjust consistently). Review by reconciling: total receivables = matrix population + specifically assessed population, with no customer in both.

Loss rates on revenue applied to balances

Loss rates computed on revenue while applied to receivable balances

A shortcut computes "bad debts ÷ revenue" (say 0.8%) and multiplies it by closing receivables. The two bases are inconsistent: revenue is a flow for the year while receivables are a point-in-time stock, so the rate is understated or overstated depending on DSO. Correct approach: compute loss rates on the same base to which they are applied — losses ÷ receivable balance in the bucket (roll-rate) or losses ÷ cohort of sales tracked to collection (vintage, then applied to the uncollected cohort). Review by checking the denominator of every rate in the working.

Practice Questions

Q1. End-to-end provision matrix for a power EPC company [L3]

Bharat Grid Projects Ltd has receivables and contract assets at 31 March 2026: private industrial customers ₹220 Cr; state DISCOMs ₹340 Cr; central PSUs ₹90 Cr; contract assets (unbilled) ₹60 Cr. Ageing of private customers: not due ₹110 Cr, 1–90 days ₹60 Cr, 91–180 days ₹25 Cr, 181–365 days ₹15 Cr, >365 days ₹10 Cr, of which ₹8 Cr relates to a customer admitted to NCLT in January 2026. Historical lifetime loss rates for private customers: 0.5%, 2%, 8%, 25%, 60%. DISCOM balances are expected to be collected on average 14 months after due date without interest; the original effective rate is 9%; DISCOM ageing: not due ₹120 Cr, 1–365 days ₹140 Cr, >365 days ₹80 Cr. Management's forward-looking adjustment: +15% on private-customer rates in the downside scenario (weight 30%), 0% base (weight 70%). Design the segments, compute ECL for each segment including time value for DISCOMs, pass the year-end entry if the opening allowance is ₹18 Cr and write-offs during the year were ₹4 Cr, and draft the Ind AS 107.35H reconciliation.

💡 Hint: Carve out the NCLT customer first; for DISCOMs the loss is mainly the discount for delayed collection.

Q2. Roll rates to Schedule III note [L2]

Using three years of quarterly ageing data supplied by the controller (opening and closing balances by 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–180, >180 days buckets, with write-offs), explain the steps to compute roll rates and bucket loss rates, and then prepare the Schedule III trade receivables ageing note showing undisputed and disputed balances split into considered good, SICR and credit impaired. Data: closing 31 March 2026 balances ₹45 Cr, ₹18 Cr, ₹9 Cr, ₹6 Cr, ₹4 Cr; of the >180 days balance, ₹1.5 Cr is disputed and ₹1 Cr relates to a customer in liquidation.

Q3. Deferred tax and FP&A forecast [L2]

A company's ECL allowance rises from ₹12 Cr to ₹16.5 Cr after write-offs of ₹3 Cr. Compute the P&L impairment charge, the DTA movement at 25.168% and the current tax effect of the write-off. Then explain how you would forecast FY 2026-27 bad-debt expense in the budget if DSO is expected to rise from 72 to 85 days.

Q4. NBFC comparison memo [L3]

An NBFC's Ind AS ECL is ₹180 Cr (Stage 1 ₹40 Cr, Stage 2 ₹55 Cr, Stage 3 ₹85 Cr). IRACP provisions would be ₹210 Cr. Explain the accounting and disclosure required by the RBI March 2020 circular, contrast the staging logic with the simplified approach used for trade receivables of a manufacturing company, and identify two areas where the 30/90 DPD presumptions might be rebutted.
