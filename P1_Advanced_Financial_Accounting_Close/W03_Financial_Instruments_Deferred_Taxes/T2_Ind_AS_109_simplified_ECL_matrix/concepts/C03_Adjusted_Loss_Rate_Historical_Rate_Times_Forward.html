Key concept

Adjusted Loss Rate: Historical Rate Times Forward-Looking Factor

Concept: Adjusted loss rate = Historical loss rate × (1 + Forward-looking adjustment)

Statement

\[ LR^{\text{adj}}_{s,b} = LR^{\text{hist}}_{s,b} \times (1 + FLA_{s}) \]

where \( LR^{\text{hist}}_{s,b} \) is the historical lifetime loss rate for segment \( s \) and bucket \( b \) (from roll-rate or vintage analysis), and \( FLA_{s} \) the forward-looking adjustment for segment \( s \), positive when conditions are expected to worsen relative to the historical period and negative when they are expected to improve. Where scenarios are used:

\[ ECL = \sum_{k} w_k \sum_{b} B_{s,b} \times LR^{\text{hist}}_{s,b} \times (1 + FLA_{s,k}) \]

with \( w_k \) the probability weight of scenario \( k \) (\( \sum w_k = 1 \)).

Explanation

Ind AS 109.B5.5.52 requires historical loss experience to be adjusted on the basis of current observable data to reflect current conditions and forecasts that did not affect the historical period, and to remove effects of past conditions that are not relevant. The multiplicative form is a pragmatic way to do this: it scales the whole curve for a segment up or down.

How \( FLA \) is derived:

  • Statistical: regress historical segment loss rates on drivers (GDP, sector index, commodity price); \( FLA = \frac{\widehat{LR}_{\text{forecast}}}{\overline{LR}_{\text{hist}}} - 1 \).
  • Scalar: a calibrated sensitivity, e.g., +8% loss rate per 1 percentage point fall in GDP growth below the historical average.
  • Judgemental overlay: management estimate based on indicators where data is thin — must be documented and back-tested.

Cautions: the adjustment should be bounded so that adjusted rates stay within 0–100%; it may differ by bucket (stress often hits older buckets harder); and a single factor applied to all segments is unlikely to be appropriate. Because the relationship between macro conditions and losses is usually convex, scenario weighting of outcomes (not averaging the factors) is required by 5.5.17(a) — though with a purely multiplicative, segment-wide factor the two give the same answer, so non-linearity must come from bucket-specific or capped factors.

Illustration

Lata Steel Traders Ltd, private customer segment. Historical loss rates: not due 0.8%, 1–90 DPD 3%, 91–180 DPD 15%, >180 DPD 55%. A regression shows segment loss rates move 10% for each 5% change in the domestic steel price index, inversely. Forecast: steel prices fall 10% in the base case (FLA +20%) and 25% in the downside (FLA +50%, with the >180 bucket capped at 80%); weights 65/35. Balances ₹30 Cr, ₹12 Cr, ₹4 Cr, ₹1.5 Cr.

Bucket Balance Hist rate Base rate Downside rate
Not due 30.0 0.8% 0.96% 1.2%
1–90 12.0 3% 3.6% 4.5%
91–180 4.0 15% 18% 22.5%
>180 1.5 55% 66% 80% (capped)
ECL 2.03 2.43 3.00

Weighted ECL = 0.65 × 2.43 + 0.35 × 3.00 = ₹2.63 Cr, against ₹2.03 Cr on unadjusted historical rates.

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply scenario-specific adjustments [L2]

Historical rates for a segment are 0.4%, 2%, 10%, 40% on balances ₹50 Cr, ₹15 Cr, ₹5 Cr, ₹2 Cr. Base FLA +10% (weight 60%), downside FLA +60% (weight 30%), upside FLA −15% (weight 10%). Compute ECL in each scenario and the weighted ECL.

💡 Hint: Compute each scenario's ECL fully before weighting.

Q2. Deriving FLA from a regression [L2]

A regression of annual loss rates on real GDP growth gives \( LR = 4.2\% - 0.35 \times g \), where \( g \) is GDP growth in percent. The historical average loss rate is 2.1%. Compute the FLA if forecast GDP growth is 5.5%, and discuss two limitations of using this single-driver model.

Q3. Negative adjustment challenge [L1]

Management proposes a −25% FLA because the economy is booming. As the reviewer, what evidence would you require before accepting a negative adjustment?
