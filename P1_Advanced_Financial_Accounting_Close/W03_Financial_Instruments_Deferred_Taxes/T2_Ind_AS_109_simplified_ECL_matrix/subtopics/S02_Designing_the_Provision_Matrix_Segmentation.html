Subtopic note

Designing the Provision Matrix: Segmentation Choices (B5.5.35)

Scope: Provision matrix design (B5.5.35): segmentation by customer type, geography, product, government vs private, credit rating

Why it matters

A provision matrix is only as reliable as its segments. A single company-wide set of ageing rates blends customers with very different loss patterns — export distributors, government departments, retail dealers, group companies — and the resulting rates are too high for some and too low for others. Auditors ask how segments were chosen and whether loss patterns actually differ; analysts and lenders look at the segment disclosures (Ind AS 107.35M–35N) to understand concentration. In FP&A, segment-level loss rates let you forecast bad-debt cost by business unit and channel.

Core notes

The B5.5.35 permission

Ind AS 109.B5.5.35 allows practical expedients when measuring ECL, if consistent with 5.5.17. An example is a provision matrix for trade receivables: the entity uses its historical credit loss experience (adjusted as appropriate) to estimate lifetime ECL, with fixed provision rates depending on the number of days a receivable is past due (e.g., 1% if not past due, 2% if less than 30 days past due, 3% if 30–90 days, 20% if 90–180 days). Depending on the diversity of its customer base, the entity uses appropriate groupings if its historical credit loss experience shows significantly different loss patterns for different customer segments. Examples of criteria to group assets include geographical region, product type, customer rating, collateral or trade credit insurance and type of customer (such as wholesale or retail).

Ind AS 109.B5.5.5 adds that, for collective assessment, instruments are grouped on the basis of shared credit risk characteristics.

Segmentation criteria in Indian practice

Criterion Why loss patterns differ Typical segments
Customer type Dealers vs OEMs vs retail vs e-commerce platforms OEM, distributors, retail, marketplace
Government vs private Government rarely defaults but pays late; private can fail Central PSU, state government/DISCOM, private
Geography Export credit risk, country risk, local enforcement Domestic, export — by region
Product / business line Different credit terms and customer bases Cement trade vs non-trade; pharma domestic vs institutional
Credit rating / internal score Direct proxy for PD Investment-grade, sub-investment-grade, unrated
Collateral / credit insurance / LC Reduces LGD LC-backed, ECGC-insured, bank-guaranteed, unsecured
Related party Parental support, offsetting balances Group, third party

How to decide the segments

  1. Start from risk drivers. List characteristics that plausibly drive default and loss given default.
  2. Test historical data. Compute loss rates for candidate segments; keep a split only if rates differ significantly and the segment has enough data to be statistically meaningful.
  3. Balance granularity vs data. Too many segments produce unstable rates; too few hide concentration. Three to six segments is common.
  4. Ensure each segment is homogeneous over time. Migrating customers between segments should follow a documented rule (e.g., rating change).
  5. Align with credit management. Segments should mirror how the credit control team sets limits and monitors collections.
  6. Document and back-test annually.

Matrix structure

Within each segment, columns are ageing buckets based on days past due (DPD), not days since invoice. Common buckets: not due, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–180, 181–365, >365 DPD. Buckets should be finer where loss rates change fastest.

Excel and Power BI implementation

=SUMPRODUCT(BucketBalances, LossRates)
=SUMIFS(Balance, Segment, $A5, Bucket, B$4)

In Power BI, a customer-level table with Segment and Bucket columns (calculated from DPD) joined to a loss-rate table by Segment–Bucket key lets you show ECL by segment and drill to customers:

ECL = SUMX(Receivables, Receivables[Balance] * RELATED(LossRates[AdjustedRate]))

Documentation for audit

The matrix memo should include: segmentation rationale and statistical support; data source (ERP ageing, reconciled to the GL); bucket definitions (DPD from due date); historical period; the loss-rate method (s03); overlays (s04); exclusions for specific provisions (s05); and back-testing.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Small segments with few defaults — combine with a similar segment or use external data (rating agency default studies) for the PD.
  • Customer-level credit insurance (ECGC, private trade credit insurers) affects LGD; insured balances may form a segment with lower rates, but the insurance must be integral to the receivable, not a separate contract recognised as an asset (B5.5.55).
  • Customers moving between segments (a distributor becoming a large chain) — apply the segment of the reporting date, but ensure historical data is mapped consistently.
  • Unallocated receipts / credit notes must be matched to invoices before ageing; otherwise older buckets are overstated.
  • Advances from the same customer — net only if there is a legally enforceable right of set-off and intention (Ind AS 32.42); otherwise age gross.

Key takeaways

  • B5.5.35 allows a matrix with DPD-based rates, segmented where loss patterns differ significantly.
  • Segment by customer type, government vs private, geography, product, rating and collateral where data supports it.
  • Buckets are days past due, not days since invoice.
  • Keep segments few, homogeneous and aligned with credit control.
  • Document the rationale and back-test annually.

Practice Questions

Q1. Designing segments for a cement company [L2]

A cement company sells through 2,000 dealers (security deposits held from each), to 40 infrastructure contractors on 60-day credit, and to central/state government projects. Historical loss rates for the last 4 years: dealers 0.6% of balances, contractors 3.1%, government 0.1% (but average collection 230 days). Propose a segmentation and bucket structure, and explain how dealer security deposits should be treated.

💡 Hint: Think about LGD for dealers and time value for government.

Q2. Matrix computation [L1]

Compute ECL for a segment with balances: not due ₹30 Cr, 1–30 DPD ₹8 Cr, 31–90 DPD ₹4 Cr, 91–180 DPD ₹2 Cr, >180 DPD ₹1 Cr, and adjusted loss rates 0.5%, 1.5%, 5%, 18%, 55%. Present the result as a provision matrix disclosure under Ind AS 107.35N.

Q3. Power BI ECL model [L3]

Design a Power BI data model for a group with five entities to calculate ECL by entity, segment and bucket from ERP ageing extracts. Specify the tables, keys, the DPD calculation in Power Query, a DAX measure for ECL and a check measure against the GL allowance.

Q4. Challenging a single matrix [L2]

An auditor finds that a pharma company uses one matrix for domestic trade, institutional (hospital and government tender) and export receivables. Write the questions the auditor should ask and the evidence needed to justify or reject the single matrix.
