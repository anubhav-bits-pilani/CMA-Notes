Roadmap topic: Ind AS 12 Income Taxes: Temporary vs permanent differences, DTA/DTL proof across PPE, Ind AS 116 leases, Ind AS 19 provisions, and Section 115BAA tax rates

Overview

Ind AS 12 requires deferred tax on temporary differences — differences between the carrying amount of an asset or liability in the balance sheet and its tax base — rather than on timing differences between accounting profit and taxable profit as under the old AS 22. The balance-sheet approach captures items that never pass through P&L (OCI remeasurements, business-combination fair value step-ups, compound instrument splits) and forces a disciplined inventory of every balance. For Indian companies the computation sits on top of a tax regime that changed fundamentally in 2019 with Section 115BAA (22% base rate without MAT and most incentives) and is being renumbered by the Income-tax Act 2025 from 1 April 2026.

For the target roles this topic is where accounting, tax and modelling meet. A financial reporting analyst prepares the deferred tax workings, the roll-forward and the ETR reconciliation for the annual report and quarterly results; a technical-accounting specialist handles the judgements — DTA recognition on losses, uncertain tax treatments, rate selection on a regime switch; an FP&A or valuation analyst forecasts the effective tax rate, cash taxes and the DTL unwind in a 3-statement model. Auditors and NFRA reviews regularly question DTA on losses, the treatment of MAT credit on switching to 115BAA and the ETR reconciliation.

The learning sequence begins with the balance-sheet method and the taxonomy of differences (s01), then the biggest recurring sources in Indian companies: PPE depreciation (s02), leases and decommissioning after the 2023 amendment (s03) and payment-basis or write-off-basis deductions under Sec 43B, 36(1)(vii) and 40(a)(ia) (s04). Next comes the rate: 115BAA mechanics and remeasurement on switching (s05). The judgement areas follow — losses and unabsorbed depreciation (s06), and items outside P&L, business combinations, outside-basis differences and uncertain tax treatments (s07). Finally, the ETR reconciliation and the remapping of section references under the new Act (s08). The key concepts give the formulas you will reuse: the DT measurement rule, the effective rates, the roll-forward, the ETR bridge, offset and no-discounting, and the DTA review at each reporting date.

Related concepts to study

MAT credit and Sec 115BAB

MAT credit (Sec 115JB) write-off on opting for 115BAA; Sec 115BAB comparison for new manufacturing units

A company opting for 115BAA cannot use MAT credit brought forward under Sec 115JAA; the provisions of Sec 115JB do not apply to it. Any MAT credit asset in the books must therefore be written off when the decision to opt is made (or when it becomes probable the company will opt), and the timing of the switch is often planned to utilise MAT credit first. MAT credit is presented as a deferred tax asset in Ind AS financial statements (ICAI guidance), so its write-off is a deferred tax charge in P&L. Sec 115BAB offered 15% (17.16% effective with 10% surcharge and 4% cess) for new manufacturing companies incorporated after 1 October 2019 that commenced manufacturing by 31 March 2024; the window was not extended, so it applies only to companies already qualified.

July 2024 capital gains changes and ICDS

July 2024 capital gains changes (LTCG 12.5%, no indexation) affecting DTL on FVOCI/FVTPL investments; ICDS-driven differences

The Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 changed capital gains rates for transfers on or after 23 July 2024: long-term gains on listed equity and equity-oriented funds under Sec 112A taxed at 12.5% (exemption threshold ₹1.25 lakh), short-term gains under Sec 111A at 20%, and other long-term gains at 12.5% without indexation under Sec 112. Holding periods were simplified to 12 months for listed securities and 24 months for others. Deferred tax on unrealised gains of FVOCI and FVTPL investments must use the rate expected to apply on sale (Ind AS 12.51), so the changes required remeasurement in FY 2024-25 — through OCI for FVOCI items and P&L for FVTPL. ICDS (notified under Sec 145(2)) create further differences, e.g., ICDS on construction contracts and on government grants, and mark-to-market losses on derivatives not allowed unless realised (ICDS I).

ASC 740 and Pillar Two

US GAAP ASC 740: valuation allowance model; IAS 12 Pillar Two mandatory exception for top-up tax deferred taxes

ASC 740 recognises all deferred tax assets in full and then reduces them with a valuation allowance if it is more likely than not (above 50%) that some portion will not be realised. Ind AS 12 recognises a DTA only to the extent it is probable that taxable profit will be available — a single-step test, but the outcome is similar. ASC 740 also has its own uncertain tax position model (two-step recognition and measurement, ASC 740-10), comparable to Ind AS 12 Appendix C. For the OECD Pillar Two global minimum tax (15%), the IASB amended IAS 12 in May 2023 with a mandatory temporary exception from recognising and disclosing deferred taxes related to top-up taxes, plus disclosures of current top-up tax and exposure. MCA has incorporated a corresponding amendment into Ind AS 12; confirm the notification date and applicable period before relying on it. Indian multinationals in large groups (consolidated revenue of at least €750 million) are within scope.

Interim tax under Ind AS 34

Ind AS 34 interim tax via estimated annual effective tax rate

Ind AS 34 requires interim income tax expense to be recognised using the best estimate of the weighted average annual income tax rate expected for the full financial year, applied to the interim period's pre-tax income (Ind AS 34.B12–B16). For an Indian company reporting quarterly under SEBI LODR Reg 33, the estimated annual effective rate includes the impact of permanent differences (CSR, penalties) and expected changes in unrecognised DTA. Tax effects of one-off items (a rate change, a settlement of prior-year assessments, MAT credit write-off on switching to 115BAA) are recognised in the quarter they occur, not spread. Where jurisdictions or categories of income have different rates (capital gains), separate estimates are used.

Schedules feeding the DT computation

→ w2_t1, w2_t2, w2_t5, w3_t1, w3_t2 schedules supply carrying amounts; → Week 4 close: tax provisioning step

Deferred tax is a downstream computation: Week 2 schedules supply the lease liability and ROU asset (w2_t1), PPE and decommissioning balances (w2_t2) and employee benefit obligations (w2_t5), while Week 3 supplies the EIR-based borrowing carrying amount and unamortised fees (w3_t1) and the ECL allowance (w3_t2). Each needs a parallel tax base. In the Week 4 close, the tax provisioning step comes after all other adjustments: compute current tax from the provisional tax computation, update the temporary-difference table, prepare the DT roll-forward and ETR reconciliation, and post the tax entries. Building the tax-base columns into each schedule avoids reconstructing them at year-end.

Common pitfalls

P&L timing differences only

Computing DT only on P&L timing differences (old AS 22 approach)

Preparers trained on AS 22 list depreciation, 43B items and provisions, apply the rate and stop. They miss differences with no P&L timing counterpart: OCI remeasurements of FVOCI investments and gratuity, fair value step-ups in business combinations, the equity component of compound instruments, revaluation of PPE, the ROU/lease pair and outside-basis differences. The correct method is to list every balance-sheet asset and liability, determine its tax base and compute the difference (12.5). In review, reconcile the DT table to the balance sheet: every line of the balance sheet should either appear in the table or be documented as having no difference.

Using the current-year rate on reversal

Using current-year rate instead of the rate applicable on reversal (e.g., planned 115BAA switch)

DT is measured at the rates expected to apply when the asset is realised or liability settled, based on enacted or substantively enacted law (12.47). A company still under the old regime that has decided to opt for 115BAA from next year should measure differences reversing after the switch at 25.168%, not 34.944%. Similarly, capital gains DT uses capital gains rates, and LTCG DT on investments must reflect the July 2024 rates. Detect the error by reviewing board minutes and tax plans for regime decisions, and by checking whether the reversal schedule supports the rates applied.

DTA on losses without convincing evidence

Recognising DTA on losses without convincing evidence of future taxable profit

Loss-making companies often recognise DTA on carry-forward losses relying on business plans showing a turnaround. Ind AS 12.35 says the existence of unused tax losses is strong evidence that future taxable profit may not be available, so a company with a history of recent losses recognises DTA only to the extent of sufficient taxable temporary differences or convincing other evidence. Correct treatment: schedule the reversal of DTLs, assess loss expiry (8 years under Sec 72), and accept only realistic, supportable projections. Review by comparing past forecasts with actual results and checking the expiry profile.

OCI deferred tax in P&L

Taking DT on OCI remeasurements to P&L

When gratuity actuarial losses or FVOCI gains are booked in OCI, the deferred tax on them is often computed in the year-end total and posted entirely to the P&L tax charge. Ind AS 12.61A requires current and deferred tax relating to items recognised outside P&L to be recognised outside P&L — in OCI or directly in equity. The P&L tax expense is overstated or understated and OCI is shown gross of tax. Fix by splitting the DT movement in the roll-forward by source (P&L, OCI, equity). In review, compare the OCI section of the P&L with the DT roll-forward's OCI column.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full DT computation and roll-forward [L3]

Ojas Engineering Ltd (115BAA from FY 2020-21) has the following balances (₹ Cr) at 31 March 2025 and 31 March 2026: PPE carrying amount 120 / 132, tax WDV 95 / 101; ROU asset 18 / 15, lease liability 20 / 17.5 (tax base nil; rent deductible on payment); gratuity obligation 6.0 / 7.1 (of the increase, ₹0.4 Cr is actuarial loss in OCI); leave encashment provision 2.5 / 2.8; ECL allowance 3.0 / 3.9; FVOCI equity investments carrying 14 / 18, cost 10 (expected LTCG rate 14.3%); borrowings with unamortised fees 0.4 / 0.3. Prepare the temporary-difference table for both dates, the DT roll-forward split between P&L and OCI, and the journal entries for FY 2025-26.

💡 Hint: Use 25.168% for business items and the capital gains rate for the investment; split OCI items before computing the P&L charge.

Q2. Regime switch with MAT credit [L3]

Prisha Textiles Ltd is in the old regime (34.944%) with MAT credit of ₹6 Cr and a net DTL of ₹18 Cr (temporary differences ₹51.5 Cr). In February 2026 the board decides to opt for 115BAA from FY 2026-27 (the return for which will be filed in 2027). Its temporary differences are expected to reverse evenly over the next 5 years. Compute the remeasurement of the DTL, the treatment of MAT credit, and explain the timing of recognition and the effect on FY 2025-26 ETR.

Q3. ETR reconciliation with losses and capital gains [L2]

A company under 115BAA has PBT ₹80 Cr including ₹6 Cr long-term capital gain on sale of unlisted shares taxed at 14.3%, CSR expense ₹1.4 Cr, a penalty of ₹0.2 Cr, ₹3 Cr dividend income from a foreign subsidiary taxed at normal rates, and a DTA of ₹1.1 Cr on losses of a newly merged unit previously unrecognised now recognised. Prior-year current tax over-provision ₹0.5 Cr. Prepare the ETR reconciliation and compute the effective rate.

Q4. Interview: explaining DT to a CFO [L2]

The CFO of a mid-cap company asks why its deferred tax liability rose by ₹9 Cr even though profits were flat, and whether deferred tax "matters for cash". Using PPE, leases and gratuity as examples, draft a concise answer covering the balance-sheet approach, the effect of 115BAA and how DT is treated in a DCF valuation.
