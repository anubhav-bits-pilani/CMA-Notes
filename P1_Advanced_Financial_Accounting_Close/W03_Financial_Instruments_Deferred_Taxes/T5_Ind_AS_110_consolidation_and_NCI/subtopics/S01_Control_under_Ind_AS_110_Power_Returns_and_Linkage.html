Subtopic note

Control under Ind AS 110: Power, Returns and Linkage

Scope: Control (110.7): power over relevant activities, variable returns, link between them; de facto control, potential voting rights, structured entities

Why it matters

Control decides the consolidation perimeter — which entities are combined line by line, which are equity-accounted and which are simple investments. In India the perimeter question is live: promoter groups hold 40–49% stakes in listed companies with dispersed public shareholders, JVs give partners veto rights, CCDs and warrants carry potential voting rights, and NBFC securitisation trusts and AIF structures are classic structured entities. A wrong control conclusion changes revenue, debt, NCI and every ratio built on them; regulators and auditors review it closely, and SEBI LODR material-subsidiary tests (Reg 24) follow from it.

Core notes

The definition (110.6–7)

An investor controls an investee when it is exposed, or has rights, to variable returns from its involvement and has the ability to affect those returns through its power over the investee. Control requires all three elements (110.7):

  1. Power over the investee — existing rights that give the current ability to direct the relevant activities (110.10, B9–B54).
  2. Exposure, or rights, to variable returns from involvement (110.15–16, B55–B57).
  3. Ability to use its power to affect the amount of its returns — the link (110.17–18, B58–B72).

Control is reassessed whenever facts and circumstances indicate a change in any element (110.8, B80–B85).

Element 1: power over relevant activities

  • Relevant activities are those that significantly affect the investee's returns — sales and marketing, selecting/acquiring assets, managing financial assets, R&D, determining capital structure and funding (B11). When different investors direct different relevant activities, the one directing the activities that most significantly affect returns has power (B13).
  • Purpose and design of the investee helps identify relevant activities and how decisions are made (B5–B8).
  • Rights giving power (B15): voting rights; rights to appoint, reassign or remove key management personnel; rights to appoint or remove another entity that directs relevant activities; rights to direct the investee to enter into or veto transactions; management contracts.
  • Substantive vs protective rights. Only substantive rights count — the holder must have the practical ability to exercise them (B22–B25: barriers such as penalties, exercise price, lack of information, legal/regulatory constraints). Protective rights (e.g., lender covenants, minority vetoes over fundamental changes like amending the MoA, issuing shares, related-party transactions) protect interests but do not give power (B26–B28).
  • Majority voting rights normally give power, unless relevant activities are directed by another party through a contract, or the voting rights are not substantive (B35–B37).

De facto control (B41–B46)

An investor with less than a majority of voting rights can still have power if its rights are sufficient to give it the practical ability to direct relevant activities unilaterally. Consider:

  • the size of its holding relative to the size and dispersion of other holdings — 45% vs many holders of under 1% each is very different from 45% vs one holder of 40%;
  • potential voting rights held by it and others;
  • rights from contractual arrangements;
  • voting patterns at previous shareholder meetings — e.g., if attendance is typically 70% of shares, 40% gives a working majority of the votes cast.

Illustration. A promoter holds 42% of an NSE-listed company; the next largest shareholder (an MF) holds 6%; the rest is held by about 3 lakh retail and institutional holders. At the last five AGMs, average turnout was 68%, and no resolution backed by the promoter has failed. The promoter's 42% equals 62% of the shares typically voted. Absent a contrary arrangement, the promoter has de facto power — consolidation, not equity accounting.

Potential voting rights (B47–B50)

Options, warrants, convertible instruments (CCDs, CCPS, FCCBs) count only if substantive: the holder must have the practical ability to exercise when decisions need to be made. Factors: whether currently exercisable or exercisable before decisions are taken; exercise price (deep out-of-the-money rights are usually not substantive); financial ability; regulatory approvals (e.g., FDI conditions, CCI approval); whether exercise would benefit the holder (synergies). Unlike some older frameworks, Ind AS 110 does not require the rights to be currently exercisable — what matters is the ability to exercise when decisions about relevant activities are made (B24).

Example. Investor A holds 40%; it also holds CCDs convertible into a further 15% at any time at a price at fair value; conversion requires no approvals. A's potential 55% is substantive → A likely controls even before conversion.

Element 2: variable returns (B55–B57)

Returns that vary with performance: dividends, changes in investment value, interest on debt exposed to credit risk, management fees, tax benefits, residual interests on liquidation, synergies (cost savings, scarce supply, economies of scale). Even fixed interest is variable from the investee's credit risk perspective. Returns may be positive, negative or both.

Element 3: link — principal vs agent (B58–B72)

A decision maker with power must use it for its own benefit, not only for others. A fund manager or trustee acting as an agent does not control. Factors: scope of decision-making authority; rights held by other parties (a single party able to remove the manager without cause → agent, B65); remuneration (commensurate and at arm's length vs significant variable fee); and exposure to variability from other interests (co-investment). AMCs of Indian mutual funds typically conclude they are agents of the schemes (SEBI-mandated trustee removal rights, small seed investment), while a sponsor holding a large share of an AIF's units with discretionary management may be a principal.

Structured entities

A structured entity is designed so that voting rights are not the dominant factor in deciding who controls it — e.g., when voting rights relate to administrative tasks only and relevant activities are directed by contractual arrangements (Ind AS 112 Appendix A). Indian examples: securitisation trusts for NBFC loan pools (pass-through certificates), employee welfare trusts holding ESOP shares, InvIT/REIT SPVs, project SPVs governed by concession agreements. For these, focus on: purpose and design, who makes decisions in the contingent events that matter (e.g., default servicing), involvement in design, implicit commitments (liquidity support, credit enhancement), and exposure to variable returns (first-loss pieces, subordinated tranches). An NBFC that retains the first-loss credit enhancement and continues servicing with discretion over collections is likely to control the trust — the loans stay on its consolidated balance sheet.

ESOP trusts: ICAI/ITFG guidance indicates that where the company controls an employee welfare trust holding its own shares, the trust is consolidated (in standalone too, as an extension of the company) and shares held are treated as treasury shares.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • 50:50 JV with a casting vote for one partner on operational matters — likely control for that partner if those are the relevant activities.
  • Franchise rights — franchisor's rights are usually protective of the brand, not power over the franchisee (B29–B33).
  • Lenders with step-in rights — protective until a default; on default, rights may become substantive and trigger control reassessment (IBC: once a resolution professional takes over, the shareholder loses power).
  • Government-appointed boards — some PSU subsidiaries may have board rights held by ministries; analyse who directs relevant activities.
  • Silent partners with operational control — a 26% investor with management rights may control.
  • Loss of power on CIRP admission — when NCLT admits a subsidiary into CIRP, power passes to the RP/CoC; groups generally deconsolidate.

Key takeaways

  • Control = power + variable returns + link; all three are required and reassessed continuously.
  • Only substantive rights count; protective rights never give power.
  • De facto control arises from a large relative holding, dispersed others and voting patterns.
  • Potential voting rights count when substantive, considering price, ability and approvals.
  • Agents (fund managers removable by others, market-rate fees) do not consolidate.
  • Structured entities: analyse purpose, design, contingent decisions and first-loss exposure.

Practice Questions

Q1. De facto control assessment [L2]

Investor X holds 38% of a listed company. Other holdings: Y 18%, Z 9%, remainder dispersed among 2 lakh holders. AGM attendance averaged 72% over 4 years; Y and Z have voted together twice against X and won once. X nominates 4 of 10 directors. Assess whether X controls, listing the evidence that would change your conclusion.

💡 Hint: Compute X's share of votes typically cast and consider whether Y and Z act together.

Q2. Potential voting rights [L2]

A holds 30% of B and warrants for a further 25%, exercisable from 1 July 2026 at ₹500 per share (current share price ₹320). B's next board meeting deciding the annual budget and capex is in May 2026. Analyse whether the warrants are substantive and whether A controls B at 31 March 2026.

Q3. Securitisation trust [L3]

An NBFC assigns ₹500 Cr of vehicle loans to a trust that issues senior PTCs (₹440 Cr) to banks; the NBFC provides a cash collateral of ₹50 Cr (first loss) and subscribes to ₹30 Cr of subordinated PTCs. It services the loans for a fee of 0.5% and decides on repossession and restructuring. Analyse control of the trust under Ind AS 110 and the consequences for the NBFC's consolidated balance sheet.

Q4. Principal or agent? [L2]

An AMC manages a Category II AIF with ₹1,000 Cr commitments; it holds 2% units, receives a 2% management fee and 20% carry above an 8% hurdle; investors can remove the manager by 75% vote without cause. Assess whether the AMC controls the AIF.

Q5. Protective vs substantive rights [L1]

Classify the following rights held by a 26% investor as protective or substantive: veto over annual budget; veto over issuing new shares; right to appoint the CFO; veto over related-party transactions above ₹10 Cr; veto over winding-up.
