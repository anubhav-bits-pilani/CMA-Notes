Subtopic note

Classification Flow: Business Model Test, Then SPPI Test

Scope: Classification flow: business model (hold to collect / hold to collect and sell / other) at portfolio level, then SPPI test on contractual terms

Why it matters

Every financial asset on an Indian balance sheet — trade receivables, inter-corporate deposits, G-secs, bonds, mutual fund units, CCDs, loans to subsidiaries — must be classified on initial recognition, and the classification fixes where gains and losses go for the life of the asset. Auditors test classification memos as a key judgement area for treasury-heavy companies, NBFCs and holding companies. A wrong conclusion moves fair value changes between P&L and OCI, distorts finance income, and can change the ECL scope (FVTPL assets carry no ECL). In modelling, classification determines whether treasury income in a forecast is stable interest or volatile mark-to-market.

Core notes

The two tests and their order

Ind AS 109.4.1.1 classifies a financial asset on the basis of both (a) the entity's business model for managing the financial assets and (b) the contractual cash flow characteristics of the asset. The combinations are:

Business model Cash flows SPPI? Classification
Hold to collect contractual cash flows (4.1.2) Yes Amortised cost
Hold to collect and sell (4.1.2A) Yes FVOCI (debt, with recycling)
Other — trading, managed on fair value basis, or primarily selling (4.1.4) Either FVTPL
Any No FVTPL

Equity instruments never pass SPPI (they have no principal or interest), so they are FVTPL unless the irrevocable FVOCI election in 5.7.5 is made for a non-trading holding. Derivatives are FVTPL (except designated hedging instruments).

In practice teams usually assess the business model first because it is a portfolio-level judgement made once and then applied to all assets in that portfolio; SPPI is then tested instrument by instrument. Either order gives the same result because both conditions must be met for amortised cost or FVOCI.

The business model test

The business model is determined by key management personnel (as defined in Ind AS 24) and reflects how groups of financial assets are managed together to achieve a particular objective (B4.1.1–B4.1.2). It is a matter of fact observed through activities, not a statement of intent for a single instrument. Evidence includes:

  • How performance of the portfolio is evaluated and reported to KMP (interest yield versus total return).
  • The risks that affect performance and how they are managed.
  • How managers are compensated (on fair value gains or on collected cash flows).
  • The frequency, volume, timing and reasons for past sales, and expectations of future sales (B4.1.2B).

One entity can have several business models: a company may hold trade receivables to collect, keep a liquidity buffer of G-secs to collect and sell, and run a trading book. The level of aggregation is a portfolio or sub-portfolio, not the entity as a whole and not a single instrument (B4.1.2).

Hold to collect. The objective is to collect contractual cash flows over the life of the instrument. Sales are not prohibited. Sales are consistent with this model if they are due to an increase in credit risk (B4.1.3A), are infrequent (even if significant in value), or are insignificant in value individually and in aggregate (even if frequent), or occur close to maturity where proceeds approximate remaining contractual cash flows (B4.1.3B). A trade-receivables portfolio held until customers pay is the classic example. A factoring programme where receivables are sold and derecognised under a regular non-recourse arrangement (for example TReDS discounting of a large share of receivables) may indicate a hold-to-collect-and-sell or other model for that portfolio.

Hold to collect and sell. Both collecting contractual cash flows and selling are integral to the objective (B4.1.4A). Typically there are greater frequency and volume of sales than in hold to collect; examples include a liquidity portfolio managed to meet day-to-day needs, or an insurer matching the duration of assets to liabilities. There is no threshold for sales frequency.

Other. Assets managed with the objective of realising cash flows through sale, managed and evaluated on a fair value basis, or held for trading (B4.1.5–B4.1.6). Collecting interest is incidental.

The business model is assessed on reasonably expected scenarios, not worst-case or stress scenarios (B4.1.2A). If cash flows are later realised differently from expectations (for example, an unexpected fire-sale), that does not cause a prior-period error or a reclassification of existing assets, but it informs the classification of new assets.

The SPPI test

Contractual cash flows must be solely payments of principal and interest on the principal amount outstanding (4.1.2(b), 4.1.3). Principal is the fair value of the asset at initial recognition and can change over its life through repayments. Interest is consideration for the time value of money, credit risk, other basic lending risks (liquidity), costs (servicing, administrative) and a profit margin (B4.1.7A). Cash flows consistent with a basic lending arrangement pass; any exposure to risks or volatility unrelated to a basic lending arrangement — equity prices, commodity prices, borrower profit — fails (B4.1.7A).

The test is performed in the currency in which the asset is denominated, on contractual terms, at initial recognition. Contractual features to examine include:

  • Modified time value of money (B4.1.9B–B4.1.9D): a rate reset to a tenor mismatch (e.g., a one-year rate reset monthly) requires a benchmark comparison; the asset fails if undiscounted cash flows could differ significantly from a benchmark instrument.
  • Prepayment and extension options (B4.1.11–B4.1.12): pass if the prepayment amount substantially represents unpaid principal and interest plus reasonable compensation; prepayment features with negative compensation can pass.
  • Contingent events that change timing or amount of cash flows (B4.1.10): pass if the resulting cash flows remain SPPI (e.g., a step-up in spread on a covenant breach reflecting increased credit risk).
  • De minimis and non-genuine features (B4.1.18): a feature that could only have a de minimis effect, or that is not genuine (only affects cash flows on an extremely rare and highly abnormal event), does not affect classification.
  • Non-recourse and contractually linked instruments (B4.1.15–B4.1.17, B4.1.20–B4.1.26): look through to the underlying assets.

Worked flow for an Indian corporate

Tarun Steels Ltd holds three portfolios at 1 April 2025:

Portfolio Evidence Business model SPPI Classification
Trade receivables ₹180 Cr Collected on due dates; no sales except occasional bill discounting with recourse Hold to collect Yes (no interest, short-term) Amortised cost
Treasury bonds ₹40 Cr (PSU bonds, 7.4%) Treasury reports yield and fair value; sells bonds when capex needs arise, ~35% turnover p.a. Hold to collect and sell Yes FVOCI-debt
Arbitrage fund units ₹15 Cr Redeemable at NAV Not relevant No (NAV-linked) FVTPL

Recourse bill discounting does not derecognise the receivable (the bank has recourse), so it is not a "sale" for business-model purposes.

Documentation

A classification memo should state the portfolio, the KMP evaluation and reporting evidence, the sales history for the past three years with reasons, the SPPI analysis of each instrument type with the specific contractual features reviewed, and the conclusion. For a large portfolio, document SPPI by instrument template (e.g., standard ICD agreement) rather than for each contract.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Sales pattern drifts. A hold-to-collect bond portfolio that starts showing regular sales for capex funding should prompt reassessment for new purchases; existing assets are not reclassified unless the business model itself changes (s08).
  • Inter-corporate deposits with a profit-linked kicker fail SPPI even if the base rate is market.
  • Credit-related sales shortly before a downgrade are consistent with hold to collect, but the policy defining "increase in credit risk" must exist and be applied consistently.
  • Factoring on TReDS without recourse for a defined customer segment can make that sub-portfolio hold-to-collect-and-sell; the remaining receivables stay at amortised cost.
  • Group-level vs entity-level. A subsidiary's portfolio may be managed by group treasury; the business model is assessed by the reporting entity's KMP for standalone accounts, which can differ from the consolidated view.
  • Foreign-currency bonds are tested for SPPI in their own currency; FX movements do not cause failure.

Key takeaways

  • Debt assets need both a hold-to-collect (or collect-and-sell) business model and SPPI cash flows to avoid FVTPL.
  • The business model is a portfolio-level, factual assessment by KMP, evidenced by reporting, compensation and sales history.
  • Infrequent, insignificant, credit-driven or near-maturity sales are consistent with hold to collect.
  • SPPI asks whether cash flows reflect only basic lending risks; any equity, commodity or profit linkage fails.
  • Unexpected sales do not reclassify existing assets but inform future classification.
  • Document the analysis by portfolio and instrument template; it is a recurring audit focus.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classifying a corporate treasury book [L2]

Meghna Pharma Ltd's treasury holds ₹60 Cr of AAA corporate bonds. In the last three years it sold ₹4 Cr, ₹22 Cr and ₹19 Cr of bonds, mainly to fund acquisitions; the treasury head's bonus depends on portfolio yield and total return. The bonds pay fixed 7.8% coupons with a prepayment option at par plus accrued interest. Determine the business model and SPPI conclusion and the resulting classification, citing the relevant Ind AS 109 paragraphs.

💡 Hint: Weigh the frequency and reasons of sales together with how performance is reported and rewarded.

Q2. Sales close to maturity and credit-driven sales [L1]

A hold-to-collect portfolio of NCDs had two sales in FY 2025-26: ₹3 Cr sold one month before maturity at a price close to remaining contractual cash flows, and ₹5 Cr sold after the issuer was downgraded from AA to BBB. State whether each sale is consistent with a hold-to-collect business model and why.

Q3. SPPI on loan terms [L2]

Assess SPPI for each of the following loans given by an NBFC: (a) MCLR-linked loan reset quarterly to 3-month MCLR plus 2%; (b) loan with interest of 9% plus 1% of borrower's EBITDA; (c) loan where the rate is reset monthly to the 1-year MCLR; (d) loan with a 2% spread step-up if the borrower's rating falls below BBB; (e) loan whose repayment is linked to the price of Brent crude.

💡 Hint: For (c), think about the modified time value of money benchmark test.

Q4. Receivables discounting programme [L3]

Sunrise Auto Components Ltd discounts about 45% of receivables from two OEM customers on TReDS without recourse each month, and collects the remaining receivables on due dates. Other customers' receivables are never sold. Explain how you would define portfolios, which business model applies to each, the measurement consequences, and what evidence the auditor would ask for.
