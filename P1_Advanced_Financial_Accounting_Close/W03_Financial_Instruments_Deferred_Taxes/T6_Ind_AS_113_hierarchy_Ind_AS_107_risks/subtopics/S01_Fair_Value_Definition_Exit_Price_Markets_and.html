Fair Value Definition: Exit Price, Markets and Participants

Scope: Fair value (113.9): exit price, orderly transaction, principal/most advantageous market, market-participant assumptions

Why it matters

Fair value appears across this week's work: PPA fair values (Ind AS 103), FVTPL/FVOCI instruments (Ind AS 109), earn-out liabilities, FVLCD for impairment (Ind AS 36), investment property fair value disclosure (Ind AS 40) and retained interests on loss of control (Ind AS 110). Ind AS 113 provides one definition and one framework for all of them — it does not say when to use fair value, only how to measure and disclose it. Disputes with auditors and valuers often reduce to the four building blocks of the definition: is this an exit price, from an orderly transaction, in the right market, using market-participant (not entity-specific) assumptions?

Core notes

The definition (113.9)

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.

Key features:

  • Market-based, not entity-specific (113.2). The entity's intentions (e.g., to hold an asset, or to settle a liability rather than transfer it) are irrelevant.
  • Exit price (113.24) — the price to sell the asset or transfer the liability, not the price paid to acquire it (entry price). At initial recognition the two are often equal but not always (113.57–60).
  • Measurement date — current conditions at that date; hindsight is not permitted.
  • Asset or liability specific characteristics (113.11) — condition, location, restrictions on sale or use, if a market participant would consider them. A restriction attached to the asset (e.g., a legal lock-in on shares) is considered; a restriction specific to the entity (a contractual agreement not to sell) is not.
  • Unit of account — determined by the Ind AS requiring the measurement (e.g., individual share under Ind AS 109); a blockage factor (discount for selling a large holding) is not permitted for Level 1 (113.69, 113.80).

Orderly transaction

An orderly transaction assumes exposure to the market for a period before the measurement date to allow usual and customary marketing activities; it is not a forced transaction (forced liquidation or distress sale) (Appendix A). When market activity has significantly decreased (B37–B47), observed prices may not be orderly — e.g., prices during the March 2020 COVID dislocation in Indian corporate bonds, or prices from a single distressed seller. Entities must assess whether transactions are orderly (B43) and weight them accordingly, possibly using an income approach. A decline in volume alone does not mean prices are not fair value.

Principal and most advantageous market (113.16–21)

  • Principal market — the market with the greatest volume and level of activity for the asset or liability. If there is a principal market, fair value is measured using its price, even if a price in another market is more advantageous at the measurement date (113.18).
  • Most advantageous market — used only if there is no principal market: the market that maximises the amount received for the asset (or minimises the amount paid for the liability), after transaction costs and transport costs.
  • The entity must have access to the market at the measurement date (113.19). It need not be able to sell the asset on that date.
  • Absent evidence to the contrary, the market where the entity normally transacts is presumed to be the principal market (113.17). The entity need not perform an exhaustive search.
  • Different entities (with different access) may have different principal markets for the same asset (113.19).

Transaction costs and transport costs (113.25–26)

  • Transaction costs are used to identify the most advantageous market, but the fair value is not adjusted for them — they are a characteristic of the transaction, not the asset.
  • Transport costs — if location is a characteristic of the asset (e.g., commodities), fair value is adjusted for costs to transport it from its current location to the principal (or most advantageous) market.

Illustration. An Indian refiner holds 10,000 tonnes of a metal at its Gujarat plant. Market A (LME-linked import parity, Singapore): price ₹6.20 lakh/tonne, transaction cost ₹0.08 lakh, transport ₹0.25 lakh. Market B (domestic MCX-linked physical market, Mumbai): price ₹6.05 lakh, transaction cost ₹0.03 lakh, transport ₹0.04 lakh.

Price 6.20 6.05
Less transport (0.25) (0.04)

Net received 5.87 5.98
  • If Market B is the principal market (greatest volume for this entity): FV = 6.05 − 0.04 = ₹6.01 lakh (transport deducted, transaction costs not).
  • If no principal market, Market B is most advantageous (net 5.98 > 5.87), FV again = ₹6.01 lakh.

Market participants (113.22–23)

Fair value uses the assumptions market participants would use when pricing the asset or liability, assuming they act in their economic best interest. Market participants are buyers and sellers in the principal (or most advantageous) market who are:

  • independent of each other (not related parties under Ind AS 24, though a related-party price may be used if at market terms);
  • knowledgeable — reasonable understanding using all available information, including from customary due diligence;
  • able to enter into a transaction; and
  • willing — motivated but not forced or compelled.

The entity does not need to identify specific market participants but should identify characteristics distinguishing them: the asset/liability, the principal market, and market participants with whom it would transact. Examples: synergies available to any strategic buyer are included; synergies unique to the reporting entity are excluded.

Fair value at initial recognition (113.57–60)

The transaction price is often fair value at initial recognition, but not where: the transaction is between related parties; under duress; the unit of account differs (e.g., a bundled purchase); or the market differs from the principal market (B4). If another Ind AS requires initial FV measurement and it differs from transaction price, the day-1 gain/loss is recognised in P&L unless the standard specifies otherwise — under Ind AS 109 B5.1.2A, a day-1 difference is deferred unless the FV is evidenced by a Level 1 price or a technique using only observable data (→ c05).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Unlisted shares with a pre-emption clause — the restriction travels with the shares (asset-specific) and affects fair value; a lock-in signed by the holder alone does not.
  • Thinly traded SME-platform shares — quoted prices may not represent an active market; assess volume and frequency.
  • Intra-day vs closing price — the convention (closing price) should be applied consistently.
  • Government-mandated prices (e.g., NPPA-controlled drug prices) — reflect them as market conditions affecting all participants.
  • Group-specific synergies in PPA — excluded from asset fair values; they end up in goodwill.
  • Distressed bond market — don't ignore observed prices entirely; weight them and document the orderly-transaction assessment.

Key takeaways

  • Fair value is a market-based exit price at the measurement date in an orderly transaction.
  • Use the principal market if one exists; otherwise the most advantageous market after transaction and transport costs.
  • Do not deduct transaction costs from fair value; do deduct transport costs where location matters.
  • Assumptions are those of independent, knowledgeable, able and willing market participants — not the entity's own.
  • Asset-specific restrictions affect fair value; entity-specific restrictions do not.
  • Transaction price may differ from fair value at initial recognition; Ind AS 109 restricts day-1 gains.

Practice Questions

Q1. Principal vs most advantageous market [L2]

An exporter holds 500 tonnes of cardamom in Kerala. Market X (Bodinayakanur auction): price ₹2,450/kg, auction charges 1.5%, transport ₹15/kg; Market Y (Dubai): price equivalent ₹2,600/kg, brokerage 2%, transport and export costs ₹110/kg. 85% of the exporter's sales historically go through Market X. Compute fair value per kg and explain the market selected.

💡 Hint: Decide first whether a principal market exists for this entity.

Q2. Restriction on sale [L2]

An entity holds shares of an NSE-listed company under a SEBI-mandated 18-month promoter lock-in following an IPO, and separately holds shares of the same company that it has contractually agreed with a lender not to sell for 12 months. Explain how each restriction affects fair value under 113.11 and Appendix A.

Q3. Orderly transactions in a dislocated market [L3]

During a credit event, an NBFC's bonds traded only three times in a month, all sales by one mutual fund facing redemption pressure, at yields 400 bps above the issuer's previous trades. As the valuation head of an investing company, describe how you would determine fair value at month-end, citing B37–B47 factors.

Q4. Entity-specific vs market-participant assumptions [L1]

In a PPA, the acquirer plans to discontinue an acquired brand (to avoid competition with its own). It argues the brand's fair value should be nil. Evaluate the argument under Ind AS 113 and Ind AS 103.
