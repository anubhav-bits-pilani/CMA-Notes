Valuation Techniques and Calibration to Transaction Price

Scope: Techniques (113.61–66): market, income (DCF, option pricing), cost; calibration to transaction price

Why it matters

Most fair values in an Indian group's balance sheet outside listed equity come from models: unlisted equity stakes in start-ups, CCPS and CCDs, earn-outs, PPA intangibles, investment property disclosures, ESOP valuations and derivative positions. Ind AS 113 does not prescribe a technique; it requires the entity to use techniques that are appropriate in the circumstances, for which sufficient data are available, and that maximise relevant observable inputs (113.61, 113.67). Understanding the three approaches — and calibration — lets a finance professional challenge valuer reports, explain fair value movements to the audit committee and build defensible Level 3 models.

Core notes

General requirements (113.61–66)

  • Use valuation techniques appropriate in the circumstances and for which sufficient data are available, maximising relevant observable inputs and minimising unobservable inputs (113.61, 113.67).
  • Single or multiple techniques — if multiple techniques are used, evaluate the results considering the reasonableness of the range; the fair value is the point within the range that is most representative (113.63).
  • Consistency — techniques are applied consistently; a change in technique (or weighting) is appropriate if it results in an equally or more representative measurement (e.g., new markets develop, new information becomes available) and is accounted for as a change in accounting estimate under Ind AS 8 (113.65–66).
  • Inputs — based on market-participant assumptions; premiums/discounts (control premium, DLOM) only if consistent with the unit of account and characteristics a market participant would consider (113.69). Bid-ask spreads: the price within the bid-ask spread that is most representative of fair value; mid-market pricing is allowed as a practical expedient (113.70–71).

Market approach (B5–B7)

Uses prices and other relevant information from market transactions involving identical or comparable assets, liabilities or groups (a business).

  • Trading multiples — EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, P/E, P/B of listed comparables, adjusted for size, growth, margins and risk.
  • Transaction multiples — from recent M&A deals.
  • Matrix pricing — for debt securities, based on the security's relationship to benchmark quoted securities (B7).
  • Recent transactions in the entity's own shares (funding rounds) — with adjustment for rights differences (liquidation preference), elapsed time and market movements.

Illustration. Unlisted SaaS investee: FY26 revenue ₹120 Cr; listed peer median EV/Revenue 6.0×; size and growth discount 25%; net cash ₹40 Cr; the holding is 8% of equity; DLOM 15%.

\[ \text{EV} = 120 \times 6.0 \times 0.75 = 540; \quad \text{Equity} = 540 + 40 = 580 \]

\[ \text{FV of 8\%} = 580 \times 8\% \times (1 - 0.15) = 39.4 \text{ Cr} \]

Income approach (B10–B30)

Converts future amounts (cash flows or income and expenses) to a single current (discounted) amount.

  • Present value techniques (B12–B30) — discount rate adjustment technique (single most-likely cash flow, risk-adjusted rate) or expected present value technique (probability-weighted cash flows; risk in either the cash flows — certainty equivalent — or the rate). Components: estimates of future cash flows, expectations about variations in amount and timing, time value (risk-free rate), price for bearing uncertainty (risk premium), other factors (liquidity), and non-performance risk for liabilities (B13).
  • DCF of a business — free cash flows to firm discounted at WACC, terminal value via Gordon growth or exit multiple; equity value = EV − net debt; adjust for minority/liquidity.
  • Option pricing models — Black–Scholes–Merton, binomial/lattice, Monte Carlo — for options, warrants, ESOPs, convertible instruments, earn-outs with non-linear payoffs; and the OPM back-solve to allocate equity value across preference share classes in start-ups.
  • MEEM — for intangibles (w3_t4 c06).

Cost approach (B8–B9)

Reflects the amount that would be required currently to replace the service capacity of an asset — current replacement cost, adjusted for physical deterioration, functional (technological) obsolescence and economic (external) obsolescence. From a market participant seller's perspective, the price received is based on the cost to a buyer to acquire or construct a substitute asset of comparable utility. Typical uses: plant and machinery in PPAs, specialised buildings, internally developed software, assembled workforce.

Calibration (113.64)

If the transaction price is fair value at initial recognition and a valuation technique that uses unobservable inputs will be used to measure fair value subsequently, the technique must be calibrated so that at initial recognition its result equals the transaction price.

Purpose: calibration ensures the technique reflects current market conditions and helps identify needed adjustments (e.g., a characteristic of the asset not captured). After initial recognition, the calibrated inputs (e.g., implied discount for illiquidity, implied risk premium) are rolled forward and updated for changes in market conditions.

Illustration. An entity buys 5% of an unlisted fintech in a Series C round for ₹50 Cr (post-money equity ₹1,000 Cr). Its valuation model (DCF) at the same date gives equity value ₹1,250 Cr at a 16% discount rate. Calibration: solve for the discount rate (or a calibration adjustment) that gives ₹1,000 Cr — say 19.5%. At the next reporting date, update cash flows and market rates, but retain the calibrated premium (3.5 pp over the model's base rate) unless evidence supports changing it. If a new funding round occurs at a different price, recalibrate, adjusting for rights differences between the round's instrument and the entity's instrument.

Technique selection guide

Asset/liability Primary technique Cross-check
Listed equity (active market) Quoted price (Level 1) — no technique —
Unlisted equity, mature company DCF; trading multiples Transaction multiples
Start-up preference shares Recent round, back-solve with OPM Multiples on revenue
Corporate bonds (illiquid) Matrix pricing / DCF at spread Dealer quotes
FX forwards, IRS DCF using observable curves Counterparty valuations
Earn-out liability Expected PV / Monte Carlo Scenario analysis
Plant and machinery Cost approach (DRC) Market for second-hand assets
Brand Relief-from-royalty Market transactions

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Funding-round price as fair value — usually a good starting point but requires adjustment for preference rights, time since round, and market movements; not automatically Level 2 (topic pitfall).
  • Weighting multiple techniques — weights must reflect reliability, not convenience; document why.
  • Economic obsolescence in the cost approach — mandatory when the business earns below its cost of capital (key in bargain purchases).
  • DLOM and control premium — double-counting is common: a DCF on a controlling basis then a further control premium.
  • Changing technique to "smooth" results — only permissible if more representative; disclosed under 113.93(d).
  • Model governance — independent price verification, valuation committee review and back-testing support auditability.

Key takeaways

  • Three approaches: market (prices and multiples), income (DCF, option pricing, MEEM) and cost (current replacement cost less obsolescence).
  • Maximise relevant observable inputs; techniques are chosen for appropriateness and data availability.
  • Multiple techniques require evaluation of the range, not a mechanical average.
  • Changes in technique are changes in estimate and must be disclosed.
  • Calibrate Level 3 models to the transaction price at initial recognition and roll calibrated inputs forward.
  • Premiums and discounts must be consistent with the unit of account.

Practice Questions

Q1. Market approach for an unlisted stake [L2]

An investor holds 12% of an unlisted specialty chemicals company with FY26 EBITDA ₹85 Cr, net debt ₹60 Cr. Listed peers trade at EV/EBITDA 18×–24× (median 21×). The investee is smaller, so a 20% discount applies. DLOM 12%. Compute the fair value of the stake and identify the hierarchy level with reasons.

Q2. Calibration back-solve [L3]

An entity invests ₹30 Cr for 3% of a start-up (post-money ₹1,000 Cr). Its DCF using a 22% discount rate gives ₹1,180 Cr. Six months later, updated cash flows with the same base rate give ₹1,240 Cr, and market multiples of peers have fallen 10%. Describe the calibration approach at initial recognition and how you would determine fair value at the later date, and list the disclosures required.

💡 Hint: Calibrate an adjustment at inception and roll it forward with updated market evidence.

Q3. Cost approach in a PPA [L2]

A plant acquired in a business combination has a replacement cost new of ₹400 Cr. Physical deterioration is 35%, functional obsolescence ₹20 Cr, and the business's capacity utilisation is 60% versus a normal 85% (economic obsolescence via a scale exponent of 0.7). Compute the fair value using the cost approach.

Q4. Interview: choosing techniques [L1]

Explain to an audit committee member why the fair value of the company's unlisted investment changed by 30% in a year in which the investee's revenue grew 20%, referring to the valuation approach, inputs and calibration.
