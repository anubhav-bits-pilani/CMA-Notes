Subtopic note

Step 3: Variable Consideration and Estimation Methods

Scope: Step 3: variable consideration — rebates, volume discounts, liquidated damages, price variation clauses, performance bonuses; expected value vs most likely amount

Why it matters

Few Indian contracts have a truly fixed price. FMCG and pharma companies give distributor rebates and trade schemes; auto component makers face annual price-downs and steel-linked price variation; EPC contractors carry liquidated damages (LDs) and early-completion bonuses; IT services firms have SLA credits and gain-share fees. Under Ind AS 115 all of these are variable consideration that must be estimated at contract inception and at each reporting date and included in revenue — subject to the constraint (s05). The estimate directly changes reported revenue and margin, and the India-specific 115.126AA reconciliation (s08) makes each adjustment visible.

Core notes

What is variable consideration (115.50–51)

If the consideration promised includes a variable amount, the entity estimates the amount to which it will be entitled in exchange for transferring the promised goods or services (115.50). Variability arises from discounts, rebates, refunds, credits, price concessions, incentives, performance bonuses, penalties or other similar items, and when entitlement is contingent on a future event (e.g., a product sold with a right of return, or a fixed fee promised as a bonus on achieving a milestone) (115.51). Variability can be explicit in the contract or implicit — customary business practice, published policies or statements create a valid expectation of a price concession, or the entity intends to accept a lower price (115.52).

The types you will meet

Rebates and volume discounts. Retrospective rebates (e.g., 2% of all purchases if annual purchases exceed ₹50 crore) are variable consideration: estimate the expected rebate and reduce revenue on each sale, recognising a refund liability (115.55) — not a provision. Prospective tiered pricing (lower price only on units beyond a threshold) may instead create a material right (s03). Distributor trade schemes, cash discounts for early payment and "secondary scheme" reimbursements are also variable consideration.

Liquidated damages (LDs) and penalties. LDs for late completion reduce the transaction price — they are a price adjustment, not an expense — unless they represent compensation for a distinct good or service received from the customer (rare). In Indian EPC contracts LDs are typically 0.5% of contract value per week of delay, capped at 5–10%. Estimate expected LDs based on the project schedule; reassess as the schedule changes.

Price variation clauses (PVC). Escalation formulas linking the price to indices (WPI, steel, cement, copper, labour indices published by RBI/Labour Bureau, or IEEMA formulas for electrical equipment) are variable consideration: estimate the expected index movement over the contract, or include amounts as the index is published, applying the constraint (index volatility is outside the entity's influence).

Performance bonuses. Early-completion bonuses, gain-share or KPI-based fees in IT and BPO contracts, and success fees in advisory are variable. Binary bonuses are usually estimated with the most likely amount.

Other: SLA credits (IT services), right of return (retail, pharma expiry returns — recognise refund liability and an asset for goods expected to be returned, B20–B27), price concessions to state customers, MRP-linked trade margins, and price protection clauses for distributors.

Estimation methods (115.53)

Estimate variable consideration using whichever method the entity expects to better predict the amount:

  • (a) Expected value — the sum of probability-weighted amounts in a range of possible amounts. Appropriate when the entity has a large number of contracts with similar characteristics (portfolio data) or many possible outcomes.
  • (b) Most likely amount — the single most likely amount in a range. Appropriate when the contract has only two possible outcomes (bonus achieved or not; LD triggered or not).

The method is applied consistently throughout the contract (115.54), and the entity considers all reasonably available information (historical, current and forecast). Different methods may be used for different uncertainties within the same contract (e.g., expected value for a rebate pool, most likely amount for a completion bonus).

Worked illustration: EPC contract

Rewa Buildtech Ltd signs a ₹200 crore highway EPC contract. Variable elements:

  • LD: ₹2 crore per month of delay, capped at ₹10 crore. Management's schedule analysis: on time 50%; 1-month delay 30%; 2-month delay 15%; 3-month delay 5%.
  • Bonus: ₹6 crore if completed 2 months early. Management assesses 25% probability.
  • PVC: steel escalation estimated at ₹3.5 crore based on current index, with significant volatility.

LD (many possible outcomes — expected value):

\[ E[\text{LD}] = 0.5 \times 0 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.15 \times 4 + 0.05 \times 6 = 1.5 \text{ crore} \]

Bonus (binary — most likely amount): most likely outcome is no bonus → ₹0.

PVC: estimate ₹3.5 crore, then apply the constraint (s05) — perhaps only the portion already reflected in published indices.

Estimated transaction price before constraint:

\[ 200 - 1.5 + 0 + 3.5 = 202.0 \text{ crore} \]

Revenue recognised each period = % complete × constrained transaction price; changes in the estimate are cumulative catch-ups (115.88).

Worked illustration: FMCG distributor rebate

A distributor earns a rebate of 1% if annual purchases ≥ ₹30 crore, 2% if ≥ ₹40 crore (retrospective on all purchases). Based on 3 years of data across 120 similar distributors: 20% achieve nil, 50% achieve 1%, 30% achieve 2%. Using expected value across the portfolio: expected rebate rate = 0.5 × 1% + 0.3 × 2% = 1.1%. On Q1 sales of ₹9 crore to this distributor, revenue = ₹9 crore × (1 − 1.1%) = ₹8.901 crore; refund liability ₹9.9 lakh. If the distributor's own trajectory makes the 2% tier highly likely, a most-likely-amount estimate for that distributor may better predict the amount — the choice must be justified and applied consistently.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • LDs paid to the customer vs deducted from bills: both reduce revenue; an LD capped and agreed in an extension-of-time (EOT) approval is a modification/price change.
  • Penalties for breach of non-performance obligations (e.g., environmental fines payable to the government) are not consideration payable to the customer — expense under Ind AS 37.
  • Claims against the customer (contractor's claims for client-caused delays) are variable consideration or modifications; recognise only to the extent the constraint allows.
  • Refund liability vs contract liability: rebates and expected returns create refund liabilities (financial liabilities if cash-settled); presentation should not net them against trade receivables unless the right to offset exists.
  • Price-downs agreed after year-end relating to prior-year sales (auto components): if the customer's practice creates an expectation of retrospective price-downs, estimate them at the reporting date (implicit variable consideration, 115.52).
  • GST: revenue excludes GST; credit notes for rebates must meet Sec 34 CGST conditions to reduce output tax — the tax and accounting treatments may differ in timing.

Key takeaways

  • Variable consideration includes rebates, discounts, LDs, penalties, PVC, bonuses, SLA credits, returns and implicit price concessions.
  • Estimate at inception and every reporting date using expected value (portfolios, many outcomes) or most likely amount (binary outcomes) — whichever better predicts.
  • Apply the chosen method consistently through the contract; different uncertainties may use different methods.
  • Rebates and returns create refund liabilities; LDs reduce the transaction price, not other expenses.
  • Every estimate then passes through the constraint (s05).

Practice Questions

Q1. Choose the method [L1]

For each variable element, state the method you would use and why: (a) 3% retrospective rebate to each of 400 retailers if annual purchases exceed ₹25 lakh; (b) a ₹5 crore bonus if a metro station is handed over by 30 September 2026; (c) SLA credits of 0–10% of monthly fees on an IT outsourcing contract, with 5 years of history; (d) a binary LD of ₹3 crore if a factory is not commissioned by 31 December 2026.

Q2. Rebate accounting through the year [L2]

A pharma company sells ₹12 crore to a distributor in Q1, ₹15 crore in Q2 and ₹18 crore in Q3 of FY 2025-26. Retrospective rebate: 1.5% if annual purchases ≥ ₹50 crore; 3% if ≥ ₹65 crore. At the end of Q1 the expected annual purchases are ₹55 crore; at the end of Q3, ₹68 crore. Compute revenue for each quarter and the refund liability at the end of Q3, showing the catch-up in Q3.

💡 Hint: Re-estimate the rebate rate on cumulative sales at each quarter-end and book the difference.

Q3. EPC transaction price [L2]

An EPC contract has a fixed price of ₹480 crore, an LD of 0.5% per week of delay capped at 5%, and a bonus of ₹8 crore for completion 4 weeks early. Probabilities: 4 weeks early 10%; on time 40%; 4 weeks late 30%; 8 weeks late 20%. Compute the expected LD and the most likely bonus, and determine the estimated transaction price before the constraint. Explain why you might use different methods for the LD and the bonus.

Q4. Auto-component price-downs [L3]

An auto-component supplier's OEM customer historically negotiates a 2–3% retrospective price-down on the full year's supplies, finalised in June after year-end, although the contract is silent. FY 2025-26 supplies are ₹600 crore. The CFO wants to recognise the price-down in FY 2026-27 when agreed. Prepare a memo under Ind AS 115.51–53 and 115.56, recommending the FY 2025-26 accounting and disclosures, and describe how you would estimate the amount.
