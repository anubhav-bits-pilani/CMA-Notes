Subtopic note

Constraining Variable Consideration (Ind AS 115.56–58)

Scope: Constraint on variable consideration (115.56–58): include only to extent highly probable of no significant reversal; reassess each reporting date

Why it matters

Estimating variable consideration (s04) is only half the job. The constraint decides how much of that estimate can actually be recognised. It is the main defence against front-loading revenue from uncertain bonuses, claims, escalations and success fees, and the main source of EPC and IT services revenue adjustments in Indian audits. It also explains why a contractor's order book value and its revenue can diverge for years, and why revenue can jump when a claim is settled.

Core notes

The rule (115.56)

An entity includes in the transaction price some or all of the estimated variable consideration only to the extent that it is highly probable that a significant reversal in the amount of cumulative revenue recognised will not occur when the uncertainty associated with the variable consideration is subsequently resolved.

Parse the words:

  • "Highly probable" — a high threshold (Ind AS 105 defines highly probable as significantly more likely than probable; probable is more likely than not). It is deliberately asymmetric: the standard prefers under-recognition to reversal.
  • "Significant reversal" — assessed relative to cumulative revenue recognised for the contract (fixed plus variable), not just the variable part.
  • "Some or all" — the constraint is not all-or-nothing; the entity includes the portion that meets the threshold (a minimum amount that is highly probable).
  • Applies to the estimate produced by expected value or most likely amount.

Factors increasing the likelihood or magnitude of reversal (115.57)

  • (a) the amount is highly susceptible to factors outside the entity's influence — market volatility, judgement or actions of third parties (engineers, arbitrators, regulators), weather, high risk of obsolescence;
  • (b) the uncertainty is not expected to be resolved for a long period;
  • (c) the entity's experience (or other evidence) with similar contracts is limited, or has limited predictive value;
  • (d) the entity has a practice of offering a broad range of price concessions or changing payment terms in similar circumstances;
  • (e) the contract has a large number and broad range of possible consideration amounts.

Royalty exception (115.58, B63)

For sales-based or usage-based royalties promised in exchange for a licence of intellectual property, the constraint is replaced by a specific rule: revenue is recognised only when (or as) the later of (a) the subsequent sale or usage occurs, and (b) the PO to which the royalty is allocated has been satisfied (B63). Royalty estimates are not included in the transaction price up front.

Reassessment (115.59) and changes in transaction price (115.87–90)

At the end of each reporting period, update the estimated transaction price — including the constraint assessment — to represent faithfully the circumstances at that date. Changes are allocated to POs on the same basis as at inception (115.88); amounts allocated to satisfied POs are recognised as revenue (or reduction) in the period of change — a cumulative catch-up. Do not reallocate for changes in SSP after inception.

Applying the constraint: practical approach

  1. Estimate the variable amount (expected value or most likely).
  2. For each variable component, assess 115.57 factors and evidence.
  3. Determine the amount (possibly a floor) for which reversal is highly improbable — for a bonus, often nil until near-certain; for LDs (which reduce price), the constraint generally pushes towards recognising more of the expected LD because under-estimating a reduction would risk a later reversal of revenue.
  4. Test significance: compare possible reversal with cumulative revenue at resolution date.
  5. Document, and reassess each quarter.

Worked illustration: EPC bonus, PVC and claim

Continuing Rewa Buildtech (s04): fixed price ₹200 crore; expected LD ₹1.5 crore; bonus estimated at nil (most likely); PVC estimate ₹3.5 crore; plus a ₹12 crore claim for client-caused delays submitted to the Independent Engineer.

Component Estimate 115.57 factors Constrained amount
LD (1.5) expected Schedule data reliable; risk is that delay is longer Include (1.5); consider larger reduction if schedule risk is skewed
Bonus 0 Binary, depends on weather and client approvals 0
PVC 3.5 Index-driven (outside influence), but 40% of the period's index already published Include portion based on published indices, say 1.4
Claim 12.0 Judgement of third parties (Independent Engineer, arbitral tribunal), long resolution, limited precedent 0 until certified/awarded; perhaps a floor if the engineer certifies part

Constrained transaction price at 31 March 2026 = 200 − 1.5 + 0 + 1.4 + 0 = ₹199.9 crore. At 45% complete, cumulative revenue = ₹89.96 crore.

If on 30 June 2026 the Independent Engineer certifies ₹7 crore of the claim and the client accepts it, the transaction price rises by ₹7 crore; at 55% complete, the catch-up includes 55% × 7 = ₹3.85 crore attributable to past performance.

Worked illustration: significance test

A fund manager earns a performance fee of 20% of returns above a hurdle, crystallised at the end of a 3-year period. At the end of year 1, accrued fee ₹30 crore; management fees recognised to date ₹10 crore. Market volatility (115.57(a)) and long resolution period (115.57(b)) mean almost no portion is highly probable: a reversal of ₹30 crore against cumulative revenue of ₹40 crore is significant. Result: performance fee constrained to nil (or a small floor) until close to crystallisation — consistent with asset managers' practice.

and a check that the constrained amount never exceeds the estimate for upward variables, or is not less negative than the estimate for downward variables (LDs, rebates).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Downward variables (LDs, rebates, returns): the constraint can require a reduction larger than the expected value if the risk of under-estimating is significant.
  • Portfolio vs contract level: estimates may use portfolio data, but the constraint is assessed per contract; a portfolio of returns with stable history allows recognition at the expected return rate.
  • Unpriced change orders (s01) and claims: treat similarly; legal enforceability does not remove the constraint.
  • Escalation under government contracts routinely paid with delays: delay is a financing/collection issue, not necessarily a reason to constrain if entitlement is clear.
  • Year-end window dressing: releasing constraints at year-end without new evidence is an error risk; require evidence (certification, award, published index).
  • Interim reporting: reassessment at each quarter-end; SEBI quarterly results reflect the catch-ups.

Key takeaways

  • Include variable consideration only to the extent it is highly probable that no significant reversal of cumulative revenue will occur (115.56).
  • Five factors (115.57) — external influence, long resolution, limited experience, history of concessions, broad range — increase reversal risk.
  • The constraint can be partial; it often sets a floor.
  • Sales- and usage-based IP royalties follow B63 instead of the constraint (115.58).
  • Reassess every reporting date; changes are cumulative catch-ups allocated on the inception basis (115.59, 115.87–90).

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply the constraint [L2]

An IT services company has a 2-year ₹60 crore fixed-fee transformation contract with a gain-share fee of 25% of verified cost savings, capped at ₹15 crore, measured and paid at the end of year 2. The company has done three such contracts; savings outcomes ranged from 20% to 110% of target. Expected gain-share ₹9 crore. At the end of year 1 (cumulative fixed-fee revenue ₹28 crore), how much gain-share can be included in the transaction price? Justify using 115.57.

💡 Hint: Consider both the likelihood of reversal and its magnitude relative to cumulative revenue.

Q2. LDs and the constraint [L2]

An EPC contract (₹150 crore, 60% complete at 31 March 2026) has LDs of ₹1.5 crore per week capped at ₹15 crore. Management's best estimate is a 4-week delay (expected LD ₹6 crore) but the schedule shows a 30% chance of a 10-week delay. Discuss whether the constraint requires a transaction price below ₹144 crore and compute cumulative revenue under your conclusion.

Q3. Reassessment catch-up [L1]

A contract's constrained transaction price was ₹80 crore at 31 March 2025 when it was 40% complete (revenue ₹32 crore). At 31 March 2026 it is 75% complete and a ₹6 crore bonus becomes highly probable. Compute FY 2025-26 revenue and identify the portion attributable to the catch-up.

Q4. Constraint policy and controls [L3]

Draft a constraint policy for an infrastructure company with 40 live EPC contracts: the evidence required to include PVC, claims, bonuses and change orders; the approval matrix; quarterly reassessment controls; and the model outputs the audit committee should see. Explain how the policy feeds the 115.126AA reconciliation.
