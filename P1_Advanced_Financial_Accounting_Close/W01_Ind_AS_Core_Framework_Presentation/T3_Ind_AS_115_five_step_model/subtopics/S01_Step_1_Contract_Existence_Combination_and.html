Subtopic note

Step 1: Contract Existence, Combination and Modifications

Scope: Step 1: contract existence criteria (Ind AS 115.9), combination of contracts, contract modifications (115.18–21)

Why it matters

Revenue cannot be recognised until there is a contract within the meaning of Ind AS 115 — and the "contract" for accounting purposes may be narrower, broader or different from the legal document. Indian practice throws up the hard cases constantly: work started on a letter of intent before the purchase order, government EPC contracts with unsigned amendments, multiple POs from the same customer negotiated together, and change orders priced months after the work is done. Step 1 decisions determine whether revenue is recognised at all, whether two contracts are accounted for as one (changing PO identification and allocation), and whether a scope change is a new contract, a prospective adjustment or a cumulative catch-up — each of which moves profit between periods.

Core notes

Contract existence criteria (Ind AS 115.9)

An entity accounts for a contract with a customer within Ind AS 115 only when all of the following are met:

  • (a) the parties have approved the contract (in writing, orally or in accordance with other customary business practices) and are committed to perform their respective obligations;
  • (b) the entity can identify each party's rights regarding the goods or services to be transferred;
  • (c) the entity can identify the payment terms;
  • (d) the contract has commercial substance (the risk, timing or amount of future cash flows is expected to change as a result of the contract); and
  • (e) it is probable that the entity will collect the consideration to which it will be entitled in exchange for the goods or services that will be transferred — considering only the customer's ability and intention to pay when due. The amount may be less than the stated price if the entity may offer a price concession (variable consideration, Step 3).

Supporting rules:

  • Enforceability (115.10): a contract is an agreement that creates enforceable rights and obligations; enforceability is a matter of law. Customary practice and oral agreements can create contracts.
  • Contract term (115.11): the contract's duration is the period in which parties have present enforceable rights and obligations — a monthly-cancellable subscription with no penalty is a month-to-month contract.
  • Wholly unperformed contracts (115.12): no contract exists if each party has the unilateral enforceable right to terminate a wholly unperformed contract without compensating the other party.
  • Reassessment (115.13): if the criteria are met at inception, reassess only if there is an indication of a significant change in facts and circumstances (e.g., significant deterioration in the customer's ability to pay).
  • Criteria not met (115.14–16): continue to assess; consideration received is recognised as revenue only when (a) the entity has no remaining obligations and all, or substantially all, consideration has been received and is non-refundable, or (b) the contract has been terminated and consideration received is non-refundable. Otherwise consideration received is a liability (US GAAP ASC 606 adds a third event — control transferred, transfers stopped, no further obligation — which IFRS 15 and Ind AS 115 do not contain).

Collectability judgement: assess the customer's credit risk — not the risk that the entity will grant a price concession. A sale to a state discom with chronic payment delays is not automatically fails-(e); the entity may expect to collect over time (consider SFC) or may have a practice of accepting lower amounts (variable consideration). A sale to a customer the entity knows cannot pay, made to meet targets, fails (e).

Combination of contracts (Ind AS 115.17)

Two or more contracts entered into at or near the same time with the same customer (or related parties of the customer) are combined and accounted for as a single contract if one or more of these criteria are met:

  • (a) the contracts are negotiated as a package with a single commercial objective;
  • (b) the amount of consideration to be paid in one contract depends on the price or performance of the other contract; or
  • (c) the goods or services promised in the contracts (or some goods or services in each) are a single performance obligation.

Indian examples: separate "supply" and "erection" contracts for a substation, signed the same week with the same state utility, where the supply contract is priced below cost and the erection contract above (criterion (a) and (b)); a software licence PO and a customisation PO where the licence is useless without the customisation (criterion (c)). Splitting contracts for tax or stamp-duty reasons (e.g., to separate goods from works-contract services under GST) does not change the accounting if the criteria are met.

Contract modifications (Ind AS 115.18–21)

A contract modification is a change in the scope or price (or both) approved by the parties (115.18) — variation orders, change orders, amendments. Approval can be written, oral or implied by customary practice. If the parties have approved a scope change but not yet the price, estimate the change in price as variable consideration (115.19), subject to the constraint. Claims and disputed change orders require judgement on enforceability.

Separate contract (115.20) — a modification is accounted for as a separate contract if both:

  • (a) the scope increases because of the addition of promised goods or services that are distinct; and
  • (b) the price increases by an amount that reflects the entity's stand-alone selling prices of the additional goods or services (with appropriate adjustments for particular circumstances, e.g., a discount reflecting saved selling costs).

Otherwise (115.21), account for the modification by one of:

  • (a) Prospective — termination of the old contract and creation of a new one: if the remaining goods or services are distinct from those transferred on or before the modification. The consideration allocated to the remaining POs = (i) consideration promised by the customer (including amounts already received) not yet recognised as revenue, plus (ii) consideration promised as part of the modification.
  • (b) Cumulative catch-up: if the remaining goods or services are not distinct and form part of a single PO partially satisfied at the date of modification. Update the transaction price and the measure of progress, and recognise the effect as an adjustment to revenue (increase or reduction) at the date of modification.
  • (c) Combination of (a) and (b) where the remaining goods are a mix.

Worked illustration: three modification outcomes

Annapurna Facility Services Ltd signs a 24-month contract for ₹4.8 crore (₹20 lakh per month, SSP) to provide housekeeping to a hospital. After 12 months (₹2.4 crore recognised):

  1. Add a second building for 12 months at ₹18 lakh per month (the current SSP for the additional building, reflecting lower mobilisation): distinct + priced at SSP → separate contract. Original contract continues at ₹20 lakh per month.
  2. Customer negotiates a price reduction to ₹16 lakh per month for the remaining 12 months (no scope change): remaining monthly services are distinct (a series) → prospective. Remaining consideration = ₹2.4 crore unrecognised − ₹0.48 crore reduction = ₹1.92 crore, recognised at ₹16 lakh per month.
  3. EPC example — a ₹50 crore plant contract, 50% complete on cost-to-cost (costs ₹20 crore of ₹40 crore estimate, revenue recognised ₹25 crore); the customer approves a design change adding ₹5 crore to the price and ₹6 crore to the estimated cost: single PO, not distinct → cumulative catch-up. New progress = 20 ÷ 46 = 43.48%; cumulative revenue = 43.48% × 55 = ₹23.91 crore; catch-up adjustment = 23.91 − 25.00 = ₹1.09 crore reduction of revenue at the modification date, even though the price went up — because progress fell as the cost base grew.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Work started before signing: if the criteria (approval, enforceable rights) are met by customary practice (an LOI with enforceable terms), the contract exists from that date; costs incurred earlier may be fulfilment costs (115.95) and revenue for pre-contract performance is recognised at contract inception (cumulative catch-up).
  • Unpriced change orders: estimate price under 115.19; if enforceability is doubtful (customer disputes), treat as a claim and apply the constraint strictly.
  • Framework/master agreements with call-off orders: often each PO is the contract; the master agreement may create material rights (volume discounts) — see s03.
  • Termination for convenience clauses without penalty shorten the contract term; renewals are new contracts.
  • Collectability failing after inception (customer in CIRP): reassess under 115.13; stop recognising revenue for future performance; existing receivables go to ECL (Ind AS 109), not revenue reversal.
  • Related-party combination: contracts with a customer's group entities negotiated together are combined if 115.17 criteria are met.

Key takeaways

  • All five 115.9 criteria must be met; collectability looks only at the customer's ability and intention to pay.
  • If criteria are not met, cash received is a liability until 115.15 conditions are satisfied.
  • Combine contracts negotiated as a package, interdependent in price, or forming a single PO (115.17).
  • Modifications: distinct + SSP = separate contract; distinct remaining = prospective; not distinct = cumulative catch-up (115.20–21).
  • Unpriced but approved scope changes are estimated as variable consideration, subject to the constraint.

Practice Questions

Q1. Does a contract exist? [L1]

A company starts manufacturing customised transformers on 10 March 2026 on the basis of an email from a state utility's chief engineer stating that "the PO will follow after board approval". The utility's board approves the PO on 15 April 2026. Costs incurred to 31 March 2026: ₹2.1 crore. Evaluate each 115.9 criterion at 31 March 2026 and state the accounting for the costs.

Q2. Combination test [L2]

On 2 June 2025 a telecom tower company signs (a) a ₹30 crore contract to construct 100 towers for a telco and (b) on 9 June 2025, a 10-year ₹180 crore O&M contract for the same towers with the telco's subsidiary. The tower price is 15% below the company's standard rate; the O&M rate is 20% above market. Should the contracts be combined? What are the consequences for allocation?

💡 Hint: Test each 115.17 criterion separately and consider who the customer is.

Q3. Modification accounting [L2]

A 36-month AMC for ₹72 lakh (₹2 lakh per month) is modified after 20 months: the customer adds 50% more equipment for the remaining 16 months for an extra ₹12 lakh in total; the SSP of the additional coverage is ₹1 lakh per month. Determine whether this is a separate contract, a prospective modification or a cumulative catch-up, and compute monthly revenue for months 21–36.

Q4. EPC change order with dispute [L3]

An EPC contractor has a ₹300 crore contract, 60% complete at 31 March 2026 on cost-to-cost (₹150 crore of ₹250 crore). The client has approved a scope change adding ₹20 crore of cost but is disputing the price; the contractor claims ₹30 crore, the engineer's interim assessment is ₹22 crore, and legal advice says the claim is enforceable but the amount is uncertain. Prepare a technical memo on modification accounting, estimation of the price change and the constraint, and compute revenue at 31 March 2026 under the amount you conclude is appropriate.
