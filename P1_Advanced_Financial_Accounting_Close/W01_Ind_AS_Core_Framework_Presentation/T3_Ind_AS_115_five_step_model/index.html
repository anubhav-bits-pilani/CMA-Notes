Roadmap topic: Ind AS 115 Five-Step Model: Identifying distinct performance obligations, variable consideration constraints, and transaction price allocation

Overview

Ind AS 115 Revenue from Contracts with Customers, effective in India from 1 April 2018, replaced Ind AS 18 and Ind AS 11 with one control-based model built on five steps: (1) identify the contract; (2) identify the performance obligations; (3) determine the transaction price; (4) allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations; and (5) recognise revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied. This topic covers Steps 1–4 in depth — the steps that decide how much revenue each promise carries. Topic 4 covers Step 5 (timing, progress measurement and contract balances).

Revenue is the largest number in most income statements, the starting point of every forecast and valuation, and the area with the highest fraud and error risk in Indian audits (SA 240 presumes fraud risk in revenue recognition). The five-step model is therefore central to every target role: technical accountants write PO and variable consideration memos; reporting analysts produce disaggregation, contract balance and the India-specific 126AA reconciliation; FP&A teams forecast revenue from contract terms (bookings, backlog, rebates, LDs) and must understand why revenue diverges from billing; modellers build monthly waterfalls that apply SSP allocation and constraint toggles. Indian business models — EPC with LDs and price variation, FMCG trade schemes, SaaS bundles from Indian IT product companies, real estate advances — make each step a live judgement.

The learning sequence follows the model. Step 1 (s01): when a contract exists, when contracts are combined and how modifications are handled (c05). Step 2 (s02, s03): distinct POs — the two-part test, series, and common bundles — plus warranties and customer options as hidden POs. Step 3 (s04, s05, s06): variable consideration and its estimation (c02), the constraint, then financing components (c03), consideration payable to customers and non-cash consideration. Step 4 (s07): relative SSP allocation (c01), SSP estimation methods including the residual approach (c06), and allocation of discounts and variable consideration. Disclosure (s08): the 126AA reconciliation. Contract costs (c04) sit alongside because commissions are incurred at Step 1 and amortised in line with Step 5.

Related concepts to study

Convergence with IFRS 15 and ASC 606

IFRS 15/ASC 606 converged; US GAAP differences: collectability "probable" threshold, shipping/handling policy election, licence renewal timing

Ind AS 115 is substantially converged with IFRS 15, which was issued jointly with the FASB's ASC 606, so most interpretive literature (Big Four guides, TRG discussions) applies in India. Differences matter when benchmarking against US peers or preparing US GAAP packs. Collectability: both require collection to be "probable", but "probable" means "likely to occur" under US GAAP (a higher threshold) versus "more likely than not" under IFRS/Ind AS. Shipping and handling: ASC 606 permits an election to treat shipping after control transfers as a fulfilment cost rather than a separate PO; Ind AS 115 does not. Licence renewals: under ASC 606, revenue for a renewal of a licence of IP is not recognised before the renewal period begins; IFRS 15 has no explicit rule, so revenue may be recognised when the renewal is agreed if the licence is already in the customer's possession. Other differences include the ASC 606 immaterial promises exemption and the sales-tax presentation election.

ICDS IV, ICDS III and deferred tax

ICDS IV (revenue) and ICDS III (construction contracts) tax timing differences → Week 3 (w3_t3) deferred tax

For tax computation, revenue follows the Income Computation and Disclosure Standards notified under Sec 145(2). ICDS IV recognises revenue from sale of goods on transfer of significant risks and rewards with reasonable certainty of collection, and service revenue generally by percentage of completion (with straight-line for an indeterminate number of acts over a period). ICDS III applies percentage of completion to construction contracts, treats retention money as part of contract revenue, and restricts early-stage recognition (outcome not reliably estimable up to 25% completion — revenue recognised to the extent of costs). Sec 43CB requires POC for construction and service contracts (except service contracts under 90 days). ICDS does not generally permit expected-loss provisions, and Sec 145B taxes certain escalation and interest claims on receipt or reasonable certainty. These differences from Ind AS 115 (constraint, SFC, PO allocation, onerous provisions) create temporary differences handled in Week 3.

Warranty provisions and ECL on contract assets

Ind AS 37 assurance warranties → Week 2 (w2_t4); Ind AS 109 ECL on contract assets → Week 3 (w3_t2)

Assurance-type warranties identified in s03 are measured under Ind AS 37 — a best estimate of expected repair and replacement costs, often computed as sales in warranty × claim rate × cost per claim, discounted where material — which Week 2 (w2_t4) covers with onerous contracts and restructuring provisions. Service-type warranties stay in Ind AS 115 as contract liabilities. On the asset side, contract assets and trade receivables arising from Ind AS 115 are within the Ind AS 109 impairment model; the simplified approach (lifetime ECL) is required for trade receivables and contract assets without a significant financing component and available as a policy choice otherwise (Ind AS 109.5.5.15). Week 3 (w3_t2) builds provision matrices that must include unbilled revenue and retention balances.

Excel: SSP allocation and constraint toggles

Excel: SSP allocation table using SUMPRODUCT, scenario toggles for variable consideration constraint

Build Step 4 as a table: one row per PO with columns for SSP evidence, SSP method, SSP amount, distinct flag, specific-discount flag, allocation percentage and allocated amount. SUMPRODUCT handles conditional denominators, e.g. =TP*SSP_i/SUMPRODUCT(SSP_Range,--(Distinct_Flag="Yes")), so switching a PO from distinct to combined re-runs allocation without rewriting formulas. Variable consideration sits in a separate block with columns for estimate method (data-validated list: Expected value / Most likely), estimated amount, constrained amount and a global toggle VC_Constraint_On. The transaction price feeding the waterfall is =Fixed_Price+IF(VC_Constraint_On="Yes",SUM(Constrained),SUM(Estimated)). Add checks: allocations sum to TP; allocation percentages sum to 100%; constrained upside never exceeds estimate.

Step 5 and contract balances

→ w1_t4: Step 5 timing and contract balances

Allocation tells you how much revenue each PO carries; Topic 4 (w1_t4) tells you when. Step 5 tests whether each PO is satisfied over time (115.35: simultaneous receipt and consumption, customer-controlled asset, or no alternative use plus enforceable right to payment) or at a point in time (115.38 control indicators), then measures progress using output or input methods. The allocated amount × progress gives cumulative revenue; comparing it with billing produces contract assets and contract liabilities, presented net per contract (115.105–107). Changes in the transaction price determined in this topic (constraint releases, modifications) flow through Step 5 as cumulative catch-ups.

Common pitfalls

Implementation treated as distinct despite significant customisation

Treating implementation as distinct when it significantly customises or integrates the software

The mistake: splitting implementation from a SaaS or licence and recognising it as hours are worked, even though the implementation builds custom functionality or integrations without which the platform does not deliver the contracted output. Why it happens: it accelerates revenue; implementation is often invoiced separately; "capable of being distinct" is confused with "distinct". Correct treatment: apply both limbs of 115.27; under 115.29(b)–(c), significant customisation or integration makes the promises a combined PO, recognised from when the customer obtains the benefit (often go-live) over the service period. Detection: review statements of work for custom code; check whether third parties perform such implementations; compare go-live dates with revenue patterns; check whether implementation fees are refundable if the SaaS fails.

Variable amounts included without applying the constraint

Including LD-exposed or bonus amounts without applying the constraint

The mistake: recognising revenue on the full contract price despite expected LDs, or including early-completion bonuses and claims at expected value. Why it happens: contract value equals order book; LDs are seen as "penalties" for other expenses; project teams are optimistic. Correct treatment: estimate LDs and bonuses (s04), then include only amounts for which a significant reversal is highly improbable (115.56–57); LDs reduce the transaction price; reassess every reporting date. Detection: compare schedule delay reports with LD estimates; list all bonuses and claims included in TP and the evidence for each; analyse the 126AA reconciliation for LD and claims lines; look for large catch-ups when claims are rejected.

Discount allocated to a single PO without meeting 115.82

Allocating a discount to one PO without meeting 115.82 criteria

The mistake: assigning the whole bundle discount to the later-delivered PO (e.g., the subscription or AMC) so that early-delivered hardware or implementation keeps full price. Why it happens: sales teams describe the discount as "on the service"; it front-loads revenue. Correct treatment: allocate discounts proportionately (115.81) unless all three 115.82 criteria are met — regular stand-alone sales of each item, regular discounted sales of a sub-bundle, and substantially the same discount providing observable evidence. Detection: recompute allocation on relative SSP; request evidence of regular bundle sales; check that allocation percentages match SSP ratios.

List prices used as SSP without evidence

Using contractual list prices as SSP without observable evidence

The mistake: using the price list or the price stated in the contract as SSP for each item, even though items are always discounted or never sold separately. Why it happens: it is easy and documented; 115.77 is misread. Correct treatment: SSP is the price at which the entity would sell the item separately; observable stand-alone sales are the best evidence; otherwise estimate using adjusted market assessment or expected cost plus margin, maximising observable inputs (115.78–79). A contractually stated price is not presumed to be SSP. Detection: compare list prices with actual stand-alone transaction data (ERP sales history by SKU and customer class); test whether SSP analyses exist and are refreshed; check the range of stand-alone prices.

Practice Questions

Q1. SaaS bundle end-to-end [L3]

Lakshya Cloud Ltd signs on 1 July 2025 a ₹2.40 crore contract with a retailer: 3-year SaaS subscription from go-live (expected 1 November 2025), implementation (2,300 hours; configuration and data migration using standard tools; partners can perform it), training (sold separately at ₹5 lakh), and an option to renew for years 4–5 at ₹45 lakh per year (standard annual price for similar customers ₹60 lakh; new customers receive at most 5% discount; expected renewal probability 70%). Subscription SSP ₹60 lakh per year; implementation expected cost ₹2,600 per hour with 30% margin; payment ₹60 lakh at signing, ₹30 lakh at go-live and ₹50 lakh on 1 November in each of 2025, 2026, 2027. Sales commission 5% (no renewal commission).

Required: (a) identify the POs, including any material right; (b) assess the SFC; (c) allocate the transaction price; (d) compute FY 2025-26 revenue by PO (implementation hours: Jul 500, Aug 700, Sep 700, Oct 400) and the contract cost asset amortisation; (e) state how the answers change if implementation is not distinct.

💡 Hint: Estimate the option's SSP with B42 before running the relative SSP allocation.

Q2. EPC transaction price and constraint [L3]

Prabal Infra Ltd signs a ₹360 crore metro viaduct contract (30 months) with: LD of 0.5% per week of delay capped at 10%; early completion bonus ₹9 crore if completed 8 weeks early; steel PVC estimated at ₹14 crore (index volatile); a ₹4 crore upfront signing payment made by Prabal to the client's nominated consultant for design review services that the client would otherwise procure (fair value ₹1.5 crore); and a mobilisation advance of 10% recovered over 24 months, interest-free. Schedule probabilities at 31 March 2026: on time 45%; 4 weeks late 35%; 8 weeks late 15%; 8 weeks early 5%. Progress at 31 March 2026: 35%.

Required: (a) estimate each variable element and choose methods; (b) apply the constraint; (c) treat the consultant payment and mobilisation advance; (d) compute constrained transaction price and cumulative revenue at 31 March 2026; (e) prepare the 126AA reconciliation line items for FY 2025-26.

Q3. Combination, modification and allocation [L2]

On 10 April 2025 Udyog Automation Ltd signs (a) a ₹12 crore contract to supply 4 robotic welding cells (SSP ₹3.2 crore each; installation by Udyog not complex) and (b) on 18 April 2025 a ₹1.6 crore 2-year AMC (SSP ₹1 crore per year) with the same customer, negotiated together. Two cells are delivered by 30 September 2025. On 1 October 2025 the customer adds 2 more cells at ₹2.6 crore each (SSP ₹3.2 crore).

Required: (a) decide whether the contracts are combined; (b) allocate the original transaction price; (c) account for the modification; (d) compute revenue for FY 2025-26 if the remaining four cells are delivered by 31 March 2026 and the AMC starts on 1 October 2025.

Q4. Warranty, loyalty and returns at a consumer durables company [L2]

Sushant Appliances Ltd sells ₹600 crore of products in FY 2025-26 with: a 1-year standard warranty (claim cost 1.5% of sales); extended 3-year warranties sold separately for ₹18 crore (SSP equals price); loyalty points worth ₹9 crore at face value with 60% expected redemption (₹2.5 crore redeemed in the year); and a 30-day return right with expected returns of 2% of sales (cost of goods 70% of sales). Prepare the journal entries and the extract of revenue, contract liabilities, refund liabilities, provisions and the return asset at 31 March 2026, and the 126AA reconciliation.
