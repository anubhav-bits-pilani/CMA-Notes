Key concept

Expected Value vs Most Likely Amount

Concept: Expected value = Σ (probability × outcome) for large portfolios; most likely amount for binary outcomes (bonus/LD triggers)

Statement

Ind AS 115.53 requires variable consideration to be estimated using whichever of two methods better predicts the amount of consideration to which the entity will be entitled:

\[ \text{Expected value} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} p_k \times x_k \]

where \(x_k\) is the \(k\)-th possible consideration amount, \(p_k\) its probability, and \(\sum p_k = 1\);

\[ \text{Most likely amount} = x_{k^*}, \quad k^* = \arg\max_k \, p_k \]

i.e., the single amount with the highest probability.

Explanation

Expected value suits situations with a large number of contracts with similar characteristics or many possible outcomes: retail returns, distributor rebate pools, SLA credits across many service lines, multi-tier LDs. Across a portfolio the probability-weighted amount predicts total consideration well, even though no single contract may end up at exactly that amount.

Most likely amount suits binary or few-outcome situations: a ₹5 crore bonus either earned or not; an LD that triggers fully if a single milestone date is missed; a success fee on a single deal. For a binary outcome, the expected value (e.g., 60% × ₹5 crore = ₹3 crore) is an amount that can never actually occur and would predict poorly.

Rules:

  • The method is chosen per uncertainty and applied consistently throughout the contract (115.54).
  • Different methods can apply to different variable elements of the same contract.
  • Use all reasonably available information (historical, current, forecast) — the same information used in bidding and management forecasts.
  • The estimate then passes through the constraint (115.56). For upside items (bonuses), the constraint often reduces the amount; for downside items (LDs, rebates), it may push towards a larger deduction.

Illustration

Contract A — portfolio rebate. A tyre company sells to 300 dealers with a retrospective rebate of 0%, 2% or 4% depending on annual volume. Historical distribution: 25% earn 0%, 45% earn 2%, 30% earn 4%.

\[ E[\text{rebate rate}] = 0.25 \times 0 + 0.45 \times 2\% + 0.30 \times 4\% = 2.1\% \]

Apply 2.1% to sales to each dealer unless dealer-specific evidence predicts better.

Contract B — binary bonus. An EPC contractor can earn a ₹4 crore bonus for completion before 30 September 2026; probability 65%.

  • Most likely amount = ₹4 crore (65% > 35%).
  • Expected value would give ₹2.6 crore — not a possible outcome.
  • Constraint: is it highly probable that including ₹4 crore will not lead to a significant reversal? At 65% likelihood, no — constrained to nil until the probability is much higher.

Contract C — multi-outcome LD. LD ₹1 crore per week, cap ₹6 crore; probabilities: 0 weeks 40%, 2 weeks 30%, 4 weeks 20%, 6+ weeks 10%.

\[ E[\text{LD}] = 0.4 \times 0 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 4 + 0.1 \times 6 = 2.0 \text{ crore} \]

Most likely amount would be nil (40%) — clearly a poorer predictor here.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute both and choose [L1]

A software company's contract includes a ₹50 lakh success fee payable if the customer's go-live occurs by 31 December 2026 (probability 55%), and SLA credits with outcomes ₹0 (50%), ₹10 lakh (30%), ₹20 lakh (15%), ₹40 lakh (5%). Compute the expected value and most likely amount for each, choose the better method for each, and state the estimate before the constraint.

Q2. Consistency [L2]

A contractor used the most likely amount (nil) for a two-outcome LD at inception. Midway through the contract, the schedule shows five possible delay outcomes. Can the contractor switch to expected value? Discuss with reference to 115.53–54 and how the estimate should be updated.

💡 Hint: Distinguish a change in method from a change in the facts the method is applied to.

Q3. Interview: why not always expected value? [L1]

Explain to an interviewer, with a numerical example, why expected value can be a poor predictor for a single binary bonus but a good predictor for a portfolio of returns.
