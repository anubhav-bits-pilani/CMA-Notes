Raw Material Write-Downs, Replacement Cost and NRV Reversals

Scope: Raw material write-down rule, replacement cost proxy, reversal when NRV recovers

Why it matters

Commodity-intensive businesses — steel, aluminium, edible oils, textiles, polymers, chemicals — see raw-material prices swing 20–40% within a year. A falling input price tempts a write-down of raw material stock, yet Ind AS 2.32 often forbids it. When prices recover, Ind AS 2.33 requires reversal of earlier write-downs, which US GAAP prohibits. Both rules routinely appear in audit adjustments, quarterly result commentary ("inventory losses/gains") and CMA/CA exams. FP&A teams modelling margins in commodity cycles need the same logic to forecast write-downs and reversals.

Core notes

The raw material rule (Ind AS 2.32)

Materials and other supplies held for use in the production of inventories are not written down below cost if the finished products in which they will be incorporated are expected to be sold at or above cost. The test is at the finished-goods level: the raw material's own market price is irrelevant if the end product remains profitable.

However, when a decline in the price of materials indicates that the cost of the finished products exceeds NRV, the materials are written down to NRV. In such circumstances, the replacement cost of the materials may be the best available measure of their NRV.

Decision sequence:

  1. Identify the finished products in which the material will be used (bill of materials).
  2. Estimate the cost of those finished products (using the actual cost of material held, plus conversion costs to complete).
  3. Estimate the finished products' NRV (selling price − costs to complete − selling costs).
  4. If FG NRV ≥ FG cost → no write-down of raw material, regardless of its market price.
  5. If FG NRV < FG cost → write down raw material (and WIP/FG). Replacement cost of the material may be used as its NRV; alternatively, allocate the FG shortfall back to the raw material.

Why replacement cost is a proxy

Raw materials are not sold in the ordinary course; their "selling price" is not the relevant measure. When finished goods are loss-making because input prices have fallen (and selling prices have followed), the current replacement cost tells you what the input is now worth to the business — the entity could buy the same material at that price. Replacement cost is "may be the best available measure", not mandatory; where the loss is driven by other factors (finished-goods demand collapse), allocating the FG shortfall is more faithful.

Illustration: two scenarios (₹ per tonne of finished product)

A wire-rod maker holds 1,000 tonnes of billets (cost ₹48,000 per tonne). Conversion cost ₹6,000 per tonne; selling costs ₹1,000 per tonne. Billet replacement cost at year-end: ₹42,000.

Scenario A Scenario B
FG selling price ₹58,000 ₹51,000
FG NRV (price − selling cost) ₹57,000 ₹50,000
FG cost (48,000 + 6,000) ₹54,000 ₹54,000
FG profitable? Yes No (shortfall ₹4,000)
Billet write-down None (2.32) Write down to NRV
Billet NRV — replacement cost method n/a ₹42,000 → write-down ₹6,000/t = ₹60 lakh
Billet NRV — FG shortfall allocation n/a 48,000 − 4,000 = ₹44,000 → write-down ₹4,000/t = ₹40 lakh

In Scenario B, the entity chooses the approach that best reflects NRV and applies it consistently. The FG-shortfall method (NRV of FG − costs to complete = \(50,000 - 6,000 = 44,000\)) ties directly to the definition of NRV including costs of completion; replacement cost is acceptable when it is the best available evidence. Document the choice.

Reversal when NRV recovers (Ind AS 2.33)

A new assessment of NRV is made in each subsequent period. When the circumstances that previously caused inventories to be written down below cost no longer exist, or there is clear evidence of an increase in NRV because of changed economic circumstances, the write-down is reversed. The reversal is capped so that the new carrying amount is the lower of cost and the revised NRV — i.e., the reversal is limited to the amount of the original write-down (see c02).

The reversal is recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period in which the reversal occurs (2.34) — not as other income. Disclose the amount of any reversal and the circumstances (2.36(f)–(g)).

Example: a textile company wrote down cotton yarn (cost ₹220/kg) to ₹190/kg at 31 March 2024 on 2,00,000 kg (write-down ₹60 lakh). At 31 March 2025, 80,000 kg of that stock remains (the rest was sold), and NRV has recovered to ₹235/kg. Reversal on remaining stock: carrying ₹190 → lower of cost ₹220 and NRV ₹235 = ₹220; reversal = \(80,000 \times 30 = ₹24\) lakh. NRV above cost (₹15/kg) is not recognised.

Reversals apply only to items still on hand that were written down; written-down items sold during the year have already passed the lower carrying amount into COGS.

Practical tracking

ERP systems often carry a single "provision for inventory obsolescence" ledger; to apply 2.33 correctly, the provision must be tracked by item or group so the reversal can be identified and capped. In Excel, maintain columns: item, quantity, cost per unit, NRV per unit (current), original write-down per unit, carrying per unit = MIN(Cost, NRV), and movement = prior carrying − current carrying, split into write-down (new) and reversal (capped).

=MIN(Cost, NRV_current)
=MAX(0, Carrying_current - Carrying_prior)

Tax and deferred tax interaction

Income-tax computation follows ICDS II (lower of cost and NRV), so NRV write-downs are generally allowable if computed item by item; general "provision for obsolescence" based on matrices may be disallowed as unascertained. Differences give rise to deferred tax assets (Week 3).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Material used in several products — some profitable, some not: allocate the material by expected usage; write down only the portion destined for loss-making products if usage is identifiable.
  • Materials held for resale or for sale as scrap are not covered by 2.32 — test at their own selling price.
  • Firm FG sales contracts fix FG NRV for contracted quantities, which can protect raw materials earmarked for them.
  • Hedged commodity purchases: fair value hedge adjustments under Ind AS 109 adjust inventory cost; NRV is then compared with the adjusted cost.
  • Interim periods: write-downs and reversals in interim reports follow the same rules (Ind AS 34 Appendix B guidance on inventories), so a Q2 write-down may reverse in Q4.
  • Reversal vs new purchases: new purchases at lower prices do not "reverse" anything; the cap applies per written-down item.

Key takeaways

  • Do not write down raw materials if finished goods will sell at or above cost (2.32).
  • When FG cost exceeds FG NRV, write down materials; replacement cost may be the best evidence of their NRV.
  • The FG-shortfall approach ties directly to NRV including costs to complete; choose and apply consistently.
  • Reverse write-downs when NRV recovers, capped at the original write-down — never above cost (2.33).
  • Reversals reduce inventory expense, not other income, and must be disclosed with circumstances.
  • Track provisions item-wise so reversals can be computed and capped.

Practice Questions

Q1. Raw material test for an edible oil refiner [L2]

A refiner holds 2,000 tonnes of crude palm oil at ₹92,000/t. Refining cost ₹6,000/t; yield 95%; refined oil price ₹1,08,000/t; selling cost ₹2,000/t of refined oil. Crude replacement cost at year-end ₹85,000/t. Determine whether a write-down is needed. Re-perform if the refined oil price falls to ₹1,00,000/t.

💡 Hint: Convert everything to a per-tonne-of-crude basis using the yield before comparing FG cost with FG NRV.

Q2. Reversal cap [L1]

At 31 March 2024, 10,000 units of a component (cost ₹500) were written down to ₹380. At 31 March 2025, 6,000 units remain and NRV is ₹540. Compute the reversal and state its P&L presentation and disclosure.

Q3. Mixed-use material [L3]

A polymer compound (cost ₹2 crore) is used 60% in automotive parts (FG margin 18%) and 40% in consumer goods (FG cost ₹1.25 crore including the compound; FG NRV ₹1.05 crore). Compound replacement cost is 15% below cost. Determine the write-down, justify the allocation basis, and explain how the conclusion changes if usage cannot be reliably allocated.

Q4. Commodity-cycle forecast [L3]

You are building a quarterly margin forecast for a copper-wire maker carrying 45 days of copper inventory. Copper prices fall 12% in Q1 and recover 15% in Q3; wire prices follow copper with a one-month lag. Describe how you would model NRV write-downs and reversals in Q1–Q4 consistent with Ind AS 2.32–2.34, including the data structure and the checks needed.
