Key concept

Antidilution and the Continuing-Operations Control Number

Concept: Potential shares are antidilutive if they increase EPS from continuing operations (control number, Ind AS 33.41–42)

Statement

Potential ordinary shares are treated as dilutive only when their conversion to ordinary shares would decrease earnings per share or increase loss per share from continuing operations (Ind AS 33.41). An entity uses profit or loss from continuing operations attributable to the parent as the control number to establish whether potential ordinary shares are dilutive or antidilutive (33.42). Antidilutive potential shares are ignored in computing diluted EPS (33.43).

\[ \text{Class } k \text{ is dilutive iff } \frac{E_c + \sum_{j \le k} \Delta E_j}{\text{WANS} + \sum_{j \le k} \Delta N_j} < \frac{E_c + \sum_{j < k} \Delta E_j}{\text{WANS} + \sum_{j < k} \Delta N_j} \]

where \(E_c\) is profit from continuing operations attributable to ordinary equity holders of the parent, and classes are taken in the 33.44 ranking order.

Explanation

The control number anchors dilution testing to continuing operations because these represent the sustainable earnings base. The same set of dilutive potential shares is then used for diluted EPS on total profit and on discontinued operations, even if the result there is antidilutive (33.42 and its illustrative examples). This means:

  • If continuing operations make a loss, every potential share class increases the denominator and therefore reduces the loss per share — all are antidilutive. Diluted loss per share = basic loss per share for continuing operations, and the same denominators are used for total and discontinued EPS, even if total profit is positive because of a discontinued gain.
  • If continuing operations are profitable but a discontinued operation makes a large loss, potential shares selected on the continuing number may increase total loss per share or reduce total EPS — that is still the required presentation.

Why it matters in practice: many new-age Indian listed companies report losses; their diluted EPS equals basic despite large ESOP pools. Analysts must not compute their own "diluted" loss per share with more shares. The excluded instruments must be disclosed (33.70(c)).

Common errors: testing dilution against total profit; testing each class in isolation instead of sequentially; showing diluted EPS higher than basic.

Illustration

Case A — continuing profit, discontinued loss. Continuing profit ₹80 crore; discontinued loss ₹50 crore; total ₹30 crore. WANS 20 crore. Convertible bonds: post-tax interest ₹6 crore, 5 crore shares.

Basic Test on continuing Result
Continuing 80 ÷ 20 = 4.00 86 ÷ 25 = 3.44 Dilutive (3.44 < 4.00)
Discontinued (50) ÷ 20 = (2.50) (50) ÷ 25 = (2.00) Use 25 crore shares
Total 30 ÷ 20 = 1.50 36 ÷ 25 = 1.44 Use the same set

Case B — continuing loss. Continuing loss ₹(40) crore; discontinued gain ₹70 crore; total ₹30 crore; same bonds. Adding bonds: \((-40 + 6) \div 25 = (1.36)\) vs basic \((-40) \div 20 = (2.00)\) — loss per share decreases → antidilutive. Diluted = basic for all three lines: continuing (2.00), discontinued 3.50, total 1.50 — even though, measured on total profit alone, the bonds would have been dilutive (36 ÷ 25 = 1.44 < 1.50).

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply the control number [L1]

Continuing profit ₹24 crore; discontinued loss ₹30 crore; WANS 12 crore. Options give 1 crore incremental shares. Present basic and diluted EPS for continuing, discontinued and total operations.

Q2. Loss-making start-up with ESOPs and CCPS [L2]

A recently listed company reports a continuing loss of ₹180 crore, no discontinued operations, WANS 60 crore, ESOPs giving 4 crore incremental shares, and CCPS (mandatorily convertible) of 6 crore shares issued on 1 January 2025. Compute basic and diluted loss per share and list the disclosures for excluded instruments.

💡 Hint: Consider whether the CCPS belong in basic EPS before thinking about dilution.

Q3. Interview — why not total profit? [L2]

Explain why Ind AS 33 uses continuing operations as the control number rather than total profit, using a numerical example where the two approaches select different dilutive sets.
