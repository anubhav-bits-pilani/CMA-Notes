Roadmap topic: Cost Structure Forecasting: Separating Variable COGS (% of revenue / unit cost) from Semi-Variable and Fixed SG&A (headcount × salary + inflation)

Overview

If revenue tells you how big the business will be, cost structure tells you how profitable and how risky it will be. Two companies with identical revenue forecasts can have very different EBITDA paths: one whose costs are mostly variable will see stable margins whatever happens to volume; one with a large fixed base will see margins expand sharply in growth years and collapse in a slowdown. A model that forecasts every cost as a percentage of revenue erases that difference — it produces static margins, no operating leverage and Bear cases in which profit falls exactly in proportion to revenue, which almost never happens in reality.

For a CMA, the underlying framework is familiar: variable, fixed, semi-variable and step costs; contribution; marginal costing; relevant range. The modelling task is to translate that framework into forecast rows driven by the right operating variables — volume and unit cost for materials and freight, headcount and CTC for people, area and stores for store opex, inflation and step-ups for overheads — while respecting Indian reporting specifics: the three-line Schedule III COGS structure, Ind AS 19 for gratuity and leave, and Ind AS 116 for leases, which has moved most rent out of opex altogether. The result is a cost build that responds sensibly when the scenario switch (Topic 6) moves volume, price, wage inflation or input costs.

The learning sequence follows the P&L. Start with COGS — choosing between % of revenue and volume × unit cost, and linking unit cost to commodities such as VAM for Pidilite or cotton for apparel (s01, c01). Build gross margin from the price-cost spread, mix and pass-through lags (s02). Then build employee cost from headcount × CTC × increments with Ind AS 19 components (s03, c02), classify the rest of SG&A by behaviour (s04), isolate the rent that genuinely remains in opex under Ind AS 116 (s05, c05), and add step-fixed costs triggered by thresholds (s06, c04). Finally, assemble the EBITDA margin bridge and analyse operating leverage, keeping D&A and finance costs out of opex (s07, c03, c06).

Related concepts to study

Phase 6: CVP, contribution margin and break-even

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): CVP, contribution margin, break-even analysis

The cost classification built here is the input to Phase 6's cost-volume-profit work. Contribution margin (revenue less variable costs) per unit and in total, break-even revenue (fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio) and margin of safety all require a credible split between variable and fixed costs — which a % of revenue model cannot supply. The degree of operating leverage (c03) is the reciprocal of the margin of safety ratio. Store-level and plant-level break-even analysis in Phase 6 uses the same per-store opex, staff and rent drivers defined in this topic, so building them cleanly now avoids rebuilding later.

Phase 1: Ind AS 19 and Ind AS 116 accounting

→ Phase 1 (W1–4): Ind AS 19 employee benefits, Ind AS 116 exemptions and variable lease payments

Two Phase 1 standards determine what appears in this topic's cost lines. Ind AS 19 separates defined contribution (PF: expense = contribution) from defined benefit plans (gratuity: service cost and net interest in P&L, remeasurements in OCI) and treats leave encashment as an other long-term benefit with remeasurements in P&L. Ind AS 116 capitalises fixed and index-linked lease payments, leaving only short-term (≤ 12 months), low-value and non-index variable payments in opex, and requires judgement on lease term and renewal options. Revisit the disclosures for expenses relating to short-term, low-value and variable leases — they are the calibration source for the residual rent line.

Phase 5: Cost-centre budgets and PVM variance analysis

→ Phase 5 (W17–20): cost-centre budgets, price/volume/mix variance analysis

FP&A budgets costs by cost centre — store, plant, region, corporate function — using the same drivers as this topic: headcount and CTC, area and opex per sq ft, volume and unit cost. Variance analysis in Phase 5 then splits actual vs budget cost into rate (price) and efficiency (volume/usage) variances, and revenue into price, volume and mix. A model whose cost lines are driver-based can be rolled into a budget template directly; one built on % of revenue cannot generate meaningful variances because every cost "variance" would simply mirror revenue.

CMA cost accounting foundations and CRA-1 records

CMA cost accounting: cost behaviour, marginal costing, Cost Audit records (CRA-1)

The CMA foundations — fixed, variable, semi-variable and step costs; high-low and regression methods for splitting semi-variable costs; marginal costing — are the conceptual core of this topic. For companies covered by the Companies (Cost Records and Audit) Rules, 2014, cost records maintained in the CRA-1 format provide product-level material, utilities, employee and overhead costs per unit. Where an analyst or FP&A team has access (internal roles, due diligence data rooms), these records are an excellent source for unit-cost calibration and for testing the variable/fixed split. Cost audit reports (CRA-3) also contain capacity and production data useful for t4 s04.

Common pitfalls

Every cost as % of revenue

Every cost as % of revenue, eliminating operating leverage and making margins static

The mistake: forecasting COGS, employee cost, A&P, freight, rent and overheads as historical-average ratios to revenue. It is popular because it is quick and always "looks reasonable". The consequence: EBITDA margin is constant, DOL is 1, Bear cases show margins unchanged, and growth years show no leverage — none of which matches how Indian retailers or manufacturers actually behave. The correct treatment is to classify each line by behaviour and drive it from volume, headcount, area, inflation and step thresholds (c01, s04), using % of revenue only for genuinely revenue-linked items such as A&P, royalty and commissions. Detect it in review by flexing revenue −10% and checking whether EBITDA margin moves; if it does not, the cost build is ratio-based.

Rent double-counted under Ind AS 116

Rent kept in opex while leases are also capitalised (double counting under Ind AS 116)

The mistake: forecasting historical "rent" as % of revenue in other expenses (often using pre-2019 ratios) while also building a lease schedule that generates ROU depreciation and lease interest for the same stores. It happens when the opex build and the lease schedule are built separately, or historical rent is taken from the cash flow statement. The correct treatment: opex rent contains only short-term, low-value and variable (e.g., revenue-share excess) rent, calibrated from the Ind AS 116 expense disclosures; all fixed rent lives in the lease schedule (s05, c05). Detect it by comparing total lease-related charges (opex rent + ROU depreciation + lease interest) with total lease cash outflows; a ratio well above 1 indicates double counting.

Constant gross margin despite input-cost inflation

Constant gross margin despite modelled input-cost inflation or pass-through lag

The mistake: the Assumptions sheet shows VAM or cotton prices rising 15%, but gross margin is hardcoded at the historical average, so the commodity assumption has no effect on profit — or price growth is set equal to cost growth with no lag. It happens because gross margin is treated as a single input rather than an output of price and cost. The correct treatment is a price-cost build: unit cost from a commodity-linked BOM, realisation with explicit pass-through ratios and lags, and gross margin as the result (s01, s02). Detect it by flexing the commodity input in a sensitivity: if EBITDA does not move, the link is missing; also compare modelled GM with the historical relationship between GM and lagged input prices.

Practice Questions

Q1. Pidilite-style cost build with VAM spike [L3]

An adhesives company's FY25 data (₹ crore unless stated): revenue 12,000 (volume 520 KT); material cost 5,880 of which VAM-linked materials 35%; employee cost 1,250 (headcount 7,600 at 31 March 2025; 8,000 at 31 March 2026); A&P 480; freight 420; power 210 (fixed 70); corporate overheads 600. For FY26: UVG 10%, price increase 3% from 1 July 2025 plus a further 4% from 1 January 2026; VAM landed cost +22%; other materials +3%; increment 9%; freight rate +5%; power tariff +3% and fixed power +5%; CPI 5%; A&P at 4.0% of revenue; a new R&D centre adds ₹25 crore of fixed overhead. (a) Build FY26 revenue, COGS, gross margin, employee cost, other opex and EBITDA; (b) present the EBITDA margin bridge in bps; (c) compute DOL using your cost classification; (d) identify which line would be wrong if all costs were held at FY25 ratios to revenue, and by how much.

💡 Hint: Time-weight both price increases before computing realisation, and scale variable materials by volume.

Q2. Trent-style store economics and Ind AS 116 [L2]

A value-fashion format has 705 stores at 31 March 2026 and 870 at 31 March 2027, average store area 8,000 sq ft and FY27 revenue per sq ft of ₹15,750 (average-area basis). Per store: 22 staff at CTC ₹2.8 lakh (FY26) with 8% increment; store opex ₹520 per sq ft a year (FY26) inflating 6%; fixed rent ₹1,050 per sq ft a year; 15% of stores are in malls with revenue-share rent where the excess over minimum guarantee averages ₹60 per sq ft. Area managers: one per 15 stores at ₹30 lakh (FY26) inflating 9%. (a) Compute FY27 store-level staff cost, store opex, area manager cost and the rent that stays in opex; (b) explain where fixed rent goes and estimate its effect on FY27 EBITDA vs a pre-Ind AS 116 view; (c) list the checks you would add to the model to prevent rent double counting.

Q3. Bear-case cost behaviour review [L3]

A model's Bear case shows revenue −12% versus Base but EBITDA margin unchanged at 18.5%. The cost sheet has COGS at 52% of revenue, employee cost at 9% of revenue, other expenses at 20.5% of revenue, and a lease schedule for 1,200 stores. (a) Diagnose every structural problem; (b) redesign the cost build with drivers for each line; (c) estimate, with stated assumptions, the more realistic Bear-case EBITDA margin; (d) describe how the Topic 6 scenario switch should flex input costs, pass-through and wage inflation coherently.

Q4. Interview case — margin outlook for a commodity-exposed consumer company [L2]

An interviewer says: "Crude derivatives are up 25% in the last six months. Walk me through how you would update your FY27 EBITDA margin forecast for a paints or adhesives company." Structure your answer around raw material share of COGS, inventory lag, pass-through history, competitive intensity, A&P flexibility and the margin bridge you would show.
