Subtopic note

COGS Forecasting: % of Revenue vs Volume × Unit Cost and Commodities

Scope: COGS: % of revenue vs volume × unit cost; commodity linkage (VAM for Pidilite, cotton/fabric for apparel retail)

Why it matters

Cost of goods sold is the largest cost line for most Indian manufacturers and retailers — roughly 45–55% of revenue for Pidilite, 55–60% for an apparel retailer like Trent, 80%+ for DMart. A one-percentage-point error in the COGS ratio moves EBITDA by 4–8% for these businesses. More importantly, the way COGS is forecast determines whether the model can answer the question every analyst asks of a commodity-exposed consumer company: what happens to margins if crude or VAM rises 20%? A COGS line forecast as a flat % of revenue cannot respond; a COGS line built from volume × unit cost, with unit cost linked to a commodity index, can.

In Schedule III Division II, COGS is not a single line. It is the sum of cost of materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade and changes in inventories of finished goods, work-in-progress and stock-in-trade. A model's "COGS" or "material cost" line must map these three lines explicitly from history (w13 t3 s02) before forecasting.

Core notes

Approach 1 — COGS as % of revenue

\[ \text{COGS}_t = \text{Revenue}_t \times (1 - \text{Gross margin}_t) \]

Gross margin (or its complement, the COGS ratio) is the driver. This is acceptable when:

  • the business is a retailer or trader whose purchase prices move with selling prices (a retailer prices off cost with a stable markup);
  • product-level volume and cost data are unavailable; or
  • the analysis is a quick screen rather than a decision model.

Its weakness: it assumes cost moves in lock-step with price. A price increase taken to protect margin and a price increase that expands margin look identical; a commodity spike with lagged price pass-through is invisible.

Approach 2 — Volume × unit cost

\[ \text{COGS}_t = \sum_i Q_{i,t} \times c_{i,t} \]

where \(Q_{i,t}\) is volume of product \(i\) and \(c_{i,t}\) is unit material cost (₹ per kg, per litre, per garment). Unit cost can be built from a bill of materials (BOM):

\[ c_{i,t} = \sum_j \text{Input quantity}_{i,j} \times \text{Input price}_{j,t} + \text{Conversion cost per unit} \]

Volume comes from the revenue build (t4 s01), so COGS and revenue share the same physical driver, and price-versus-cost dynamics become explicit: realisation and unit cost are separate rows that can move at different rates and times.

In practice a simplified BOM is enough: identify the two to four inputs that dominate cost and model the rest as "other materials" growing with inflation.

Commodity linkage — Pidilite and VAM

Vinyl acetate monomer (VAM) is the key raw material for Pidilite's polyvinyl acetate (PVA) adhesives such as Fevicol; other inputs include acrylics, solvents, epoxy resins and packaging. VAM prices are imported-parity linked (Asian contract prices in USD per tonne × USD/INR, plus duties and freight), so the model needs:

Row Driver Source
VAM price (USD/tonne) Input per year, scenario-flexed Industry price reports, management commentary
USD/INR Input per year Consensus / forward rates
VAM landed cost (₹/kg) USD price × FX × (1 + duty + freight %) ÷ 1,000 Calculation
VAM share of material cost Calibrated from history Annual report, concalls
Material cost index Weighted index of VAM, other chemicals, packaging Calculation

Pidilite management discusses gross margin explicitly in terms of VAM consumption cost; historical calibration is therefore possible: regress or compare gross margin against lagged VAM prices.

Commodity linkage — apparel retail and cotton/fabric

A retailer such as Trent buys finished garments (Westside and Zudio are largely private-label, sourced from vendors), so COGS appears mostly as purchases of stock-in-trade plus inventory change. Cotton and fabric prices feed through vendor costs with a lag of one to two seasons. Model:

\[ \text{COGS}_t = \text{Revenue}_t \times (1 - \text{Gross margin}_t), \quad \text{Gross margin}_t = \text{Base GM} - \beta \times \Delta \text{Cotton index}_{t-\text{lag}} + \text{Pricing response}_t \]

Gross margin in fast-fashion value retail is also a function of markdowns (end-of-season sales) and shrinkage, not just input costs; full-price sell-through is a key operating driver.

Worked illustration — volume × unit cost with VAM

An adhesives business sells 200 KT in FY26 at a realisation of ₹230 per kg (₹4,600 crore). Material cost per kg is built as follows (₹ per kg of finished product):

Component FY25A FY26E driver FY26E
VAM (0.35 kg × landed price ₹110 → ₹121) 38.5 VAM +10% 42.4
Other chemicals 45.0 +4% 46.8
Packaging 20.0 +5% 21.0
Total material cost per kg 103.5 110.2

FY26E material cost = 200 × 110.2 ÷ 10 = ₹2,204 crore (KT × ₹ per kg → ₹ crore requires ÷ 10, since 1 KT = 10 lakh kg). Gross margin = 1 − 2,204 ÷ 4,600 = 52.1%. If FY25 realisation was ₹225 per kg, gross margin fell from 54.0% to 52.1% — price rose 2.2% but unit cost 6.5%. A % of revenue model at FY25's 54% would overstate FY26 gross profit by about ₹88 crore.

Inventory change and COGS

Material consumed is not purchases. Under Schedule III:

\[ \text{COGS} = \text{Materials consumed} + \text{Purchases of stock-in-trade} + (\text{Opening} - \text{Closing inventory of FG, WIP, SIT}) \]

In a forecast, COGS is calculated first (from volume × unit cost or margin), and inventory is forecast separately as days of COGS in the working capital schedule (Week 14). Do not forecast purchases and inventory change separately in the P&L — that duplicates the working capital logic.

Excel structure

Unit_Cost_t   = SUMPRODUCT(BOM_Qty, Input_Price_t) + Conversion_t
COGS_t        = Volume_t * Unit_Cost_t / 10           ' KT x Rs/kg -> Rs crore
GM_pct_t      = IF(Revenue_t=0, 0, 1 - COGS_t/Revenue_t)

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Manufacturing overheads. Indian companies show power, fuel and stores under "other expenses", not COGS; peers abroad include them. Decide whether the model's gross margin is material margin or full manufacturing margin, and be consistent across peers.
  • Hedging and inventory holding. Companies holding 2–3 months of VAM inventory consume older, cheaper stock when prices rise — the lag (s02).
  • Excise and GST history. Pre-GST years included excise duty in revenue and expenses; re-base before computing historical gross margins.
  • Outsourced manufacturing. Contract-manufactured goods appear as purchases of stock-in-trade with higher unit cost and no conversion overhead.
  • Inventory write-downs and markdowns. NRV write-downs flow through inventory change; treat large one-off write-downs as normalisation items.

Key takeaways

  • Map COGS from the three Schedule III lines before forecasting.
  • % of revenue is acceptable for retailers/traders and quick screens; volume × unit cost is better for manufacturers.
  • Build unit cost from a simplified BOM with the dominant commodity (VAM for Pidilite) on its own row.
  • For apparel retail, link gross margin to lagged cotton/fabric costs, markdowns and pricing response.
  • Keep COGS and volume on the same physical driver as revenue.
  • Forecast inventory in working capital, not via purchases in the P&L.

Practice Questions

Q1. Unit-cost build with FX [L2]

An adhesives maker uses 0.32 kg of VAM per kg of product. VAM is USD 1,050 per tonne in FY25 and USD 1,200 in FY26; USD/INR averages 83.5 and 86.0; duty and freight add 9%. Other materials cost ₹62 per kg in FY25, rising 4%. FY26 volume is 180 KT at a realisation of ₹240 per kg. Compute unit material cost for both years, FY26 material cost in ₹ crore and the gross margin.

Q2. Choosing the method [L1]

For DMart, Pidilite and an auto-component maker, state whether you would forecast COGS as % of revenue or volume × unit cost, and justify each choice.

Q3. Schedule III mapping [L1]

A company reports cost of materials consumed ₹3,120 crore, purchases of stock-in-trade ₹640 crore and changes in inventories of ₹(85) crore (an increase in inventory). Compute COGS and explain the sign of the inventory line.

💡 Hint: An increase in closing inventory reduces COGS relative to purchases and consumption.

Q4. Apparel gross margin driver [L3]

Design a gross margin driver block for a value-fashion retailer that captures cotton/fabric costs with a two-season lag, markdown intensity and private-label share. Specify inputs, formulas and the historical data you would use to calibrate it.
