Gross Margin Build: Price-Cost Spread, Mix and Pass-Through Lags

Scope: Gross margin build: price-cost spread, mix, commodity pass-through lags

Why it matters

Gross margin is where pricing power, input costs and product mix meet. For Indian consumer and materials companies, the gross margin cycle drives most of the earnings volatility: paint and adhesive margins expanded sharply when crude derivatives fell in FY20–FY21, compressed in FY22–FY23 as VAM and other inputs spiked, and recovered as prices stayed sticky while inputs eased. Analysts who modelled the lag between input costs and price increases called these cycles; those with a constant gross margin missed them. In due diligence and FP&A, the gross margin bridge (price, cost, mix) is the first exhibit in any margin discussion.

Core notes

Price-cost spread

For a single product, gross profit per unit is the spread between realisation and unit material cost:

\[ \text{GP per unit}_t = P_t - c_t, \qquad \text{GM}_t = 1 - \frac{c_t}{P_t} \]

Gross margin moves when price and cost grow at different rates:

\[ \text{GM}_t = 1 - (1 - \text{GM}_{t-1}) \times \frac{1 + g_{c,t}}{1 + g_{P,t}} \]

Two different management objectives produce different outcomes. Protecting the rupee spread (passing on the absolute cost increase) keeps gross profit per unit constant but lowers gross margin %. Protecting the margin % requires a larger percentage price increase than the percentage cost increase when GM is high. With GM of 50%, a 10% cost increase needs a 10% price increase to keep GM at 50%, but only a 5% increase to keep the rupee spread.

Mix

Different products carry different gross margins. Portfolio gross margin is the revenue-weighted average:

\[ \text{GM}_t = \sum_i w_{i,t} \times \text{GM}_{i,t}, \qquad w_{i,t} = \frac{R_{i,t}}{R_t} \]

Mix shifts change the consolidated margin even if no product's margin changes — Pidilite's C&B segment carries higher margins than B2B; Trent's private-label share drives gross margin; premium waterproofing outperforms standard adhesives. Model GM at the segment or category level and let mix emerge from the revenue build.

Commodity pass-through lags

Prices rarely adjust instantly to input costs. Reasons:

  • Inventory holding — raw material bought months earlier flows through COGS at historical cost (Ind AS 2 weighted average or FIFO), so COGS lags spot prices by inventory days.
  • Price-setting cycles — consumer companies take price increases a few times a year; dealers need notice; competition delays moves.
  • Contracts — B2B customers often have quarterly or half-yearly price-revision clauses.

A practical approach is to model price as a partial, lagged response to cost:

\[ g_{P,t} = \alpha \times g_{c,t} + (1 - \alpha) \times g_{c,t-1} \times \phi + g_{P}^{\text{base}} \]

or, more simply in annual models, specify a pass-through ratio (the % of cost inflation recovered through price in the same year) and a catch-up in the following year. Calibrate from the historical relationship between input cost indices and realisation.

Inventory lag in COGS can be modelled by weighting current and prior-period input prices by inventory days:

\[ c^{\text{COGS}}_t = c^{\text{spot}}_t \times \left(1 - \frac{\text{RM days}}{365}\right) + c^{\text{spot}}_{t-1} \times \frac{\text{RM days}}{365} \]

Worked illustration — two-year lag cycle

A paint-like business has FY25 GM of 42%, realisation index 100, unit cost index 58. Input costs rise 15% in FY26 and are flat in FY27. The company passes through 40% of the cost increase in FY26 and recovers the rest of the rupee spread in FY27.

Line FY25A FY26E FY27E
Unit cost index 58.0 66.7 66.7
Rupee cost increase vs FY25 — 8.7 8.7
Price increase (rupees) — 3.5 (40%) 8.7 (100% cumulative)
Realisation index 100.0 103.5 108.7
Gross margin 42.0% 35.6% 38.6%

Even after full rupee pass-through in FY27, GM is lower than FY25 (38.6% vs 42.0%) because the company protected the spread, not the percentage. Restoring 42% requires the realisation index to reach 115 (66.7 ÷ 0.58).

Gross margin bridge output

The model should output a year-on-year gross margin bridge:

Bridge item Measurement
Opening GM % Prior year
Price effect Change in realisation at prior-year cost
Input cost effect Change in unit cost at current price
Mix effect Change in weights at prior-year segment margins
Other (markdowns, overhead absorption) Residual — should be small
Closing GM % Current year

Fix the sequence and document it; the residual should be near zero if the build is granular.

Excel implementation

Price_Growth_t = Base_Price_Growth_t + PassThru_Same_Yr*Cost_Growth_t + PassThru_Lag*Cost_Growth_{t-1}
Unit_Cost_t    = Unit_Cost_{t-1}*(1+Cost_Growth_t)
GM_t           = 1 - Unit_Cost_t/Realisation_t

Pass-through ratios are inputs on the Assumptions sheet and are among the most valuable drivers to flex in scenarios (Topic 6: input-cost spike narrative).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Downward pass-through. When input costs fall, companies often hold prices (margin expansion) or cut slowly; asymmetry should be modelled explicitly.
  • Competitive intensity. New entrants (e.g., large conglomerates entering paints) limit pass-through; reduce pass-through ratios in Bear cases.
  • Grammage changes. Reducing pack size at constant MRP is a hidden price increase — realisation per kg rises.
  • Regulated prices. Pharma (NPPA price controls under DPCO) and fertiliser businesses have limited price freedom; pass-through may be near zero or formula-based.
  • Overhead absorption. If the model includes manufacturing overheads in COGS, utilisation changes move GM through fixed-cost absorption, not price-cost.
  • Hedging. Commodity or FX hedges smooth input cost; model the hedged cost, not spot, for the hedged proportion.

Key takeaways

  • GM moves when price and cost grow at different rates; protecting the rupee spread lowers GM %.
  • Portfolio GM is the revenue-weighted average of segment margins; mix shifts move it independently.
  • Pass-through lags arise from inventory, price-setting cycles and contracts; model a same-year ratio and a catch-up.
  • Inventory days introduce a lag between spot input prices and COGS.
  • Output a GM bridge with price, cost and mix effects and a small residual.
  • Pass-through ratios are key scenario drivers.

Practice Questions

Q1. Spread vs percentage protection [L1]

A product sells at ₹400 per unit with a unit cost of ₹240. Unit cost rises 12%. Compute the price increase needed to (a) protect the rupee gross profit per unit and (b) protect the gross margin %, and the resulting gross margin in each case.

Q2. Inventory lag in COGS [L2]

A company holds 75 days of raw material inventory. Spot input cost is ₹80 per kg in FY25 and ₹96 per kg in FY26. Estimate FY26 input cost flowing through COGS and explain how this lag would reverse if prices fell in FY27.

💡 Hint: Weight current and prior-year spot prices by the share of the year covered by inventory days.

Q3. Mix-driven margin [L2]

A company has two segments: Consumer (revenue ₹7,800 crore, GM 55%) and B2B (₹2,200 crore, GM 38%). In FY26 Consumer grows 8% and B2B 18%, with no change in segment margins. Compute portfolio GM for both years and the mix effect in bps.

Q4. Pass-through calibration [L3]

You have ten years of a company's realisation growth, a weighted input-cost index and gross margins. Describe how you would estimate same-year and lagged pass-through ratios, what pitfalls (mix, GST changes, acquisitions) would bias the estimate, and how you would present the result in the assumption log.
