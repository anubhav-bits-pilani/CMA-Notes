Key concept

Variable vs Fixed Cost Forecasting Formulas with Step-Ups

Concept: Variable cost = Volume × Unit cost; Fixed cost_t = Fixed cost_t−1 × (1 + inflation) + step-ups

Statement

\[ VC_t = Q_t \times v_t, \qquad v_t = v_{t-1} \times (1 + \pi^{v}_t) \]

\[ FC_t = FC_{t-1} \times (1 + \pi_t) + S_t \]

where \(VC_t\) is variable cost in year \(t\), \(Q_t\) is the volume driver (units, tonnes, orders, area — whatever the cost physically scales with), \(v_t\) is unit variable cost, \(\pi^{v}_t\) is unit-cost inflation (commodity, freight or tariff index), \(FC_t\) is fixed cost, \(\pi_t\) is general or cost-specific inflation (CPI, WPI, wage increments), and \(S_t\) is the step-up in year \(t\) — an explicit addition when a capacity threshold is crossed or a new fixed commitment begins (a new regional office, a DC, an ERP licence). Semi-variable costs combine both: \(\text{Cost}_t = FC_t + VC_t\).

Explanation

This is the CMA cost-behaviour framework expressed as forecasting formulas. Its purpose in a model is to make costs respond to the right driver:

  • Variable costs respond to volume, not revenue. If price rises 5% with no volume change, variable costs should not rise (unless input costs also rise). Using % of revenue would wrongly raise them.
  • Fixed costs respond to time and inflation, not activity. If volume falls 10%, fixed costs do not fall — the core reason profits fall faster than revenue in a downturn.
  • Step-ups acknowledge that "fixed" is fixed only within a relevant range. Over a five-year forecast in which stores double, fixed costs cannot simply inflate; they step up as thresholds are crossed. Step-ups are either explicit inputs (\(S_t\) as a schedule) or generated by threshold formulas (c04).

Conditions: choose the volume driver carefully (freight scales with tonnes and distance; packing with units; store consumables with area or footfall). Choose the inflation index by cost type — wages use increments (8–10% in India), general overheads CPI (around 4–5%), industrial inputs WPI or commodity indices. One inflation row per index on the Assumptions sheet should feed every cost that uses it, so that scenarios flex inflation in one place.

The model must never let a fixed cost be re-derived as % of revenue in some years and inflation-grown in others; that breaks row consistency (w13 t2) and makes margin behaviour inexplicable.

Illustration

FY25: packing cost ₹180 crore on 150 KT volume (₹12 per kg); fixed plant overheads ₹220 crore. FY26: volume 165 KT, packing cost per kg +6%, general inflation 5%, and a new QC lab adds ₹8 crore of fixed cost.

\[ VC_{FY26} = 165 \times 12 \times 1.06 \div 10 = ₹209.9 \text{ crore} \]

\[ FC_{FY26} = 220 \times 1.05 + 8 = ₹239.0 \text{ crore} \]

Total grows from ₹400 crore to ₹448.9 crore (+12.2%). If revenue fell 10% with volume −10% instead, variable cost would be ₹171.7 crore but fixed cost still ₹239.0 crore — total ₹410.7 crore, up 2.7% while revenue falls.

Var_Cost_t   = Volume_t*Unit_Cost_t/10
Unit_Cost_t  = Unit_Cost_{t-1}*(1+Unit_Infl_t)
Fixed_Cost_t = Fixed_Cost_{t-1}*(1+CPI_t)+Step_Up_t

Practice Questions

Q1. Semi-variable forecast [L1]

Freight cost is ₹60 crore fixed (contracted trucks) plus ₹2,400 per tonne variable in FY25. FY26 volume is 2.1 lakh tonnes; diesel-linked variable rate rises 7%; the fixed contract escalates 4%. Forecast FY26 freight in ₹ crore.

Q2. Revenue down, cost up [L2]

Using the illustration's structure, construct an example where revenue falls 6% but total operating cost rises. Explain which cost behaviours produce this outcome and why a % of revenue model cannot.

💡 Hint: Combine a volume fall, a price rise and a fixed-cost step-up.

Q3. Choosing inflation indices [L2]

For a Pidilite-style business, assign an inflation index (CPI, WPI, wage increment, commodity index, specific tariff) to each of: plant power, corporate IT, packaging, factory wages, insurance, outward freight. Justify each choice.
