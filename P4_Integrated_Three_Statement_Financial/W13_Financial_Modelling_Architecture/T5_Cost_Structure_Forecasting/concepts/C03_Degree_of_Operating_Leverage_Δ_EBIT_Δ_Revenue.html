Key concept

Degree of Operating Leverage: % Δ EBIT ÷ % Δ Revenue

Concept: Degree of operating leverage = % Δ EBIT ÷ % Δ Revenue = Contribution ÷ EBIT

Statement

\[ \text{DOL} = \frac{\Delta \text{EBIT} \div \text{EBIT}}{\Delta R \div R} = \frac{C}{\text{EBIT}} = \frac{C}{C - F} \]

where \(R\) is revenue, \(C = R - VC\) is contribution (revenue less variable costs), \(F\) is fixed costs (cash fixed costs plus D&A for an EBIT-based measure) and \(\text{EBIT} = C - F\). The equality of the two forms holds when revenue changes through volume only, with selling price, unit variable cost and fixed costs unchanged, and DOL is measured at the base level of activity. EBITDA-based leverage replaces EBIT with EBITDA and \(F\) with cash fixed costs only.

Explanation

Operating leverage is the amplification of revenue changes into profit changes caused by fixed costs. If all costs were variable, EBIT would move exactly with revenue (DOL = 1). The larger fixed costs are relative to contribution, the larger DOL — and the more profit falls in a downturn and rises in an upturn.

Key properties:

  • DOL depends on the base. It is highest just above break-even (EBIT close to zero) and falls as EBIT grows. A company with DOL of 4x today will not have 4x after two years of strong growth.
  • Sign and symmetry. DOL applies to both increases and decreases in volume; a 10% volume fall with DOL of 3x cuts EBIT by 30%.
  • Price changes differ. A price increase with no change in volume flows almost entirely to profit (variable cost does not rise), giving a much higher effective leverage than a volume change. That is why the c03 equality is defined for volume changes.
  • Model behaviour. A model with every cost as % of revenue has DOL = 1 by construction — the first pitfall of this topic.

In valuation, high DOL raises earnings volatility and supports higher beta and wider scenario ranges. In FP&A, DOL indicates how much budget risk sits in volume shortfalls. For Indian retailers post-Ind AS 116, EBITDA-based DOL is lower than pre-116 (fixed rent left EBITDA), but EBIT-based DOL still captures ROU depreciation.

Illustration

A plant: revenue ₹2,000 crore, variable costs ₹1,200 crore, fixed cash costs ₹450 crore, D&A ₹110 crore.

\[ C = 2{,}000 - 1{,}200 = 800, \quad \text{EBIT} = 800 - 450 - 110 = 240, \quad \text{DOL} = 800 \div 240 = 3.33 \]

A 10% volume increase: revenue ₹2,200 crore, variable cost ₹1,320 crore, contribution ₹880 crore, EBIT ₹320 crore — EBIT growth 33.3% = 3.33 × 10%, as the formula predicts.

Check in the model by flexing volume ±10% in a sensitivity and comparing % Δ EBIT with the formula — the two should agree if the cost classification is coherent.

Contribution_t = Revenue_t - Variable_Costs_t
DOL_t          = IF(EBIT_t=0, NA(), Contribution_t/EBIT_t)

(Returning NA() near zero EBIT signals that DOL is undefined rather than displaying a misleading huge number.)

Practice Questions

Q1. DOL and margin of safety [L1]

Revenue ₹1,500 crore, variable costs 58% of revenue, fixed costs including D&A ₹480 crore. Compute DOL, the break-even revenue and the margin of safety, and verify that margin of safety equals 1 ÷ DOL.

Q2. DOL over time [L2]

Using the illustration's plant, compute DOL after two successive years of 10% volume growth with fixed costs inflating 5% a year. Explain why DOL changes.

💡 Hint: Recompute contribution and EBIT at each new volume level with the inflated fixed costs.

Q3. Price vs volume leverage [L2]

For the same plant, compare the % change in EBIT from (a) a 5% volume increase and (b) a 5% price increase with volume unchanged. Explain why the c03 formula applies to (a) but not (b).
