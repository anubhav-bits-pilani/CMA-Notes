Subtopic note

Coherent Scenario Narratives vs Single-Variable Tweaks

Scope: Coherent scenario narratives (demand slowdown, input-cost spike) vs independent single-variable tweaks

Why it matters

A scenario is a story about the world in which several drivers move together for a common reason. "Bear" should not mean "every driver set 10% worse"; it should mean something like "urban discretionary demand slows for two years, competitors discount, and we slow store openings". The distinction matters because decisions made on scenarios — whether a company can service its debt, whether an acquisition still clears its hurdle, how much budget headroom to hold — depend on the combined, internally consistent effect. Investment committees and lenders routinely challenge scenarios that cannot be explained in one sentence. Single-variable tweaks still have a role, but that role is sensitivity analysis (Week 16), not scenarios.

Core notes

Scenarios vs sensitivities

Aspect Scenario Sensitivity
What changes A coherent set of drivers, linked by a narrative One or two drivers, others held constant
Question answered "What if this world happens?" "How much does output depend on this variable?"
Number of cases Few (3–5) Many points on a grid
Mechanism Scenario switch and driver blocks Data tables, tornado charts
Use Planning, credit, investment decision Identifying key drivers, valuation ranges

Building a narrative

Each case should have a written narrative of three to five sentences on the Assumptions sheet (or a Scenario notes sheet), then translate it into driver values:

  1. Trigger — what happens and why (macro, industry, company-specific).
  2. Transmission — which drivers move and in which direction, including second-order effects.
  3. Management response — what the company would plausibly do (cut A&P, slow openings, take price).
  4. Duration — how long the shock lasts and how drivers recover.
  5. Evidence — historical analogues (FY20–FY21 lockdowns, FY22–FY23 commodity spike, FY17 demonetisation and GST transition) that calibrate magnitudes.

Example narrative 1 — demand slowdown (Bear for Trent)

Urban discretionary consumption slows for FY27–FY28 as real income growth weakens; value fashion competition intensifies with new entrants discounting. Trent slows store additions by a third and holds prices; LFL turns flat; markdowns rise. Recovery from FY29.

Example narrative 2 — input-cost spike (Bear for Pidilite)

Crude and VAM prices rise 35% over two quarters on supply disruption; the rupee depreciates 4%. Pidilite passes on 40% of the cost increase in the first year and the rest over the following year; demand elasticity reduces UVG by two points in the first year. A&P is trimmed to protect margins.

Each driver change is traceable to the narrative. In the model, the Bear rows in each driver block hold these values; the switch flips them together.

Why independent tweaks fail as scenarios

  • Incoherence. Lower volume with unchanged capex and NWC days implies a company that keeps building stores into a downturn and holds stock perfectly — implausible.
  • Double severity. Setting every driver to its 10th percentile simultaneously produces a case far more extreme than any plausible world (joint probability is tiny); lenders reject it as unrealistic, and management ignores it.
  • Missed offsets. Real downturns carry offsets — commodity deflation in a demand slowdown, lower A&P, slower hiring. Ignoring them overstates the downside.
  • Unexplainable outputs. Without a narrative, an output change cannot be attributed to a cause.

Macro-linked scenarios

Where drivers share a macro cause, flex the macro driver and let the model derive the rest: CPI affects price, wages and overheads; USD/INR affects import costs and international revenue; GDP growth affects UVG via elasticity (t4 s07). This enforces coherence automatically. Keep company-specific responses (store adds, A&P) as explicit case inputs.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Bull case discipline. Bull narratives also need coherence — faster growth requires more capex, working capital, hiring and possibly lower margins on new stores.
  • Management case vs Base. A management business plan is often more optimistic than an analyst Base; keep it as a separate case with its own narrative rather than relabelling.
  • Stress tests. Lender stress cases (Week 15) may deliberately combine adverse moves beyond a coherent narrative to test covenant headroom; label them as stress tests, not scenarios.
  • Probability weighting. If cases are probability-weighted for valuation, narratives should be mutually exclusive and the weights justified.
  • Updating. Narratives age; refresh them when guidance or macro conditions change, and record changes in the assumption log.

Key takeaways

  • Scenarios are coherent stories moving several drivers together; sensitivities flex one variable at a time.
  • Write a short narrative per case: trigger, transmission, management response, duration, evidence.
  • Translate every driver change back to the narrative; untraceable changes do not belong.
  • Avoid setting every driver to its worst value — implausible joint severity.
  • Flex shared macro drivers and let the model derive dependent effects.
  • Keep stress tests and management cases labelled separately.

Practice Questions

Q1. Write a Bull narrative [L2]

Write a coherent Bull narrative for Trent covering demand, store rollout, margins, capex and working capital, and translate it into a driver table with Base and Bull values for six drivers.

Q2. Diagnose an incoherent Bear [L2]

A model's Bear case cuts UVG from 9% to 3% but keeps price realisation +3%, gross margin, A&P %, capex, wage increment and DIO at Base values. List the inconsistencies and propose corrected Bear values with a one-paragraph narrative.

💡 Hint: Ask how management and the supply chain would respond to sharply lower volume.

Q3. Scenario vs sensitivity [L1]

Explain to a junior analyst the difference between a scenario and a sensitivity, with an example of each for a cement company, and state which mechanism in the model implements each.

Q4. Calibrating from history [L3]

Using the FY22–FY23 commodity spike as an analogue, describe how you would calibrate the magnitude and duration of the input-cost-spike scenario for a paints or adhesives company: which data you would extract from quarterly results and concall transcripts, and how you would translate them into pass-through ratios and gross margin paths.
