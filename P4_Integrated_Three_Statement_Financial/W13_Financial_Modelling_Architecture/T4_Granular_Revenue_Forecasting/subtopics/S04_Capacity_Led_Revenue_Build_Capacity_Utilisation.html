Subtopic note

Capacity-Led Revenue Build: Capacity, Utilisation, Realisation

Scope: Capacity-led build: installed capacity × utilisation × realisation per unit; debottlenecking linked to capex

Why it matters

In manufacturing-heavy sectors — cement, steel, specialty chemicals, paper, auto components, pharma API, and for Pidilite's VAM-based and construction-chemical plants — the binding constraint on revenue is physical capacity, not demand. A cement company cannot sell 40 million tonnes from 35 million tonnes of grinding capacity, however strong housing demand is. A capacity-led build forces the model to respect that ceiling and, crucially, ties revenue growth to capex: every additional tonne of volume beyond the current practical maximum requires a greenfield plant, brownfield expansion or debottlenecking project that appears in the PPE schedule (Week 14) with a cost, a commissioning date and a ramp-up. Lenders and rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA) evaluate project finance and capex-led growth on exactly this logic — capacity, utilisation ramp, realisation and cost per unit — so the build doubles as a credit-appraisal framework you already know from CMA project appraisal.

Core notes

The core identity

\[ \text{Revenue}_t = \text{Capacity}_t \times \text{Utilisation}_t \times \text{Realisation}_t \]

  • Installed capacity — rated annual output (MTPA for cement, KTPA for chemicals, units for auto components). Use effective capacity in the year: a plant commissioned on 1 October contributes half its annual capacity in that year.
  • Utilisation — production ÷ effective capacity. Practical maximum is typically 85–90% for continuous process plants (planned shutdowns, maintenance, grade changes) and lower for batch or multi-product plants.
  • Realisation per unit — net revenue ÷ volume sold, net of GST, freight-on-sales recoveries if presented net, discounts and incentives (Ind AS 115). Cement companies quote realisation per tonne; chemical companies per kg.

Sales volume can differ from production by the change in finished-goods inventory; for most annual models assume sales ≈ production, or model inventory days explicitly in working capital.

Effective capacity with commissioning dates

\[ \text{Effective capacity}_t = \text{Opening capacity}_t + \sum_{k} \text{New capacity}_k \times \frac{\text{Months operating in } t}{12} \]

New lines also ramp: a new kiln may run at 50–60% utilisation in its first year. Model either (a) a separate utilisation path for new capacity, or (b) a blended utilisation for the whole fleet. Option (a) is more transparent and mirrors the vintage logic of s03.

Worked illustration — cement

Line (MTPA unless stated) FY25A FY26E FY27E
Opening capacity 30.0 30.0 36.0
New line commissioned 1 Oct 2025 — 6.0 —
Effective capacity 30.0 33.0 36.0
Utilisation — existing 78% 82% 85%
Utilisation — new line — 55% 75%
Volume (mn tonnes) 23.40 24.60 + 1.65 = 26.25 25.50 + 4.50 = 30.00
Realisation (₹ per tonne) 5,200 5,330 5,437
Revenue (₹ crore) 12,168 13,991 16,311

FY26E new-line volume = 6.0 × 6/12 × 55% = 1.65 mn tonnes. Revenue = volume (mn tonnes) × ₹ per tonne ÷ 10 → ₹ crore. Realisation growth (2.5%, 2.0%) is an input tied to industry pricing, not a plug.

Utilisation cap and the capacity constraint

Demand-led and capacity-led logic should meet in the model. Forecast demand-driven volume (market growth × share), then cap it:

Volume_t = MIN(Demand_Volume_t, Effective_Capacity_t * Max_Utilisation)
Shortfall_t = MAX(0, Demand_Volume_t - Effective_Capacity_t * Max_Utilisation)

The Shortfall row is a diagnostic: a persistent positive shortfall signals that capex needs to be scheduled (or that the demand assumption is aggressive). Display it on the Checks sheet as a warning rather than a hard error.

Debottlenecking linked to capex

Debottlenecking adds capacity by removing a constraint in an existing plant — an extra mill, a larger cooler, a new reactor in a multi-stage chemical process — at a fraction of greenfield cost and with shorter lead time (6–18 months vs 24–36 months greenfield). Model each project as a row with:

Field Example
Capacity added 1.5 MTPA
Capex ₹600 crore (₹400 crore per MTPA vs ₹1,000–1,200 crore greenfield)
Capex phasing 40% FY26, 60% FY27
Commissioning date 1 January 2027
Ramp-up utilisation 70% first year

The capacity row in revenue and the capex row in the PPE schedule must reference the same project table, so flexing a project's date in the Bear case delays both the spend and the revenue. Capital-work-in-progress (CWIP) capitalises to gross block on commissioning; depreciation starts when the asset is available for use (Ind AS 16).

Capex intensity per unit of capacity — ₹ crore per MTPA, ₹ crore per KTPA — is a key cross-check against peer announcements and the company's own past projects. An expansion modelled at half the industry cost per tonne needs a stated reason (brownfield, land already owned).

Multi-product plants

Specialty chemicals and FMCG plants make many products on shared lines. Capacity is then a constraint on total throughput (tonnes or machine-hours), and mix decisions (s01) change realisation. A simple approach: cap total tonnes, allocate to products by forecast mix, and apply product-level realisations.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Nameplate vs effective capacity. Announced capacity may include clinker capacity but not grinding, or vice versa; cement revenue is constrained by the bottleneck stage. Model at the bottleneck.
  • Outsourced/contract manufacturing. Pidilite and many FMCG companies use third-party manufacturers; volume can exceed owned capacity without capex. Model contract volume separately with its own (lower) margin.
  • Utilisation above 90%. Reported utilisation over 100% often reflects old nameplate ratings; re-base capacity rather than carry utilisation above the practical maximum.
  • Regional capacity. Cement is regional; national utilisation hides regional surpluses. For regional players, model by region with regional realisation.
  • Delays. Indian greenfield projects frequently slip 6–12 months (land acquisition, environmental clearances). Bear cases should delay commissioning, not just lower utilisation.
  • PLI and incentives. State capital subsidies and PLI incentives affect realisation or other operating revenue; Ind AS 20 governs presentation. Keep them on a separate row.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue = effective capacity × utilisation × realisation; use time-weighted capacity for mid-year commissioning.
  • Cap utilisation at a practical maximum (~85–90%) and surface any demand shortfall as a diagnostic.
  • New capacity ramps; model its utilisation path separately from existing capacity.
  • Debottlenecking adds cheaper, faster capacity; link every capacity row to a capex row via one project table.
  • Cross-check capex per unit of capacity against peers and past projects.
  • Constrain at the bottleneck stage and treat contract manufacturing separately.

Practice Questions

Q1. Effective capacity and revenue [L1]

A specialty chemicals company has 120 KTPA of capacity at 31 March 2025 and commissions a 40 KTPA line on 1 December 2025. Existing utilisation in FY26 is 84%, the new line runs at 50% of its effective capacity, and realisation is ₹2.20 lakh per tonne. Compute FY26 effective capacity, volume and revenue in ₹ crore.

Q2. Demand vs capacity [L2]

A cement company's regional demand model gives FY27 volume of 31.5 mn tonnes. Effective capacity is 34 MTPA and the practical maximum utilisation is 88%. Write the Excel formulas for constrained volume and shortfall, compute both, and recommend what the model should do about the shortfall.

💡 Hint: Compare demand with capacity times maximum utilisation before deciding whether capex is needed.

Q3. Debottlenecking project table [L2]

Design a project table for three capacity projects (one debottlenecking, two greenfield) with capacity, capex, phasing and commissioning date, and explain how both the revenue sheet and the PPE/CWIP schedule will reference it. Include what happens when the Bear case delays the greenfield projects by nine months.

Q4. Capex intensity cross-check [L3]

Management announces a 5 MTPA brownfield cement expansion for ₹3,500 crore while recent peer greenfield announcements average ₹1,100 crore per MTPA. Assess the reasonableness of the guidance, list the questions you would ask on the concall, and explain how you would model a cost-overrun scenario.

Q5. Outsourced volume [L1]

An adhesives company sells 15% of its volume from third-party manufacturers. Explain why this volume should not be constrained by owned capacity and how it changes the cost and capex modelling.
