Subtopic note

Top-Down Revenue Cross-Check: Market Size, Share and Nominal GDP

Scope: Top-down cross-check: market size × market share vs industry growth and nominal GDP

Why it matters

A bottom-up build (stores × density, volume × price, capacity × utilisation) is internally consistent but can still be wrong in aggregate: stacking optimistic store adds, LFL and maturity curves can produce a forecast in which a retailer captures an implausible share of the Indian apparel market by year five. The top-down cross-check asks the outside-in question: given the size and growth of the market, what market share does my forecast imply, and is that share path believable? Investment committees, IPO due-diligence teams and rating committees always ask it; industry reports from CRISIL, Redseer, Technopak or Euromonitor that are quoted in Indian DRHPs provide the market data. In FP&A the same check sits behind strategic-plan reviews — a five-year plan that needs 40% share in a fragmented market is a plan to be challenged.

Core notes

The identity

\[ \text{Company revenue}_t = \text{Market size}_t \times \text{Market share}_t \]

and therefore

\[ (1 + g_{\text{company}}) = (1 + g_{\text{market}}) \times \frac{\text{Share}_t}{\text{Share}_{t-1}} \]

Company growth above market growth requires share gain; a forecast implying 25% annual growth in a market growing 11% implies share rising by about 12.6% relative each year — compounding to more than 1.8x the starting share in five years.

Building the market size

  1. Anchor to an industry report. Indian apparel market, organised share and segment (value, mid, premium); adhesives and sealants market; construction chemicals; cement demand (mn tonnes). Note the base year, currency (₹ vs USD), and whether the figure is at retail selling prices (consumer spend) or manufacturer realisation.
  2. Convert to the company's revenue basis. Market size at MRP includes GST and channel margins; a manufacturer's revenue is net of both. Multiply by \(1 \div (1 + \text{GST})\) and by \((1 - \text{channel margin})\) before computing share. A retailer's revenue is at net retail prices (excluding GST), so only GST needs stripping.
  3. Grow the market with macro drivers. Link to nominal GDP growth (real growth + inflation; India's nominal GDP growth has typically been 10–12%) via an elasticity:

\[ g_{\text{market}} = \beta \times g_{\text{nominal GDP}} \]

where \(\beta\) is the category's income elasticity — above 1 for discretionary categories with rising penetration (organised apparel, home improvement, construction chemicals), close to 1 for staples, and below 1 for mature categories. Calibrate \(\beta\) from history.

  1. Split organised vs unorganised. In India much growth for listed players comes from formalisation — the organised share of apparel retail rising, or branded adhesives replacing loose/unbranded products. Model organised share separately from the company's share of the organised segment.

Worked illustration — value-fashion retailer

Line FY25A FY30E CAGR
Indian apparel market at net retail prices (₹ crore) 5,00,000 8,05,000 10.0%
Organised share 40% 50% —
Organised market (₹ crore) 2,00,000 4,02,500 15.0%
Bottom-up company revenue (₹ crore) 10,000 40,000 32.0%
Implied share of organised market 5.0% 9.9% —

The bottom-up forecast doubles the company's share of the organised market in five years. That may be defensible for a format gaining share rapidly — but it must be stated, compared with the share gains of the best historical performer, and flexed in scenarios. If the implied share reached 20%, the bottom-up drivers would be revisited.

Industry growth and nominal GDP checks

  • Industry growth vs GDP. A category growing at 2x nominal GDP for a decade needs a penetration or formalisation story. Check against per-capita consumption benchmarks (e.g., per-capita adhesives or paint consumption in India vs China/South-East Asia).
  • Company vs industry. Compare company volume growth with industry volume growth (cement company volume vs national cement demand; Pidilite UVG vs construction activity and housing starts).
  • Terminal-year sanity. By the final forecast year, company growth should converge towards industry growth, which should converge towards nominal GDP — the basis for the terminal growth rate in DCF (Week 16). A model still growing 25% in year five makes the terminal value hypersensitive.

Presenting the cross-check

Add a "Top-down" block on the Revenue sheet (or an Analysis sheet) with market size, organised share, implied company share and share gain per year, plus a flag:

Implied_Share_t      = Company_Revenue_t / Organised_Market_t
Share_Gain_bps_t     = (Implied_Share_t - Implied_Share_{t-1}) * 10000
Flag_Share_t         = --(Share_Gain_bps_t > Max_Share_Gain_bps)

Max_Share_Gain_bps is an input on the Assumptions sheet (e.g., 100 bps a year) justified from historical share gains. The flag feeds the Checks sheet as a warning, not a hard error — it prompts review rather than blocking.

Reconciling the two approaches

Do not average the bottom-up and top-down answers. The bottom-up build is the forecast; the top-down view is the test. Where they conflict, decide which driver is wrong — store adds too high, density growth too aggressive, market report too conservative — and document the decision in the assumption log.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Market data quality. Industry reports in DRHPs are commissioned and often optimistic; triangulate with government data (MOSPI private consumption, cement production indices, IIP).
  • Definitional mismatch. "Apparel market" may include footwear and accessories; "construction chemicals" may exclude waterproofing. Align the scope with the company's actual portfolio.
  • New-category creation. Where a company creates the category (e.g., early branded waterproofing), market data lag; use penetration-based market sizing (households × penetration × spend).
  • Export-led businesses. For international segments, the relevant market is global or regional, and FX matters; nominal GDP of India is the wrong anchor.
  • Inflation regimes. In high-inflation years, nominal market growth jumps without volume growth; compare volume with real GDP and value with nominal GDP.

Key takeaways

  • Company revenue = market size × market share; growth above market growth requires share gain.
  • Convert market data to the company's revenue basis (strip GST and channel margins) before computing share.
  • Grow the market with nominal GDP × an elasticity, and model organised share separately in India.
  • Show implied share and share gain per year with a warning flag against a justified maximum.
  • Use the top-down view to test the bottom-up forecast, never to average with it.
  • Growth should converge to industry and nominal GDP growth by the terminal year.

Practice Questions

Q1. Implied share path [L1]

A construction-chemicals market is ₹30,000 crore at manufacturer prices in FY25, growing at 13% a year. A company's bottom-up revenue is ₹3,600 crore in FY25 rising to ₹7,800 crore in FY30. Compute the market size in FY30, the implied share in each end year, and the average annual share gain in basis points.

Q2. Converting an MRP market size [L2]

An industry report sizes the Indian adhesives market at ₹42,000 crore at MRP (consumer prices including 18% GST). Distributor and retailer margins average 22% of the GST-exclusive retail price. A manufacturer reports domestic adhesives revenue of ₹9,500 crore. Compute the manufacturer's share on a comparable basis.

💡 Hint: Strip GST first, then the channel margin, to reach a manufacturer-price market size.

Q3. Elasticity calibration [L2]

Over FY15–FY25 organised apparel retail grew at a 14% CAGR while nominal GDP grew at 10.5%. Estimate the elasticity, explain why it may not persist, and specify how you would set the elasticity for FY26–FY30 in Base, Bull and Bear cases.

Q4. Conflict between bottom-up and top-down [L3]

Your bottom-up Zudio build implies the format's share of organised value fashion rises from 8% to 22% in five years. Describe the review you would conduct, which drivers you would revisit first, and how you would document the final decision for an investment committee.
