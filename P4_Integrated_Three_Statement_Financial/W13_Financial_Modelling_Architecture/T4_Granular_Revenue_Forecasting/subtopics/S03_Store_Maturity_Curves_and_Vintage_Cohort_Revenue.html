Subtopic note

Store Maturity Curves and Vintage Cohort Revenue Builds

Scope: Store maturity curve with vintage cohorts: new stores ramp to mature throughput over 2–3 years

Why it matters

A new store does not open at mature productivity. Footfall builds as the catchment learns about the store, assortment is tuned to local demand, and staff learn the location. In Indian apparel and grocery retail, a new store typically delivers 55–75% of mature revenue per sq ft in its first full year, 80–90% in year two and reaches maturity in year three. For a rapidly expanding chain — Zudio has added well over 150 stores a year recently — a large share of the fleet is always immature. Applying mature density to the whole fleet overstates revenue; applying the reported blended density understates future growth as recent vintages mature. A vintage (cohort) build resolves both errors and gives a more defensible answer to the question every analyst asks of a high-growth retailer: how much growth is "baked in" from stores already opened?

The same logic underlies capacity ramp-ups (a new plant at 50% utilisation in year one), branch maturity in NBFCs and microfinance, and new-customer cohorts in subscription businesses (s05).

Core notes

Vintage cohort definition

A vintage is the set of stores opened in the same financial year. Each vintage carries:

  • Number of stores and area at opening (net of any later closures in that vintage).
  • Age in year \(t\): \(t - \text{vintage year}\), with age 0 being the opening year.
  • Maturity factor \(m_{a}\): revenue per sq ft at age \(a\) as a fraction of mature revenue per sq ft.

The mature stores (vintages old enough to be at full throughput) form one block whose density rolls forward at LFL. Revenue for year \(t\):

\[ R_t = \sum_{v} A_{v} \times w_{v,t} \times m_{t-v} \times D_t \]

where \(A_v\) is area of vintage \(v\), \(w_{v,t}\) is the fraction of year \(t\) the vintage operated (about 0.5 in the opening year if openings are evenly spread; 1 thereafter), \(m_{t-v}\) is the maturity factor at age \(t-v\), and \(D_t\) is mature revenue per sq ft in year \(t\) (rolling forward with LFL).

Typical maturity curve

Age (full years since opening) Opening year (age 0) Age 1 Age 2 Age 3+
Maturity factor \(m_a\) 65% 85% 95% 100%
Time weight in year ~0.5 1.0 1.0 1.0

Calibrate the curve using: (a) management commentary on payback and new-store productivity; (b) the gap between blended density and LFL-based mature density in history; (c) peer disclosures. Curves differ by format: value fashion in Tier-2/3 towns often ramps faster (impulse, price-led traffic) than premium formats in malls, which depend on mall footfall building up.

The cohort triangle in Excel

The standard layout is a triangle: vintages down the rows, forecast years across the columns.

Vintage Stores Area (mn sq ft) FY25A FY26E FY27E FY28E
Mature (≤ FY22) 250 2.00 100% 100% 100% 100%
FY23 60 0.48 95% 100% 100% 100%
FY24 90 0.72 85% 95% 100% 100%
FY25 160 1.28 65% × 0.5 85% 95% 100%
FY26 180 1.44 — 65% × 0.5 85% 95%

The maturity factor in each cell is looked up from the curve using age, not typed:

=IF(Year<Vintage, 0,
    INDEX(Maturity_Curve, MIN(Year-Vintage+1, Max_Age+1)) * IF(Year=Vintage, Open_Year_Weight, 1))

Maturity_Curve is a named input row (65%, 85%, 95%, 100%) and Max_Age caps the lookup so every vintage aged 3+ uses 100%. One formula fills the whole triangle — the age arithmetic replaces any hand-placed diagonal.

Worked illustration

Using the table above and mature density \(D\) of ₹15,000 per sq ft in FY26 (₹ crore = mn sq ft × ₹ psf ÷ 10):

Vintage Effective area FY26 (mn sq ft) Revenue FY26 (₹ crore)
Mature + FY23 2.48 × 100% 3,720
FY24 0.72 × 95% = 0.684 1,026
FY25 1.28 × 85% = 1.088 1,632
FY26 1.44 × 65% × 0.5 = 0.468 702
Total 4.72 7,080

The fleet's "effective" area is 4.72 mn sq ft against average physical area of 5.20 mn sq ft [(4.48 + 5.92) ÷ 2], so blended revenue per sq ft is ₹13,615 — about 9% below mature density. As FY25 and FY26 vintages mature, blended density rises even with zero LFL. That embedded growth is what the cohort model makes visible.

Linking to LFL

LFL (s02) applies to mature density \(D_t\). Immature stores grow faster than LFL simply by ramping. Companies usually exclude stores under 12 months old from LFL, so a store in its second year is inside the LFL base while still maturing — one reason reported LFL can exceed "true" mature productivity growth during rapid expansion. Document whether your curve's year-2 step-up is already captured in reported LFL to avoid counting it twice.

Ramp-up costs and new-store economics

The cohort build should mirror cost: pre-opening costs (expensed under Ind AS — not capitalisable as PPE unless directly attributable), fit-out capex, rent (ROU asset from lease commencement), and staff from day one. New-store EBITDA margins are therefore lower than mature margins — a fast-growing chain's consolidated margin is diluted by immature stores. Payback period (fit-out capex + working capital ÷ annual store EBITDA at maturity) is the metric management quotes; 18–36 months is typical for value retail.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Cannibalisation. New stores in an existing catchment take revenue from nearby stores; LFL of mature stores falls. Apply a cannibalisation haircut to mature LFL when density of openings in existing cities rises.
  • Vintage closures. Underperforming stores are closed in years 2–3; closures should be deducted from the relevant vintage, not the mature base, or the maturity mix is distorted.
  • New city vs infill. New-city stores ramp slower (brand awareness) but cannibalise less. Separate curves by store type if the data supports it.
  • Curve that overshoots. Some stores peak above long-run maturity after a launch "honeymoon"; a curve above 100% at age 1 needs evidence.
  • Data scarcity. Most listed companies do not disclose store-level data. Calibrate to the blended density gap and state the curve as a key assumption on the assumption log; flex it in Bear/Bull (Topic 6).
  • Exogenous shocks. FY21 lockdowns broke all maturity patterns; exclude or normalise those vintages when calibrating.

Key takeaways

  • New stores ramp to mature density over 2–3 years; a vintage build captures the ramp explicitly.
  • Revenue = Σ vintage area × time weight × maturity factor × mature density.
  • One age-based lookup formula fills the cohort triangle; never hand-place diagonal factors.
  • Blended density below mature density signals embedded growth as recent vintages mature.
  • Avoid double-counting ramp-up already inside reported LFL.
  • Mirror the revenue ramp with pre-opening costs and diluted new-store margins.

Practice Questions

Q1. Cohort triangle for three years [L2]

A retailer has 1.80 mn sq ft of mature area at 31 March 2025 and plans additions of 0.50, 0.60 and 0.70 mn sq ft in FY26, FY27 and FY28 (openings evenly spread). The maturity curve is 60%, 80%, 95%, 100% (age 0 to 3), with opening-year time weight 0.5. Mature density is ₹12,000 per sq ft in FY26 growing at 5% LFL. Build revenue by vintage for FY26–FY28 in ₹ crore and compute blended revenue per sq ft.

Q2. Excel lookup design [L2]

Write a single Excel formula, using named ranges for the vintage year, model year, maturity curve and maximum age, that returns the maturity factor for any cell of a vintage triangle, including zero before opening and the half-year weight in the opening year.

💡 Hint: Combine an age calculation with INDEX and MIN, then multiply by an opening-year flag.

Q3. Calibrating a curve from disclosed data [L3]

A listed value retailer discloses only total area and revenue each year, plus an annual LFL figure. Over FY22–FY25, blended revenue per sq ft grew 3% a year while LFL averaged 7%. Explain what this gap implies about the maturity curve, and set out a calibration approach and its limitations.

Q4. Judgement — cannibalisation [L3]

Management guides 200 new stores in FY26, 60% of them in cities where the company already has 10+ stores. How would you reflect cannibalisation in the cohort model, and what evidence from concalls or store-level data would you seek?
