Subtopic note

Volume × Price × Mix Revenue Decomposition (UVG, Realisation, Mix)

Scope: Volume × Price × Mix decomposition: underlying volume growth (UVG), price realisation, mix shift

Why it matters

Every Indian consumer and materials company reports revenue growth in rupees, but managements guide, and analysts argue, in volume. Pidilite's quarterly commentary leads with "underlying volume growth" in Consumer & Bazaar; Asian Paints talks of "volume growth vs value growth"; cement companies report tonnes and realisation per tonne. A forecast that models only rupee growth cannot answer the questions an investment committee or a CFO actually asks: how much of next year's 12% growth is price we have already taken, how much is real demand, and how much is customers trading up to premium products? Decomposition also matters for costs (Topic 5): volume drives raw-material consumption and freight; price does not. If price and volume are blurred, gross margin and operating leverage in the model are wrong by construction.

In FP&A, the same decomposition becomes the price-volume-mix (PVM) variance analysis of Phase 5: budget versus actual revenue is split into the effect of selling more units, charging a different price and selling a different blend. Building the forecast in the same three components means the variance bridge falls out of the model automatically.

Core notes

The identity

Revenue for a single product is volume times realisation. For a portfolio, revenue is the sum over SKUs or categories:

\[ R_t = \sum_{i} Q_{i,t} \times P_{i,t} \]

where \(Q_{i,t}\) is volume of product \(i\) in year \(t\) (tonnes, litres, units) and \(P_{i,t}\) is net realisation per unit (net of GST, trade discounts and schemes, as recognised under Ind AS 115). Growth then compounds multiplicatively:

\[ (1 + g_R) = (1 + g_Q) \times (1 + g_P) \times (1 + g_M) \]

with \(g_Q\) = total volume growth, \(g_P\) = like-for-like price change and \(g_M\) = mix effect. The cross-term matters: 8% volume and 5% price give 13.4% value growth, not 13%.

Underlying volume growth (UVG)

UVG is the volume growth of the existing business, excluding acquisitions, divestments, new categories launched in the year, and one-offs such as channel stocking ahead of a price increase. Pidilite, for example, reports UVG for Consumer & Bazaar separately from reported value growth. Steps to derive a clean historical UVG:

  1. Start from reported volume (or management-disclosed volume growth from the investor presentation/concall).
  2. Strip acquired volumes for the first twelve months after acquisition (inorganic contribution).
  3. Adjust for known channel inventory movements — e.g., pre-buying in March ahead of an April price hike inflates Q4 and depresses Q1.
  4. Use a consistent base: if the company changes its volume metric (kg vs litres, or excludes a category), re-base the prior year.

Forecast UVG is a judgement anchored to real drivers: category penetration, rural/urban demand, construction and housing activity for adhesives/paints, and the company's own guidance. Typical Indian consumer UVG ranges 6–12% in normal years; anything above 15% for several years needs an explicit story (new distribution, category creation).

Price realisation

Price realisation is the change in net revenue per unit on a like-for-like basis — the same SKU at this year's net price versus last year's. Its components:

  • List price increases/decreases taken during the year (weighted by the months each applied).
  • Trade schemes, discounts and consumer promotions — under Ind AS 115 these reduce revenue, so an increase in schemes is a negative price effect even if list price is unchanged.
  • GST rate changes — revenue is net of GST, so a GST cut passed on to consumers reduces gross price but not net realisation; a cut not passed on increases net realisation.

Timing weighting is essential. A 6% increase effective 1 October contributes roughly 3% to the current year's average realisation and the remaining ~3% to next year (the "carry-over" or "annualisation" effect):

\[ g_{P,t} \approx \text{Hike} \times \frac{\text{Months effective in year } t}{12} \]

Mix shift

Mix is the change in average realisation caused by selling a different blend of products at unchanged prices — for example, a larger share of premium waterproofing within construction chemicals, or Zudio growing faster than Westside within Trent. Mix is computed as a residual or directly:

\[ \text{Mix effect} = \sum_i \left(s_{i,t} - s_{i,t-1}\right) \times P_{i,t-1} \]

where \(s_{i,t}\) is product \(i\)'s share of total volume. Positive mix means volume is shifting to higher-realisation products. Note that mix can be negative even in a healthy business: Trent's mix shifts towards the lower-price Zudio format, so blended revenue per sq ft can fall while every format is growing.

Model implementation

Lay out one block per product or category on the Revenue sheet, each with three driver rows linked from Assumptions (volume growth, price change, optional months-effective) and calculated rows below:

Volume_t      = Volume_{t-1} * (1 + Vol_Growth_t)
Realisation_t = Realisation_{t-1} * (1 + Price_Change_t)
Revenue_t     = Volume_t * Realisation_t
Check_t       = Revenue_t - SUM(Segment_Revenues_t)   ' must be 0

In Excel with the constant column in E and timeline from column H, a typical row is =G15*(1+Inputs!H$22) copied across, with historical columns holding blue hardcoded actuals and a timeline flag switching between them (see w13 t2 c06). Mix is not a separate input when you model products separately — it emerges from differential volume growth. If you model only a total, you need an explicit mix row, which is weaker and harder to defend.

Add a decomposition output block (volume %, price %, mix %, value %) for each forecast year, and compare it with the historical decomposition. A forecast in which price contributes 8% a year in a 4% CPI economy without a commodity story is an immediate red flag.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • No disclosed volumes. Many Indian companies disclose only volume growth in concalls, not absolute volumes. Build an index (FY21 = 100) and forecast the index; realisation is then revenue ÷ index, which is usable for growth decomposition even without true units.
  • Grammage/pack-size changes. FMCG companies cut pack sizes instead of raising prices (hidden price increase). Volume measured in packs overstates real volume; measure in kg/litres.
  • Deflation pass-through. When commodity prices fall (VAM, crude derivatives), companies pass on price cuts; value growth can be below volume growth. Do not assume price growth is always positive.
  • Export revenue. Realisation in rupees moves with the exchange rate; split price into local-currency price and FX translation for international segments.
  • Channel filling. A launch year often includes pipeline stocking at distributors; UVG for year 2 can look weak against an inflated base.
  • Mix definitions. Decide whether mix is within a segment (premium vs standard adhesives) or across segments (Consumer vs B2B); mixing both levels double-counts.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue = Σ volume × net realisation; growth components compound multiplicatively.
  • UVG is organic volume growth excluding acquisitions, launches and channel stocking; it is the core demand driver.
  • Price realisation must be time-weighted for mid-year hikes and is net of schemes and GST.
  • Mix is the realisation change from selling a different blend at constant prices; it can be negative.
  • Model products separately so mix emerges from the build rather than as a plug.
  • Always output a historical and forecast decomposition and sanity-check price against CPI and commodity trends.

Practice Questions

Q1. Three-way bridge for a paint company [L2]

A decorative paint company sold 1,20,000 KL in FY25 at an average realisation of ₹2.10 lakh per KL across economy (50,000 KL at ₹1.40 lakh), mid (45,000 KL at ₹2.20 lakh) and luxury (25,000 KL at ₹3.32 lakh) ranges. For FY26 it expects economy volume +4%, mid +9%, luxury +16%, and a 3% list-price increase effective 1 July 2025 across all ranges. Build the FY26 revenue and a volume–mix–price bridge in ₹ crore, stating the order of steps you use.

💡 Hint: Time-weight the price increase for nine months and compute mix at FY25 prices.

Q2. Carry-over price effect [L1]

A company took a 5% price increase on 1 January 2025 and a further 2% on 1 October 2025. With no other price change, estimate the like-for-like price growth for FY25 and FY26 (years ending 31 March).

Q3. Value growth with negative mix [L2]

Trent's blended revenue per sq ft fell in a year when Westside and Zudio each reported positive like-for-like growth. Explain the arithmetic of how this happens, and describe how you would present the decomposition to an investment committee so the fall is not misread as weakness.

Q4. UVG cleanup from concall data [L3]

A construction-chemicals company reports value growth of 18% in FY25. The concall states: 3% from an acquisition completed on 1 October 2024 (annual revenue run-rate ₹240 crore on a base of ₹4,000 crore), price increases contributing 2%, and channel stocking of ₹60 crore in March 2025 ahead of an April hike. Derive the underlying volume growth, set out your assumptions, and explain how you would treat the channel stocking in FY26.

Q5. Interview — why not just grow revenue at 12%? [L1]

An interviewer asks why you build revenue from volume and price when consensus simply has 12% growth. Give a structured answer covering cost linkage, scenario flexing and variance analysis.
