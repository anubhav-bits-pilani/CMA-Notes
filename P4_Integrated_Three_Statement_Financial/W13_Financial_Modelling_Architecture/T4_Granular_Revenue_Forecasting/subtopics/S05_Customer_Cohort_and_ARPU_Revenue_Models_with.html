Subtopic note

Customer Cohort and ARPU Revenue Models with Retention Curves

Scope: Customer cohort/ARPU: active customers × ARPU with retention/churn curves and new cohort additions

Why it matters

For subscription and platform businesses — telecom (Bharti Airtel, Jio's ARPU disclosures), broking (Zerodha, Angel One's active clients), insurance (persistency), SaaS (Freshworks, Zoho-style models), D2C brands with repeat purchase, fintech lending and wealth platforms — revenue is a function of how many customers are active and how much each spends. Neither stores nor capacity explains revenue; customer acquisition and retention do. A cohort build separates the two growth engines: new customers acquired this year and the decay (or expansion) of earlier cohorts. It exposes the economics investors care about — CAC payback, lifetime value (LTV), churn — and prevents the most common forecasting error in such businesses: assuming customers acquired today remain forever at today's spend.

Even for traditional companies the approach is useful: a B2B chemicals business can model dealer or key-account cohorts; Pidilite's Bazaar business tracks dealer counts and influencer (carpenter, contractor) programmes.

Core notes

Simple active-customer model

The top-level identity:

\[ \text{Revenue}_t = \overline{\text{Active customers}}_t \times \text{ARPU}_t \]

where \(\overline{\text{Active customers}}_t\) is the average of opening and closing active base (the same averaging logic as store counts) and ARPU is average revenue per user per year (or per month × 12). The active-base roll-forward:

\[ \text{Closing active}_t = \text{Opening active}_t \times (1 - \text{Churn}_t) + \text{Gross adds}_t \]

Churn rate is customers lost ÷ opening customers. Monthly churn compounds: annual retention = \((1 - \text{monthly churn})^{12}\); 2% monthly churn is about 21.5% annual churn, not 24%.

Cohort (vintage) model

A single churn rate hides the fact that churn is highest early in a customer's life and falls for survivors. A cohort build tracks each acquisition year separately:

\[ \text{Revenue}_t = \sum_{c \le t} N_c \times r_{t-c} \times \text{ARPU}_{t-c,t} \]

  • \(N_c\) — customers acquired in cohort year \(c\) (gross adds).
  • \(r_{a}\) — cumulative retention at age \(a\) (share of the cohort still active).
  • \(\text{ARPU}_{a,t}\) — ARPU of a customer aged \(a\) in year \(t\); often rises with age (upsell, price increases, more products) and with general price levels.

A typical retention curve (cumulative): 100% at acquisition, 60% after year 1, 48% after year 2, 42% after year 3, then flattening (survivors are loyal). Note the decaying incremental churn — 40%, 20%, 12.5% of the remaining base.

Worked illustration — broking platform

Cohorts (lakh customers), cumulative retention 100%/60%/48%/42% at age 0/1/2/3, ARPU ₹2,000 at age 0 (half-year of activity effectively captured by a 0.5 weight), ₹2,400 at age 1, ₹2,600 at age 2+:

Cohort Gross adds (lakh) FY26E active (lakh) FY26E ARPU (₹) FY26E revenue (₹ crore)
FY24 30 30 × 48% = 14.4 2,600 374.4
FY25 40 40 × 60% = 24.0 2,400 576.0
FY26 50 50 × 100% × 0.5 = 25.0 2,000 500.0
Total 63.4 1,450.4

Revenue (₹ crore) = lakh customers × ₹ ARPU ÷ 100 (because 1 crore = 100 lakh). The FY26 cohort contributes a third of revenue; if gross adds fall in a market downturn (as broking saw after F&O regulation tightened in FY25), the fall feeds through immediately while older cohorts decay.

Excel layout

As with store vintages, use a triangle with cohorts down and years across. Retention and ARPU-by-age are input rows; one formula fills the grid:

=IF(Year<Cohort, 0,
    Gross_Adds_Cohort * INDEX(Retention_Curve, MIN(Year-Cohort+1, Max_Age+1))
    * IF(Year=Cohort, New_Cohort_Weight, 1))

A second triangle multiplies active customers by ARPU-by-age (with an inflation index for price changes). Add checks: retention curve non-increasing; active customers ≤ cumulative gross adds; total revenue = Σ cohort revenue.

Unit economics outputs

Cohort models naturally produce:

  • LTV = Σ (retention × ARPU × contribution margin) over customer life, discounted.
  • CAC = acquisition spend (marketing, referral, onboarding) ÷ gross adds.
  • LTV ÷ CAC (healthy > 3x) and CAC payback (months of contribution to recover CAC).

These connect revenue to the A&P/marketing cost line in Topic 5: gross adds should be a function of marketing spend and CAC, not a free input, otherwise the Bull case adds customers without paying for them.

Ind AS 115 points

ARPU must be on the Ind AS 115 revenue basis: net of GST, discounts, cashbacks and incentives paid to customers (consideration payable to a customer reduces revenue unless for a distinct good/service). Subscription revenue is recognised over time; upfront activation fees that are not a distinct performance obligation are deferred over the expected customer life. Platform businesses must also apply principal-vs-agent analysis: a marketplace recognising commission (net) will show very different ARPU from one recognising GMV (gross).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Definition of "active". Brokers define active as traded in the last 12 months (NSE active client); telecoms use 30-day activity. Inconsistent definitions across peers distort ARPU comparisons.
  • Reactivation. Dormant customers returning inflate later-year retention; either model reactivation as a separate flow or accept retention curves that tick up.
  • Negative churn. In B2B SaaS, expansion revenue from surviving customers can exceed revenue lost to churn (net revenue retention > 100%) — model ARPU-by-age rising, not just retention.
  • Regulatory shocks. SEBI rules on F&O, TRAI tariff rulings and RBI digital-lending norms change ARPU and acquisition economics abruptly; treat them as scenario drivers.
  • Price increases. Telecom tariff hikes lift ARPU across all cohorts at once — apply them through a year-based index, not the age curve.
  • Data limits. Listed companies rarely disclose cohort retention; use management's churn commentary and the roll-forward of reported active customers to calibrate.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue = average active customers × ARPU; roll the active base with churn and gross adds.
  • Cohort builds capture front-loaded churn and ARPU expansion by customer age.
  • One age-indexed formula fills the cohort triangle; inputs are retention and ARPU curves.
  • Convert monthly churn to annual retention by compounding.
  • Link gross adds to marketing spend via CAC so growth is paid for.
  • ARPU must follow Ind AS 115 — net of incentives, deferral of non-distinct fees, gross vs net.

Practice Questions

Q1. Active-base roll-forward [L1]

A D2C brand has 18 lakh active customers at 1 April 2025, annual churn of 35% and gross adds of 9 lakh in FY26. ARPU is ₹1,450. Compute closing active customers, average active customers and FY26 revenue in ₹ crore.

Q2. Monthly to annual churn [L1]

A SaaS company reports 1.8% monthly logo churn. Compute annual retention and annual churn, and explain why multiplying by 12 is wrong.

Q3. Three-cohort build [L2]

A fintech app acquires 20, 28 and 35 lakh customers in FY25, FY26 and FY27. Cumulative retention is 100% (age 0, weight 0.5), 55% (age 1) and 45% (age 2). ARPU is ₹600 at age 0, ₹900 at age 1 and ₹1,050 at age 2, with a 5% annual price uplift applied to all ages from FY27. Build FY25–FY27 revenue in ₹ crore by cohort.

💡 Hint: Apply the price uplift through a year index multiplied on top of the age-based ARPU.

Q4. CAC-linked gross adds [L3]

Design an assumption and calculation block in which gross adds are driven by marketing spend and CAC, CAC rises as the addressable market saturates, and LTV ÷ CAC is reported each year. Explain how the block would behave in a Bull case that raises marketing spend by 30%.

Q5. Gross vs net ARPU [L2]

A travel platform sells ₹8,000 crore of bookings (GMV) and earns ₹960 crore of commissions. Explain how principal-vs-agent analysis under Ind AS 115 determines reported revenue and ARPU, and what it means for peer comparison.
