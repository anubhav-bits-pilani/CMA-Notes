Roadmap topic: Granular Revenue Forecasting: Volume × Price models, Store/Capacity rollout models, or Customer Cohort/ARPU drivers rather than flat % growth assumptions

Overview

Revenue is the first line of every forecast and the line from which almost everything else is derived — variable costs, working capital, capex, tax and ultimately free cash flow and valuation. A model whose revenue is "prior year × (1 + 12%)" can be arithmetically perfect and still useless: it cannot tell you what has to be true for 12% to happen, it cannot be stress-tested against a slowdown in a specific driver, and it gives costs, capex and working capital nothing physical to scale with. Granular revenue forecasting replaces the growth rate with the operating drivers that management actually runs the business on — volumes and prices, stores and space, plants and utilisation, customers and spend per customer.

For the target roles this is the core skill. Equity research and buy-side analysts are judged on whether their driver assumptions are better than consensus; transaction-services and valuation teams in Big 4 firms test management's business-plan drivers in due diligence; FP&A teams build annual budgets and rolling forecasts from exactly these drivers and explain variances in price, volume and mix. The Indian context adds specifics: revenue is net of GST and dealer schemes under Ind AS 115; UVG is the language of consumer company concalls; store and area disclosures are the backbone of retail models; and capacity in MTPA or KTPA governs cement, chemicals and metals.

The learning sequence runs from general to specific. Start with the volume × price × mix identity (s01, c01) — every other model is a variant of it. Then learn three archetypes: the store rollout (s02, c02, c03) and its vintage refinement (s03); the capacity-led build (s04, c04); and the customer cohort/ARPU model (s05, c05). Apply them to the Week 13 exercise companies through the segment builds for Trent and Pidilite (s06). Finally, test the bottom-up answer against the outside-in view of market size and share (s07), and make sure every realisation driver is on the Ind AS 115 basis (c06).

Related concepts to study

Phase 1: Ind AS 115 variable consideration and principal vs agent

→ Phase 1 (W1–4): Ind AS 115 variable consideration, principal vs agent (gross vs net revenue)

Every realisation driver in a revenue build is only as good as its revenue definition. From Phase 1, revisit how dealer schemes, volume rebates, price protection and rights of return are estimated as variable consideration and constrained so that a significant reversal is not highly probable, and how consideration payable to customers reduces revenue. Principal-vs-agent analysis decides whether a marketplace, concessionaire or consignment arrangement reports gross sales or commission — which changes ARPU, revenue per sq ft and gross margin by multiples, not percentages. Before calibrating historical realisations, check the revenue note for changes in these judgements; a switch from gross to net presentation looks like a revenue collapse in a growth-rate model.

Phase 2: Peer benchmarking of revenue drivers

→ Phase 2 (W5–8): peer benchmarking of revenue/sq ft, volume growth, market share

Phase 2's ratio and benchmarking work supplies the reasonableness tests for driver assumptions. Revenue per sq ft for Trent's formats can be compared with V-Mart, ABFRL's Pantaloons and DMart (allowing for category mix); Pidilite's UVG can be compared with paints and construction-chemicals peers; cement volume growth with the industry's. Market-share trends from industry reports and peer revenue give the top-down check in s07. Use common-size and growth-decomposition tables built in Phase 2 to place your forecast drivers inside the historical peer distribution: a forecast that puts the company in the top decile on every driver simultaneously needs a stated competitive reason.

Week 14: Store additions and capacity drive capex and NWC

→ Week 14: store additions/capacity drive capex and NWC schedules

The physical drivers chosen here become the inputs to next week's schedules. Store additions × fit-out capex per sq ft drive capex; store counts drive lease additions and ROU assets under Ind AS 116; capacity projects in the s04 project table drive CWIP, capitalisation and depreciation. Inventory is often forecast per store or as days of COGS, receivables as days of revenue. If this week's revenue is a growth rate, Week 14 is forced to forecast capex as % of sales — losing the link between growth and investment that drives free cash flow and returns on capital. Build the revenue rows with that downstream use in mind: keep store adds, area and capacity additions on explicit rows.

Phase 5: Driver-based budgets and rolling forecasts

→ Phase 5 (W17–20): driver-based budgets and rolling forecasts reuse these drivers

FP&A planning in Phase 5 reuses the same driver trees at monthly or quarterly granularity. A driver-based budget sets targets for stores, LFL, UVG and price, not just revenue; a rolling forecast updates the drivers each quarter as actuals land. Variance analysis decomposes budget vs actual into price, volume and mix — exactly the decomposition of s01 — and into new-store vs LFL contributions for retail. Designing the revenue build so its drivers match management's KPIs means the same logic serves both the valuation model and the business's planning cycle.

Common pitfalls

Flat % growth disguised as a driver

Flat % growth disguised as a driver (single row: prior year × (1 + g))

The mistake: a "driver" sheet with rows labelled "Zudio revenue growth 30%" or "C&B growth 12%", each simply multiplying the prior year — the forecast is still a growth rate, only with more rows. It happens because analysts anchor on consensus growth and back into it, or because the driver data seem hard to find. The correct treatment is to decompose growth into the physical and price drivers (stores, area, density; volume and realisation; capacity and utilisation; customers and ARPU), each with a source in the assumption log. Detect it in review by asking for each revenue row: what physical quantity is this, and where does it come from? If every revenue row contains a growth rate and nothing feeds capex or cost schedules, the model has this pitfall.

Full-year revenue from stores opened at year-end

Full-year revenue from stores opened at year-end

The mistake: multiplying closing store count (or closing area, closing capacity, closing customers) by an annual productivity figure. It is common because closing counts are what annual reports headline. Stores opened in March generate almost no revenue in that year, so a fast-growing chain's revenue is overstated by about half the year's additions. The correct treatment is the average count, or a timing-weighted count when openings are back-ended (c02). Detect it by recomputing revenue per average store and per closing store for history: if the model's forecast revenue per store jumps compared with history, check which count is used, and look for references to closing rows in revenue formulas.

Double-counting price inside volume growth

Double-counting price inflation inside volume growth (value vs volume confusion)

The mistake: treating management's value growth or market value growth as volume growth, then adding a separate price increase on top — or using an industry growth figure in rupees as a volume driver. It arises because Indian disclosures mix "growth" in volume and value terms, and industry reports quote market growth in nominal rupees. The correct treatment: identify whether each source figure is volume or value, convert value figures to volume by removing price (c01), and apply price only once. Detect it by computing the implied revenue decomposition in the model and comparing price/mix with CPI and input-cost trends; revenue growth well above nominal GDP plus share gain with no stated reason is a symptom.

Revenue growth beyond capacity without matching capex

Revenue growth beyond capacity without matching capex

The mistake: volume growing for five years while installed capacity, store count or headcount capacity is flat, so implied utilisation exceeds 100% (or revenue per store doubles). It happens when revenue and capex are built by different people or capex is forecast as % of sales. The correct treatment: cap volume at practical capacity (c04), schedule capacity projects with capex and commissioning dates when demand exceeds it, and reference the same project table from revenue and the PPE schedule. Detect it with a utilisation output row and a warning check on the Checks sheet; also compare capex/incremental revenue with history.

Practice Questions

Q1. Trent five-year format build [L3]

Using the following illustrative data (not actuals), build FY26E–FY30E revenue for a two-format apparel retailer and evaluate the result top-down.

Item Format A (premium) Format B (value)
Stores at 31 March 2025 240 760
Area at 31 March 2025 (mn sq ft) 5.4 6.1
Gross additions per year 30 200 (FY26), declining by 20 a year thereafter
Closures per year 6 15
Average new/closed store area (sq ft) 22,000 / 18,000 8,500 / 7,000
FY25 mature revenue per sq ft (₹) 12,500 10,000
LFL per year 7% 6%
Maturity curve (age 0/1/2+) 60% / 85% / 100% 70% / 90% / 100%

Openings are evenly spread (age-0 time weight 0.5). The organised apparel market is ₹2,00,000 crore at net retail prices in FY25 growing at 14% a year. (a) Build store and area roll-forwards; (b) build revenue by vintage for each format; (c) compute blended revenue per sq ft and the revenue mix; (d) compute implied market share and annual share gain in bps, and comment on plausibility.

💡 Hint: Build the roll-forward first, then a vintage triangle per format driven by an age lookup.

Q2. Pidilite-style segment build with input-cost deflation [L2]

An adhesives company has FY25 revenue of ₹10,500 crore: Consumer & Bazaar ₹8,600 crore, B2B ₹2,100 crore, inter-segment eliminations ₹200 crore. International subsidiaries (included in C&B) contribute ₹900 crore, earned in currencies whose average rate is expected to depreciate 6% against the rupee in FY26. For FY26: domestic C&B UVG 9% with price/mix −2% (price cuts after a fall in VAM); B2B volume 7% and price/mix +1%; international local-currency growth 14%; eliminations stay at 1.9% of total segment revenue. (a) Build FY26 revenue by segment; (b) show reported value growth vs UVG for domestic C&B; (c) explain how you would present the FX effect; (d) state which items you would record in the assumption log with sources.

Q3. Cement capacity and capex linkage [L3]

A cement company has 40 MTPA of grinding capacity at 31 March 2025, with a 6 MTPA brownfield line due on 1 January 2027 (capex ₹4,200 crore, 30% in FY26 and 70% in FY27) and a 2 MTPA debottlenecking project due 1 October 2025 (capex ₹700 crore, all in FY26). Regional demand allows volume growth of 9% a year from FY25 volume of 31 mn tonnes. Practical maximum utilisation is 88%; new capacity runs at 60% in its first twelve months of operation. Realisation is ₹5,300 per tonne in FY25 rising 2% a year. (a) Build effective capacity, constrained volume, utilisation, shortfall and revenue for FY26–FY29; (b) quantify revenue lost if the brownfield line slips nine months; (c) describe how the capex rows and revenue rows should share one project table.

Q4. Cohort model review [L2]

A broking platform model shows active clients rising from 80 lakh to 2.4 crore over five years with revenue growing at a 28% CAGR. The model uses closing active clients × a flat ARPU of ₹3,200 and a single 20% churn rate on the opening base; gross adds are an input rising 15% a year with no link to marketing spend. List every structural weakness, then specify a corrected design (cohort triangle, retention and ARPU-by-age curves, averaging, CAC link, Ind AS 115 basis for incentives and cashbacks), and identify which regulatory change would be your first Bear-case driver.

Q5. Interview case — "Defend your revenue line" [L3]

You present a five-year forecast for a listed consumer company with 16% revenue CAGR. The interviewer asks you to defend it in three minutes. Prepare a structured answer covering: the driver decomposition (volume, price, mix, new stores or capacity), how each driver is sourced, the top-down share and nominal GDP check, the capacity or capex consistency check, and the two drivers whose downside you would show in a Bear case.
