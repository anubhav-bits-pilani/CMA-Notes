Key concept

Cash Conversion Cycle

Concept: Cash conversion cycle = DSO + DIO − DPO

Statement

\[ \text{CCC} = \text{DSO} + \text{DIO} - \text{DPO} \]

where \( \text{DSO} = \text{AR} \div \text{Revenue} \times 365 \) (days to collect from customers), \( \text{DIO} = \text{Inventory} \div \text{COGS} \times 365 \) (days goods sit in stock), and \( \text{DPO} = \text{AP} \div \text{COGS (or purchases)} \times 365 \) (days the company takes to pay suppliers). CCC is the number of days between paying for inputs and collecting cash from the sale of the output.

Explanation

Think of a single rupee of inventory: it is bought (supplier credit starts), held for DIO days, sold on credit, and collected DSO days later. Supplier credit funds the first DPO days; the company funds the rest. A lower CCC means less capital tied up per rupee of sales; a negative CCC (grocery, quick commerce, some FMCG and QSR chains) means suppliers fund the operating cycle and growth releases cash.

The operating cycle (DSO + DIO) is the same concept before supplier credit; it is used in Ind AS 1.68 to define "current" for operating items — assets realised within the normal operating cycle are current even if that exceeds 12 months (relevant for real estate, shipbuilding, liquor ageing).

Conditions and limitations:

  • Mixed bases: DSO is on revenue, DIO/DPO on COGS. The days are not strictly additive in rupee terms; CCC is a heuristic. A rupee-consistent alternative is NWC ÷ (Revenue ÷ 365).
  • Excludes contract balances, other operating items and customer advances — a capital-goods company with large advances may show high CCC but low NWC.
  • Year-end distortion: 31 March balances can be seasonally unrepresentative (t1 s06).
  • Purchases vs COGS for DPO must match how DPO is forecast.

In modelling, CCC is not a driver; it is a diagnostic row under the working-capital schedule. Use it to test forecast drift versus history and peers.

Illustration

Company type DSO DIO DPO CCC
Vastra Retail (fashion) 15 80 100 −5
Specialty chemicals 75 95 70 100
Auto ancillary 60 45 55 50
Quick-commerce grocery 2 12 40 −26

If the specialty chemicals company has COGS of ₹3,650 crore (₹10 crore per day) and revenue of ₹5,110 crore (₹14 crore per day), a 10-day cut in DIO frees ≈ ₹100 crore, while a 10-day cut in DSO frees ≈ ₹140 crore — the same "10 days" of CCC are worth different rupees, illustrating the mixed-base caveat.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute and interpret CCC [L1]

From FY25 data (₹ crore): revenue 7,300; COGS 5,110; receivables 1,000; inventory 840; payables 700. Compute DSO, DIO, DPO and CCC. Then compute NWC ÷ (Revenue ÷ 365) for the three lines and explain why it differs from CCC.

Q2. Forecast drift test [L2]

Your model's CCC moves from 42 days (FY25A) to 18 days (FY30E) for an auto-ancillary because you assumed DIO falls 2 days a year and DPO rises 3 days a year, with DSO flat. Assess whether this is defensible given MSMED payment limits and typical OEM terms, and propose how to present the assumption to an investment committee.

💡 Hint: Identify which component is legally or commercially constrained before defending the trend.

Q3. Interview: negative CCC [L2]

Explain why a negative CCC is attractive for a growing retailer but can become a liquidity risk if sales decline, using a numerical example where revenue drops 10% and DPO is unchanged.
