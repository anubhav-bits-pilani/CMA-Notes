Key concept

Days-Based Formulas for Receivables, Inventory and Payables

Concept: Receivables = Revenue × DSO ÷ 365; Inventory = COGS × DIO ÷ 365; Payables = COGS × DPO ÷ 365

Statement

\[ \text{AR}_t = \text{Revenue}_t \times \frac{\text{DSO}_t}{365} \qquad \text{Inv}_t = \text{COGS}_t \times \frac{\text{DIO}_t}{365} \qquad \text{AP}_t = \text{COGS}_t \times \frac{\text{DPO}_t}{365} \]

where \( \text{AR}_t \), \( \text{Inv}_t \), \( \text{AP}_t \) are closing trade receivables, inventory and trade payables in year \( t \); \( \text{Revenue}_t \) is revenue from operations; \( \text{COGS}_t \) is cost of goods sold (materials consumed + purchases of stock-in-trade + change in inventories, per the model's definition); and \( \text{DSO}_t \), \( \text{DIO}_t \), \( \text{DPO}_t \) are the days assumptions. Historical days are the same formulas inverted, e.g. \( \text{DSO} = \text{AR} \div \text{Revenue} \times 365 \).

Explanation

Each balance is expressed as "days of its causal flow". Receivables arise from sales, so they scale with revenue. Inventory is carried at cost, so it scales with COGS. Payables arise from purchases of the goods that become COGS, so COGS (or, more precisely, purchases) is the base. Matching numerator and denominator on the same price basis is the whole point: if margin expands, revenue rises relative to COGS, and revenue-based inventory or payables days would change even though physical stock and supplier terms have not.

Why days rather than % of revenue? Days are interpretable against contractual terms ("45-day credit to distributors"), comparable across companies of different size, and stable when growth is uneven. Mathematically a days assumption is just a percentage (\( \text{DSO} \div 365 \)), so nothing is lost.

Conditions and refinements:

  • Purchases vs COGS for payables — purchases is the correct base when inventory moves materially (t1 s03). COGS is the pragmatic default.
  • Credit vs total revenue — receivables strictly arise from credit sales; using total revenue is acceptable when the cash/credit mix is stable.
  • 365 vs 360 — use 365 consistently; quarterly models use 91 or 90 days per quarter, monthly models actual days.
  • Closing vs average balances — use the same basis for history and forecast (c06).
  • Days are inputs — blue cells on the assumptions tab, scenario-switchable, not hard-coded inside the formula.

Illustration

Vastra Retail FY26E: revenue ₹14,400 crore, COGS ₹8,160 crore; assumptions DSO 15, DIO 77, DPO 99.8.

Line Calculation ₹ crore
Receivables \( 14{,}400 \times 15 \div 365 \) 591.8
Inventory \( 8{,}160 \times 77 \div 365 \) 1,721.4
Payables \( 8{,}160 \times 99.8 \div 365 \) 2,231.1

If gross margin rises from 43.3% to 45% in FY27 and you had (wrongly) set DIO on revenue, inventory would be overstated by the ratio of revenue to COGS for the same physical stock.

Excel:

=Revenue_t*DSO_t/365
=COGS_t*DIO_t/365
=COGS_t*DPO_t/365

Practice Questions

Q1. Apply the days formulas [L1]

An NSE-listed electricals company forecasts FY27 revenue ₹9,125 crore and COGS ₹6,570 crore. Assumptions: DSO 52, DIO 68, DPO 58. Compute the three closing balances and the operating NWC from these lines alone.

Q2. Value of one day [L2]

Using the Q1 data, compute the cash released by a 1-day reduction in DSO, a 1-day reduction in DIO and a 1-day increase in DPO. Rank the three levers for a CFO targeting ₹100 crore of working-capital release, and comment on which is most constrained in practice for purchases from micro and small suppliers.

💡 Hint: One day of a balance equals its base divided by 365.

Q3. Wrong base diagnosis [L2]

A model sets inventory = revenue × 50 ÷ 365. Revenue is ₹2,000 crore and gross margin moves from 30% to 40% over three years with constant physical inventory of 70 days of COGS. Compute the forecast error in year 3 and explain the direction of the bias in CFO.
