Key concept

Consistent Day-Count and Balance Basis for Working Capital Days

Concept: Same day-count and balance basis (year-end vs average) for historical and forecast days

Statement

For any days metric \( D \) on balance \( B \) and flow \( F \):

\[ D^{\text{closing}}_t = \frac{B_t}{F_t} \times N \qquad D^{\text{average}}_t = \frac{(B_t + B_{t-1}) \div 2}{F_t} \times N \]

where \( N \) is the day-count (365 for annual, 90/91 per quarter, actual days per month). The rule: the same \( N \) and the same balance basis must be used to compute historical days and to convert forecast days back into balances. Mixing bases introduces a systematic error equal to the difference between the two definitions.

Explanation

Day-count. If history is computed on 365 and the forecast on 360, every balance is understated by \( 5 \div 365 \approx 1.4\% \). Small, but it creates an artificial ΔNWC in the first forecast year — a cash inflow that does not exist. In quarterly models, using 365 ÷ 4 for every quarter misstates the shorter Q4 (90 days in a non-leap year) and longer Q2/Q3; use actual days or consistent 91.25.

Balance basis. Closing-balance days produce the closing balance directly: \( B_t = F_t \times D_t \div N \). Average-balance days require solving for the closing balance:

\[ B_t = 2 \times \frac{F_t \times D_t}{N} - B_{t-1} \]

This recursion makes any noise in the opening balance flip sign each year — if FY25 inventory was unusually high, FY26 forecast closing inventory is pushed unusually low, FY27 high again, producing a saw-tooth ΔNWC. That is why most valuation and sell-side models use closing balances, and analysts who prefer average-based metrics for ratio analysis still forecast on closing.

If you compute historical averages but forecast on closing (or vice versa), growth distorts the comparison: in a growing company average balance < closing balance, so average-basis days are lower. Applying low average days to forecast closing balances understates NWC.

Documentation. State the basis in the assumptions tab header ("Days on closing balance, 365-day year") and in the model's methodology note. In review, recompute one historical days figure manually to confirm.

Illustration

Revenue ₹3,650 crore (FY25) growing 20% to ₹4,380 crore (FY26). Receivables FY24 ₹400 crore, FY25 ₹500 crore.

Basis FY25 DSO FY26 receivables if DSO held
Closing, 365 \( 500 \div 3{,}650 \times 365 = 50.0 \) \( 4{,}380 \times 50 \div 365 = 600 \)
Average, 365 \( 450 \div 3{,}650 \times 365 = 45.0 \) \( 2 \times 4{,}380 \times 45 \div 365 - 500 = 580 \)
Mixed: average DSO applied as closing 45.0 \( 4{,}380 \times 45 \div 365 = 540 \) — understated by ₹60 crore 
Closing basis:  =Revenue_t*DSO_t/Days_in_Year
Average basis:  =2*Revenue_t*DSO_t/Days_in_Year-AR_t_1

Keep Days_in_Year as a single named input so it cannot drift between rows.

Connections

Applies to every days driver in c01, the CCC in c02 and the GST adjustment in c05. The same consistency principle governs depreciation conventions in t2 and interest on opening vs average balances in t3/t4 (where average-balance interest creates circularity). Links to the cross-cutting rule that the first forecast opening balance is linked, not retyped.

Practice Questions

Q1. Quantify a day-count mismatch [L1]

Historical DIO of 72 days is computed on a 365-day year against COGS of ₹5,475 crore. The forecast template converts days to balances using 360. For FY26 COGS of ₹6,000 crore, compute the inventory under both conventions and the spurious cash flow created.

Q2. Saw-tooth diagnosis [L2]

A model uses average-balance DIO of 60 days for all forecast years. FY25 closing inventory was ₹900 crore (inflated by a one-off pre-buy); COGS is ₹4,380 crore in FY26, ₹4,818 crore in FY27 and ₹5,300 crore in FY28. Compute forecast closing inventory for FY26–FY28 and ΔNWC from inventory, explain the pattern, and recommend a fix.

💡 Hint: Use closing = 2 × average − opening, year by year.

Q3. Quarterly model day-count [L2]

You are converting an annual DSO of 45 days into a quarterly model for FY27. Explain how you would set the day-count for each quarter, and what happens to the Q4 balance if you use 365 ÷ 4 for every quarter while Q4 revenue is 35% of annual revenue.
