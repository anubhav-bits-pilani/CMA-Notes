Key concept

GST in Receivables vs GST-Exclusive Revenue

Concept: Receivables include GST while revenue excludes it; historical DSO embeds this, so forecast on the same basis

Statement

Trade receivables are invoiced amounts inclusive of GST (the customer owes the full invoice), while revenue from operations under Ind AS 115.47 excludes amounts collected on behalf of third parties, including GST. Hence:

\[ \text{DSO}_{\text{reported}} = \frac{\text{AR}_{\text{incl. GST}}}{\text{Revenue}_{\text{excl. GST}}} \times 365 = \text{DSO}_{\text{true}} \times (1 + g) \]

where \( g \) is the effective GST rate on credit sales and \( \text{DSO}_{\text{true}} \) is the collection period measured on a like-for-like (GST-inclusive) basis. Provided the forecast uses the same reported DSO against GST-exclusive revenue, the receivable forecast is correct.

Explanation

The mismatch is real but harmless if handled consistently. Historical DSO computed from the annual report already contains the \( (1+g) \) factor; applying that DSO to forecast GST-exclusive revenue reproduces GST-inclusive receivables. Problems arise only when the basis is mixed:

  • Mixing contractual terms with reported DSO. If management says "we give 45 days' credit" and you set DSO = 45 against GST-exclusive revenue, you understate receivables by the GST factor (at 18% GST, 45 true days appear as 53 reported days).
  • GST rate changes. A rate change (e.g., GST rationalisation moving a product from 12% to 5% or 18% to 28%) changes reported DSO with no change in collection behaviour. Adjust: \( \text{DSO}_{\text{new}} = \text{DSO}_{\text{true}} \times (1 + g_{\text{new}}) \).
  • Mix shifts between exempt/export sales (zero-rated, no GST in the receivable) and domestic taxable sales change the blended factor.
  • Peer comparison. A company with exports or exempt products will look like it collects faster than a domestic peer even with identical terms.

The same logic applies to payables: trade payables include input GST, while COGS/purchases (for a GST-registered business claiming input credit) exclude it. Historical DPO embeds the factor too; contractual supplier terms must be grossed up before use. The MSMED 45-day limit (t1 s03) is a true-days test, so convert reported DPO to true days before testing it.

Inventory is carried net of recoverable GST (Ind AS 2.11: purchase cost excludes taxes subsequently recoverable), so DIO has no GST distortion.

Illustration

An FMCG distributor sells at 18% GST with 30-day credit terms; FY26E revenue (excl. GST) ₹3,650 crore, i.e., ₹10 crore per day.

Approach Receivables (₹ cr)
Correct: \( 10 \times 30 \times 1.18 \) 354
Reported historical DSO 35.4 days × ₹10 crore 354
Wrong: contractual 30 days × ₹10 crore 300

If in FY27 the product moves to 5% GST, true DSO stays 30 but reported DSO falls to \( 30 \times 1.05 = 31.5 \) days. Keeping 35.4 days overstates receivables by ₹39 crore.

Reported DSO from terms:  =Terms_Days*(1+GST_Rate*Taxable_Share)
Forecast AR:              =Revenue_t*Reported_DSO_t/365

Practice Questions

Q1. Gross-up contractual terms [L1]

A cable manufacturer offers distributors 60 days' credit; 80% of sales are domestic at 18% GST and 20% are exports (zero-rated). FY26E revenue is ₹2,920 crore. Compute the reported DSO you should use and the forecast receivables.

Q2. GST rate change [L2]

Historical reported DSO is 41.3 days for a company selling entirely at 18% GST. From FY27 its products move to 12% GST with no change in credit terms. Compute the revised reported DSO and the impact on FY27 receivables for revenue of ₹5,475 crore. Explain the direction of the CFO effect in FY27 and whether it is a genuine working-capital improvement.

💡 Hint: Back out true days first using the old rate, then reapply the new rate.

Q3. MSMED test on reported DPO [L2]

A company's reported DPO to micro and small suppliers is 50 days, computed on GST-exclusive purchases; inputs carry 18% GST. Determine whether the company is likely breaching the 45-day limit and explain what further data you would need.
