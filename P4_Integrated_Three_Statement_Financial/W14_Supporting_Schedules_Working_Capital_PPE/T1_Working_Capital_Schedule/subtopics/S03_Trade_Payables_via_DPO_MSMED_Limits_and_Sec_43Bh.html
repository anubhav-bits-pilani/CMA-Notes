Subtopic note

Trade Payables via DPO, MSMED Limits and Sec 43B(h)

Scope: Trade payables via DPO on COGS or purchases; MSMED Act 45-day limit and Sec 43B(h) disallowance

Why it matters

Supplier credit is the cheapest working-capital funding a company has, and in Indian retail and FMCG it is often large enough to make the cash conversion cycle negative. That makes DPO the single most tempting lever for an optimistic forecast — "we'll stretch suppliers by 10 days" releases crores of cash on paper. Since FY 2023-24 the lever is legally constrained for purchases from micro and small enterprises: the MSMED Act caps credit at 45 days and Sec 43B(h) of the Income-tax Act disallows the expense until payment if that cap is breached. A credible payables schedule must therefore pick the right base, segment MSE vs non-MSE suppliers where material, and cap MSE days.

Core notes

DPO on COGS or on purchases

\[ \text{DPO} = \frac{\text{Trade payables}}{\text{Base}} \times 365, \qquad \text{Payables}_t = \text{Base}_t \times \frac{\text{DPO}_t}{365} \]

Purchases is the theoretically correct base: payables arise from purchases, and in a year of inventory build, purchases exceed COGS. For a trader or retailer:

\[ \text{Purchases} = \text{COGS} + \text{Closing inventory} - \text{Opening inventory} \]

For a manufacturer, purchases of materials = material consumed + closing RM − opening RM.

COGS is the pragmatic base used in most sell-side models because it is directly available from the cost build, avoids a circular-looking dependency on the inventory line, and differs from purchases only when inventory moves sharply. Use purchases when inventory growth is large relative to COGS (rapid store roll-out, pre-buying ahead of price hikes); otherwise COGS is acceptable. Never use revenue — it embeds margin, the same error as for inventory.

Schedule III trade payables also include creditors for services and expenses (rent, freight, marketing, power). If these are material, a cleaner approach is DPO on COGS for goods creditors plus "expense payables" as days of cash opex. Capital creditors (for PPE) are not trade payables in a well-built model — they belong to the capex schedule (Week 14 t2).

Illustration: base choice matters during inventory build

Vastra Retail, FY26E: COGS ₹8,160 crore; inventory rises from ₹1,490 crore to ₹1,722 crore, so purchases are ₹8,392 crore. FY25 payables were ₹1,860 crore against FY25 purchases of ₹7,050 crore (DPO on purchases 96.3 days) and COGS of ₹6,800 crore (DPO on COGS 99.8 days).

Method FY26E payables (₹ cr)
DPO on COGS held at 99.8 days \( 8{,}160 \times 99.8 \div 365 = 2{,}231 \)
DPO on purchases held at 96.3 days \( 8{,}392 \times 96.3 \div 365 = 2{,}214 \)

The gap is small here; it widens in years with heavy inventory build or destocking.

MSMED Act, 2006: the 45-day limit

  • Sec 15: a buyer must pay a micro or small enterprise supplier on or before the agreed date, and the agreed period cannot exceed 45 days from the day of acceptance (or deemed acceptance) of goods/services. Without a written agreement the limit is 15 days.
  • Sec 16: delayed payment attracts compound interest with monthly rests at three times the bank rate notified by RBI.
  • Sec 22: the buyer must disclose in its annual accounts the principal and interest unpaid to MSEs, interest paid, and accrued interest.
  • Sec 23: the Sec 16 interest is not deductible for income-tax.

Schedule III (Division II) requires trade payables to be split into "total outstanding dues of micro and small enterprises" and "of creditors other than micro and small enterprises", with ageing buckets. This disclosure is how you size the MSE share in a model.

The Act protects micro and small enterprises only (not medium) and, for delayed-payment purposes, suppliers who are manufacturers or service providers registered on Udyam; retail and wholesale traders registered for priority-sector lending do not get the delayed-payment protection. Verify supplier status — the buyer bears the classification risk.

Sec 43B(h): disallowance until paid

Inserted by the Finance Act 2023, effective from FY 2023-24 (AY 2024-25): any sum payable to a micro or small enterprise beyond the time limit in Sec 15 of the MSMED Act is deductible only in the year of actual payment. Unlike the other clauses of Sec 43B, the proviso allowing payment up to the return due date does not apply to clause (h) — a 31 March payable outstanding beyond 45 days is added back in that year's computation even if paid in April.

Modelling consequences:

  1. DPO constraint: blended DPO cannot be stretched freely. Segment payables:

\[ \text{Payables} = \text{Base} \times \left[ s_{\text{MSE}} \times \frac{\min(\text{DPO}_{\text{MSE}}, 45)}{365} + (1 - s_{\text{MSE}}) \times \frac{\text{DPO}_{\text{other}}}{365} \right] \]

where \( s_{\text{MSE}} \) is the share of purchases from MSEs.

  1. Tax schedule link: any forecast MSE overdue balance is a Sec 43B disallowance in the taxable-income bridge (Week 14 t5) and reverses when paid — a deferred-tax-style timing difference, though most companies simply avoid it by paying on time.

  2. Behavioural shift: after FY24 many large buyers cut MSE credit to ≤ 45 days or switched to non-MSE distributors, so historical DPO before FY24 may overstate sustainable DPO. Check the trend in the MSE ageing table.

Note the Income-tax Act, 2025 (effective 1 April 2026) renumbers sections; confirm the equivalent of Sec 43B(h) when modelling FY27 onwards.

Excel implementation

Purchases:        =COGS_t+Inventory_t-Inventory_t_1
Blended DPO:      =Purchases_t*DPO_t/365
Segmented:        =Purchases_t*(MSE_Share*MIN(DPO_MSE,45)+(1-MSE_Share)*DPO_Other)/365
43B(h) add-back:  =MAX(0,DPO_MSE-45)/365*Purchases_t*MSE_Share   'only if overdue modelled

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Supply-chain finance / reverse factoring. If a bank pays the supplier and the buyer pays the bank later, the obligation may be a borrowing rather than a trade payable. The 2023 amendments to Ind AS 7 and Ind AS 107 (supplier finance arrangements) require disclosure of terms and the carrying amount; treat extended-tenor arrangements as debt (s07).
  • Acceptances / LC-backed creditors (common for imported raw materials) are often shown in trade payables but are bank-funded; analysts frequently reclassify them to debt.
  • Day-count mismatch: 45 days runs from acceptance, not invoice date; small timing gaps can tip a 44-day payable into breach. Model a buffer (e.g., 40 days).
  • Negative working capital retailers (grocery, quick commerce) rely on very high DPO; a forecast DPO above contractual terms is a red flag.
  • Foreign-currency payables revalue at closing rates under Ind AS 21; a rupee depreciation inflates payables without any change in days.

Key takeaways

  • Payables = Base × DPO ÷ 365; use purchases when inventory swings, COGS otherwise; never revenue.
  • Keep capital creditors and expense creditors out of, or separately within, trade payables.
  • MSMED Act Sec 15 caps MSE credit at 45 days; Sec 16 interest is penal and non-deductible under Sec 23.
  • Sec 43B(h) disallows overdue MSE dues until actually paid, with no return-due-date relief.
  • Segment MSE vs non-MSE payables using the Schedule III split and cap MSE DPO at 45 days.
  • Reclassify supplier-finance and acceptance-backed balances as debt when they are in substance borrowings.

Practice Questions

Q1. Purchases-based DPO [L1]

A trading company has FY25 COGS ₹2,400 crore, opening inventory ₹300 crore, closing inventory ₹420 crore and closing trade payables ₹410 crore. Compute FY25 purchases and DPO on both purchases and COGS. If FY26 COGS is ₹2,700 crore and closing inventory ₹470 crore, forecast payables holding DPO on purchases constant.

Q2. Segmented payables with an MSE cap [L2]

An FMCG company buys ₹6,000 crore of goods in FY26E. MSE suppliers account for 25% of purchases. Historical blended DPO is 75 days, made up of an estimated 62 days for MSEs and 79 days for others. Management wants to stretch non-MSE suppliers to 90 days. Compute forecast payables (a) ignoring the MSMED limit, and (b) with MSE days capped at 45. State the cash difference and explain the tax consequence if the company kept MSE days at 62.

💡 Hint: Payables beyond 45 days to MSEs at 31 March feed the Sec 43B(h) add-back.

Q3. Sec 43B(h) computation [L2]

At 31 March 2026 a manufacturer owes ₹18 crore to micro and small suppliers, of which ₹5 crore has been outstanding for more than 45 days from acceptance. The ₹5 crore is paid on 20 April 2026. The written agreements specify 45-day terms. Determine the FY 2025-26 add-back, the FY 2026-27 deduction, and the deferred-tax effect at 25.17%. Comment on any MSMED Sec 16 interest.

Q4. Reverse factoring reclassification [L3]

A consumer-durables company introduced a supplier-finance programme in FY25: a bank pays suppliers at 30 days and the company pays the bank at 120 days. Payables rose from ₹900 crore to ₹1,650 crore and CFO jumped by ₹700 crore. Explain how you would (a) treat the arrangement in the NWC schedule and net-debt calculation, (b) adjust FY26–FY28 DPO assumptions, and (c) use the Ind AS 7 supplier-finance disclosures in your review.

Q5. Interview: why is DPO on revenue wrong? [L1]

Explain to a junior analyst, with a numerical example of a 2-percentage-point gross margin expansion, why DPO computed on revenue gives a misleading forecast of payables.
