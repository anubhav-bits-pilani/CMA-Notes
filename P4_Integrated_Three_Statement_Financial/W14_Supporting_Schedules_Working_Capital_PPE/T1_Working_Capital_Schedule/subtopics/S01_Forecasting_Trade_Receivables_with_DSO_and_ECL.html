Forecasting Trade Receivables with DSO and ECL Netting

Scope: Trade receivables via DSO on revenue; Ind AS 109 ECL allowance netted

Why it matters

Trade receivables are usually the largest operating asset in a B2B model (FMCG distributors, chemicals, capital goods, IT services) and a meaningful one even for retailers with franchise, institutional or marketplace income. In a three-statement model the receivable line does three jobs at once: it sets the closing Balance Sheet asset, its movement flows into cash flow from operations, and the days metric behind it is the lever an FP&A team uses when management asks "what if collections slip by ten days?". Credit analysts and auditors look at the same number from the other side — a rising DSO with flat revenue is one of the oldest revenue-recognition and collectability red flags. Getting the base, the day-count and the ECL treatment right is what separates a schedule that reconciles to the annual report from one that silently drifts.

Core notes

Definition and the DSO driver

Days Sales Outstanding expresses the receivable balance as a number of days of revenue:

\[ \text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Trade receivables (net)}}{\text{Revenue from operations}} \times 365 \]

Inverting the formula gives the forecast:

\[ \text{Receivables}_t = \text{Revenue}_t \times \frac{\text{DSO}_t}{365} \]

Revenue is the correct base because receivables arise from credit sales. Strictly the denominator should be credit revenue, but listed companies do not disclose the cash/credit split, so modellers use total revenue from operations and accept that the historical DSO already embeds the cash-sale mix. As long as the mix is stable, the forecast is internally consistent. Where the mix is expected to change (a retailer adding a wholesale/B2B channel, or a D2C brand moving to marketplace), build channel-wise receivables: cash retail at near-zero days, marketplace at settlement days (typically 7–15), institutional at contract days (45–90).

Building the schedule

A clean receivable block on the working-capital tab looks like this:

Row FY25A FY26E FY27E FY28E
Revenue from operations (₹ cr, linked from revenue tab) 12,000 14,400 17,000 19,700
Gross trade receivables 520 — — —
Less: ECL allowance (27) — — —
Net trade receivables 493 592 699 810
DSO on net receivables (days) 15.0 15.0 15.0 15.0

Historical DSO is a calculated (black) cell; forecast DSO is an input (blue) cell, usually set as the last actual, a three-year average, or a glide path toward a target. The forecast net receivable is then =Revenue*DSO/365. The FY26E figure above: \( 14{,}400 \times 15 \div 365 = 591.8 \).

Ind AS 109 expected credit loss: why we forecast the net balance

Under Ind AS 109 (Section 5.5) trade receivables without a significant financing component must use the simplified approach: the loss allowance is always measured at lifetime expected credit losses (Ind AS 109.5.5.15). Most Indian companies implement this through a provision matrix — ageing buckets (not due, 0–90, 91–180, 181–365, > 365 days) with historical loss rates adjusted for forward-looking information. Schedule III requires receivables to be presented with the allowance shown separately and split into "considered good – secured", "considered good – unsecured", "significant increase in credit risk" and "credit impaired", plus the ageing schedule introduced by the 2021 amendment.

For modelling, the Balance Sheet carries net receivables, so the default is to compute DSO on net receivables and forecast the net line. The ECL charge sits inside other expenses (or "impairment losses on financial assets") and is already part of the historical cost base forecast on the opex tab. This keeps the asset and the charge consistent without a separate allowance corkscrew.

When you should model gross and allowance separately:

  • The company has large or volatile provisions (infrastructure, EPC, government receivables, NBFC-like trade finance).
  • You want to stress-test credit quality: allowance as % of gross receivables rising from 5% to 10% in a downside case.
  • Write-offs are material, so allowance movement ≠ P&L charge.

The allowance corkscrew is then:

\[ \text{Closing allowance} = \text{Opening} + \text{ECL charge} - \text{Write-offs} \pm \text{Reversals} \]

and the cash flow consequence is the change in net receivables only — the ECL charge is a non-cash expense that reduces the asset, and a write-off reduces gross and allowance equally with no cash or net effect.

Day-count and balance basis

Use 365 consistently (366 is pointless precision; 360 is a US bank convention). Decide whether DSO uses the closing balance or the average of opening and closing. Closing-balance DSO is standard in Indian sell-side models because the forecast directly produces the closing Balance Sheet figure; average-balance DSO is analytically better but makes the forecast recursive (closing = 2 × average − opening), which amplifies any opening-balance noise into oscillating forecasts. Whatever you pick for the history, use for the forecast.

Excel implementation

Historical DSO:   =IFERROR(Net_Receivables/Revenue*365,0)
Forecast balance: =Revenue_t*DSO_t/365
ECL (if split):   =Gross_Receivables_t*ECL_Pct_t
Gross (if split): =Net_Receivables_t/(1-ECL_Pct_t)

Link revenue from the revenue build; never re-key it. Put DSO assumptions on the assumptions tab under the scenario switch so that base/downside/upside can carry different collection behaviour.

What drives a sensible DSO assumption

  • Contractual credit terms and channel mix (disclosed in the MD&A or credit policy notes).
  • Ageing trend from the Schedule III ageing table — growth in "> 6 months" buckets signals collectability stress.
  • Seasonality of the quarter ending 31 March (see s06): a strong Q4 inflates year-end receivables.
  • Factoring, bill discounting or TReDS usage — derecognised receivables lower DSO; if a facility is withdrawn, DSO jumps.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Unbilled revenue. For IT services and projects, unbilled revenue is a contract asset or an unbilled receivable depending on whether the right to consideration is unconditional (Ind AS 115.105–108). Forecast it separately (days of revenue) rather than lumping into DSO; treatment is covered in s05.
  • Related-party and group receivables. Group companies often run receivables on different terms; if material, model separately or flag as quasi-financing.
  • Large one-off ECL reversals or write-offs distort historical DSO; normalise by using gross DSO or excluding the year.
  • Acquisitions mid-year. Closing receivables include the acquiree while revenue includes only post-acquisition months — DSO spikes. Annualise the acquiree revenue or use pro-forma figures.
  • Factoring with recourse does not derecognise under Ind AS 109 (risks retained), so receivables stay on the Balance Sheet with a matching borrowing; without-recourse factoring derecognises. Check the note before interpreting a DSO change.
  • GST inside the balance — covered in c05; historical DSO embeds it, so keep the forecast on the same basis.

Key takeaways

  • Forecast receivables as Revenue × DSO ÷ 365, with DSO as a blue scenario input and revenue linked from the revenue build.
  • Default to the net-of-ECL balance because that is what the Balance Sheet reports; the ECL charge already sits in historical opex.
  • Model gross receivables and an allowance corkscrew only when credit losses are material or are a stress-test variable.
  • Keep day-count (365) and balance basis (closing vs average) identical for history and forecast.
  • Channel-wise DSO beats a blended DSO when the revenue mix is shifting.
  • Rising DSO without a terms or mix explanation is a forensic flag, not a modelling assumption to extrapolate.

Practice Questions

Q1. Forecasting net receivables [L1]

Ganga Chemicals Ltd reported FY25 revenue from operations of ₹3,650 crore and net trade receivables of ₹600 crore. Revenue is forecast to grow 12% in FY26 and 10% in FY27. Management guides that DSO will improve by 3 days per year from the FY25 level. Compute the historical DSO and forecast net receivables for FY26 and FY27, using closing balances and a 365-day year.

Q2. Gross vs net with an ECL allowance corkscrew [L2]

A capital-goods company has FY25 gross trade receivables of ₹1,200 crore and an ECL allowance of ₹90 crore. For FY26: revenue ₹5,400 crore, gross DSO 85 days, ECL charge in P&L ₹40 crore, write-offs ₹25 crore, reversals nil. Build the gross receivable, allowance and net receivable lines for FY26, and state the amount that flows to CFO from the receivables line. Explain why the write-off has no CFO effect.

💡 Hint: Work the allowance corkscrew first; the CFS uses only the change in the net balance.

Q3. Channel-wise DSO for a shifting mix [L2]

A D2C apparel brand earns 70% of FY25 revenue of ₹800 crore from its own website (collected upfront, 0 days), 30% from marketplaces (12-day settlement). By FY28 the marketplace share is expected to be 50% and a new B2B corporate-gifting channel will contribute 10% of revenue at 60 days. Revenue grows 25% a year. Set out the channel-wise receivable build for FY26–FY28 assuming linear mix change, and compare the implied blended DSO with simply holding FY25 blended DSO flat.

Q4. Interpreting a DSO jump [L3]

An NSE-listed auto-ancillary's DSO rose from 58 to 74 days in one year while revenue grew 6%. The notes disclose that a bill-discounting arrangement with a bank was moved from "without recourse" to "with recourse" during the year, and the ageing schedule shows the "> 6 months" bucket doubling. As the FP&A analyst, explain how much of the jump you would treat as presentational versus genuine collection deterioration, what forecast DSO you would assume, and which disclosures you would examine.

💡 Hint: Separate the derecognition effect (balance sheet gross-up with matching borrowing) from the ageing evidence.

Q5. Interview: why not average-balance DSO? [L1]

An interviewer asks why most sell-side models forecast receivables on closing-balance DSO even though average-balance DSO is a better measure of collection efficiency. Give a structured answer covering mechanics, oscillation risk and consistency.
