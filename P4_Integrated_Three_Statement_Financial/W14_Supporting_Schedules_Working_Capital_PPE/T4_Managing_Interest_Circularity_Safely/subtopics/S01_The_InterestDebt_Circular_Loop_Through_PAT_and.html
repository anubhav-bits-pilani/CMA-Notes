Subtopic note

The Interest–Debt Circular Loop Through PAT and Cash Flow

Scope: Loop: interest → PAT → cash flow → revolver/sweep → closing debt → average balance → interest

Why it matters

Every integrated model with a revolver or a cash sweep and average-balance interest contains this loop, whether the builder knows it or not. When a model "goes circular" unexpectedly, or when a reviewer asks "why is your interest circular and is it intended?", you need to be able to trace the exact chain of cells, name the closure points and explain what economic reality the loop represents. In a lender base case, a PE LBO or an equity-research model for a leveraged Indian mid-cap (cement, hospitals, infrastructure developers), the loop determines interest cost, PAT, DSCR and closing debt — the very numbers on which credit and valuation decisions are made.

Core notes

The economics behind the loop

Interest accrues continuously on the balance outstanding during the year. If debt moves from ₹500 crore to ₹400 crore through the year, the true interest is closer to rate × ₹450 crore than rate × ₹500 crore. Using the average of opening and closing balance is the annual-model approximation of that continuous accrual. The problem is that the closing balance of a revolver or swept tranche is not known in advance: it is whatever the cash generation of the year allows, and cash generation is itself after interest. The loop is therefore a faithful representation of a real interdependence, not a modelling bug.

The chain link by link

Step Cell (typical location) Depends on Feeds
1 Interest expense (Debt tab) Rate × average(opening, closing debt) P&L finance costs
2 PBT and tax (P&L / Tax tab) EBIT − interest Current tax, PAT
3 PAT (P&L) PBT − tax CFO start line
4 Cash flow from operations (CFS) PAT + D&A ± ΔNWC ± other non-cash Cash available for debt service
5 Cash available (Debt tab waterfall) Opening cash + CFO + CFI − dividends − mandatory repayment − minimum cash Revolver and sweep
6 Revolver draw/repay and sweep (Debt tab) Cash available, limits, MIN/MAX caps Closing debt
7 Closing debt (Debt tab) Opening + draws − mandatory − sweep Average balance
8 Average balance (Opening + closing) ÷ 2 Back to step 1

Two features of the chain matter for modelling. First, tax sits inside the loop: interest is deductible, so each extra rupee of interest reduces PAT by only \(1 - t\) — at the 115BAA rate of 25.168%, by about ₹0.75. This damps the loop. Second, the closure points are the revolver and sweep cells (step 6). Remove them and the loop breaks: a term loan with a fixed repayment schedule has a known closing balance, so average-balance interest on it is not circular.

Why the loop converges

Consider a revolver at rate \(r\). An extra ₹1 of interest reduces cash by \((1-t)\), which increases the revolver draw by \((1-t)\), which increases average revolver balance by \((1-t)/2\), which increases interest by

\[ \Delta I_{k+1} = \frac{r(1-t)}{2} \times \Delta I_k \]

where \(\Delta I_k\) is the change in interest at iteration \(k\), \(r\) is the revolver rate and \(t\) is the tax rate. With \(r = 9\%\) and \(t = 25.168\%\), the multiplier is about 0.034, so each pass shrinks the change by roughly 97%. The fixed point is reached within three to five iterations. The loop would diverge only if the multiplier exceeded 1, which needs absurd rates — but it can oscillate when MIN/MAX logic switches regime (e.g., revolver flips between draw and repay) on successive passes.

What the loop includes in a full model

  • Finance costs on every average-balance tranche whose closing balance depends on cash (revolver, swept term loans, cash-sweep NCDs).
  • Commitment fees on the undrawn revolver average, which also depend on the closing revolver.
  • Interest income on average cash (the second loop, s02).
  • Tax: current tax on PBT; if the model computes advance tax or tax payable movements, those also feed CFO.
  • Any covenant-driven mechanic, such as a cash trap when DSCR falls below 1.20×, where DSCR uses interest — this adds a third path.

Illustration

Opening revolver ₹100 crore, revolver rate 10%, tax 25.168%. Before interest, the year's cash available is a deficit of ₹20 crore (pre-revolver interest). Under opening-balance interest, revolver interest = ₹10.0 crore; post-tax cash cost ₹7.48 crore; total draw ₹27.48 crore; closing ₹127.48 crore. Under average-balance interest, the solution satisfies

\[ I = 0.10 \times \frac{100 + 100 + 20 + 0.74832\,I}{2} \]

giving \(I \approx 11.43\) crore and closing revolver about ₹128.55 crore. The ₹1.43 crore difference is the materiality question explored in c04. (The illustration ignores interest income and other tranches.)

Mapping the loop in Excel

Use Formulas → Trace Precedents on the interest cell and Trace Dependents on the closing revolver; the arrows will meet. With iteration disabled, Formulas → Error Checking → Circular References lists one cell in the loop — useful to confirm that the loop is the intended one. Document the loop on the Model map tab with a short arrow diagram and the name of the breaker cell.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Fixed repayment schedule only: if all debt has scheduled repayments and there is no revolver or sweep, average-balance interest is non-circular. Do not add iteration just because "average interest is circular" — check.
  • Cash sweep on a fixed-rate NCD: a sweep on a tranche with prepayment penalties may be modelled as an annual sweep at year-end, in which case opening-balance interest is economically accurate and the loop can be avoided.
  • Tax losses: where the company has brought-forward losses and pays no current tax, the damping from \((1-t)\) disappears; the multiplier rises to \(r/2\) but still converges quickly.
  • Capitalised borrowing cost (Ind AS 23): if interest is partly capitalised to CWIP, the capitalised part flows into capex rather than PAT, adding the loop to the PPE schedule. Many modellers fix the capitalised amount as an input to avoid a second path.
  • Monthly or quarterly models: shorter periods reduce the gap between opening and average interest, often making opening-balance interest acceptable.
  • Regime switching: if the revolver is near zero, one iteration may compute a repayment and the next a draw; check convergence (c05) rather than assuming it.

Key takeaways

  • The loop exists only because closing debt depends on cash generated after interest — revolver and sweep are the closure points.
  • Tax deductibility damps the loop; the convergence multiplier is roughly \(r(1-t)/2\), so iterations settle quickly.
  • The loop represents real economics (interest accrues on the moving balance), which is why many practitioners accept it.
  • Term loans with scheduled repayments do not create circularity on their own.
  • Map and document the loop explicitly, and use Excel's circular-reference tracing to confirm it is the only one.
  • Regime switches in MIN/MAX logic, not the arithmetic, are the usual source of non-convergence.

Practice Questions

Q1. Identify the closure points [L1]

A model contains: a ₹400 crore term loan repaying ₹50 crore a year, interest on average balance; a ₹150 crore CC limit drawn to fund any deficit after minimum cash of ₹25 crore, interest on average balance; and fixed deposits earning interest on opening balance. List which of these items are inside the circular loop and explain why each is or is not.

Q2. Solve the fixed point algebraically [L2]

Opening revolver ₹80 crore; revolver rate 9.5%; tax rate 25.168%; pre-interest cash deficit for the year ₹35 crore; no other debt; no interest income. Compute revolver interest and closing revolver under (a) opening-balance interest and (b) average-balance interest solved as a fixed point. Show the equation you solve.

💡 Hint: Write closing revolver as opening + deficit + post-tax interest, then substitute into the average-balance interest formula.

Q3. Convergence speed [L2]

For a model with revolver rate 11% and tax rate 25.168%, compute the per-iteration damping multiplier. Starting from an initial error of ₹5 crore in interest, how many iterations are needed for the change to fall below ₹0.001 crore? Repeat for a company in a tax-loss position.

Q4. Unexpected second path [L3]

A cement company model includes a covenant cash trap: if DSCR (PAT + depreciation + interest) ÷ (interest + principal) falls below 1.25×, 50% of excess cash is trapped in a reserve account rather than swept. Trace every path by which interest now influences closing debt, explain why this can cause oscillation between iterations, and propose how you would restructure the logic to keep the model stable.

Q5. Explaining the loop to a CFO [L1]

In no more than 150 words, explain to a CFO with no modelling background why the interest number in your FY27E plan "depends on itself" and why that is a reasonable feature rather than an error.
