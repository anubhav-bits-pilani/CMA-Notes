Subtopic note

The Second Loop: Interest Income on Average Cash

Scope: Second loop: interest income on average cash balance

Why it matters

Cash-rich Indian companies — IT services, FMCG, pharma, asset-light consumer brands — report substantial "other income", much of it interest on bank deposits, treasury bills and debt mutual-fund returns. For a company such as an IT major with tens of thousands of crore in treasury, interest income can be 5–8% of PBT. If the model earns interest on the average cash balance, and closing cash depends on PAT, which includes that interest income, the model has a second circular loop independent of the debt loop. Even a net-cash company with no revolver becomes circular. Understanding this loop explains why "we have no debt, so why is the model circular?" is a common question in FP&A and research reviews.

Core notes

The chain

\[ \text{Interest income} \rightarrow \text{PBT} \rightarrow \text{Tax} \rightarrow \text{PAT} \rightarrow \text{CFO} \rightarrow \text{Closing cash} \rightarrow \text{Average cash} \rightarrow \text{Interest income} \]

The closure point is closing cash itself, which is the plug of the cash flow statement. Unlike the debt loop, which closes through the revolver/sweep, this loop closes through the very definition of cash: closing cash = opening cash + net cash flow, and net cash flow includes the interest received (whether classified in CFO or CFI — see below).

Where interest income sits in the statements

  • P&L: Under Schedule III Division II, interest income is part of "Other income" and disclosed separately in the notes.
  • CFS: Ind AS 7.33 permits interest received to be classified as operating or investing for non-financial entities, provided the choice is applied consistently. Most Indian corporates show interest received under investing activities; the indirect-method CFO then deducts interest income from PBT and adds it back in CFI. The circularity is the same either way — only its route through the CFS differs.
  • Accrued interest: Interest accrued but not received sits in other current financial assets. If the model accrues, closing cash is affected only by the received portion, which changes the strength of the loop but not its existence.

Strength of the loop

The multiplier is the mirror of the debt loop. An extra ₹1 of interest income adds \((1-t)\) to cash, raising average cash by \((1-t)/2\) and interest income by \(r_c(1-t)/2\), where \(r_c\) is the deposit yield. At \(r_c = 6.5\%\) and \(t = 25.168\%\), the multiplier is about 0.024 — very weak. It converges almost immediately but it is still a circular reference and still needs iteration or a breaker.

Interaction with the debt loop

In a model with both a revolver and cash interest, the two loops share the cash-available line. Typical behaviour:

  • When the company is in surplus, the revolver is repaid first, then cash accumulates above the minimum and earns interest. The debt loop is active in the year the revolver is repaid; the cash loop is active once cash exceeds the minimum.
  • When in deficit, cash is held at minimum (fixed), so average cash is (opening cash + minimum) ÷ 2 and the cash loop is effectively broken; the debt loop is fully active.
  • In a transition year the regime can flip between iterations, which is the main convergence risk (c05).

Building it

Typical rows on the Debt or Cash tab:

Row Formula logic
Opening cash = prior closing cash
Closing cash = link to CFS closing cash (or cash after revolver and sweep)
Average cash = AVERAGE(opening, closing) if switch on; else opening
Interest yield Input (e.g., 6.5% on FD/treasury)
Interest income = yield × average cash, through the same breaker as interest expense 
=IF(Circ=1, Cash_Yield*AVERAGE(Open_Cash,Close_Cash), IF(Circ=2, Cash_Yield*Open_Cash, 0))

Use the same breaker cell for both loops. A model with one switch for interest expense and a forgotten second loop on interest income will remain circular when the "breaker" is off — a frequent reviewer finding.

Excluding restricted and minimum cash

Decide whether the yield applies to total cash or only to cash above the operating minimum. Operating cash in current accounts often earns little; restricted balances such as DSRA deposits, unpaid-dividend accounts and margin money earn deposit rates but cannot be used for the sweep. Model them on separate rows with their own yields where material.

Illustration

Opening cash ₹1,200 crore; pre-interest-income net cash flow for the year ₹300 crore; yield 7%; tax 25.168%; no debt. Under opening-balance interest, interest income = ₹84.0 crore; post-tax ₹62.86 crore; closing cash ₹1,562.86 crore. Under average-balance interest, the fixed point satisfies

\[ I = 0.07 \times \frac{1{,}200 + 1{,}200 + 300 + 0.74832\,I}{2} \]

giving \(I \approx 97.0\) crore and closing cash about ₹1,572.6 crore. The ₹13 crore difference in pre-tax other income is nearly 1% of PBT for a company of this size — not trivial for an EPS estimate.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mutual-fund investments at FVTPL: treasury surplus held in liquid/debt funds produces fair-value gains rather than interest. Model these on the investments line with a return assumption; they still create a loop if returns are on the average balance.
  • Cash netting with overdraft: if the company has both a cash balance and an overdraft, do not net them for interest purposes unless a legal pooling arrangement exists; the debit rate exceeds the deposit rate.
  • Negative cash: a cash balance should never go negative if the revolver works; if it does, interest income at a positive yield on a negative balance produces nonsense. Floor average cash at zero or rely on the revolver to keep cash at the minimum.
  • Tax on interest income: taxed at the normal company rate under 115BAA; no concessional treatment. TDS on interest is a timing issue for cash tax (t5), not for the loop.
  • Large treasury companies: for net-cash companies, the cash loop can be removed with opening-balance interest at little materiality cost, especially if cash grows steadily.

Key takeaways

  • Interest income on average cash creates a circular loop even in debt-free models.
  • The closure point is closing cash, the CFS plug; Ind AS 7 classification choice changes the route but not the loop.
  • The loop is weak (multiplier ≈ \(r_c(1-t)/2\)) but still needs iteration or a breaker.
  • Route interest income through the same breaker as interest expense.
  • Debt and cash loops interact at the minimum-cash boundary — the usual source of oscillation.
  • Separate restricted and operating cash where yields or availability differ.

Practice Questions

Q1. Debt-free but circular [L1]

An FMCG company has no borrowings. Its model shows a circular-reference warning. List the possible causes, starting with interest income, and describe how you would confirm which one is responsible.

Q2. Fixed-point interest income [L2]

Opening cash ₹650 crore; net cash flow before interest income ₹140 crore; deposit yield 6.75%; tax rate 25.168%; interest received classified in investing activities. Compute interest income under opening-balance and average-balance methods and the resulting difference in PAT.

💡 Hint: Classification in CFI does not change closing cash; set up the same fixed-point equation as for interest expense.

Q3. Two loops, one breaker [L2]

A model's breaker cell Circ controls interest expense on the revolver but interest income uses =Yield*AVERAGE(Open_Cash,Close_Cash) with no IF. Explain what happens when the analyst sets Circ to 0 and disables iteration, and rewrite the interest-income formula to fix it.

Q4. Minimum-cash regime switch [L3]

A company holds minimum cash of ₹100 crore with a revolver opening at ₹30 crore. Pre-financing cash flow (before interest expense and income) is +₹32 crore. Revolver rate 10%, deposit yield 6%, tax 25.168%. Explain why the revolver may be fully repaid in one iteration and partly outstanding in the next, describe what you would observe in the model, and propose a modelling change to stabilise it.
