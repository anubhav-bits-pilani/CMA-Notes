Subtopic note

Sec 115BAA Regime Choice and the 25.168% Rate

Scope: Regime choice: Sec 115BAA 22% + 10% surcharge + 4% cess = 25.168%; no MAT; irrevocable option

Why it matters

Since the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019 inserted Sec 115BAA with effect from AY 2020-21 (FY 2019-20), most profitable Indian companies pay tax at an all-in rate of 25.168%. That rate is the default marginal rate in Indian equity research models, in NOPAT for DCF and in the post-tax cost of debt. But not every company has opted in: companies sitting on large MAT credits, SEZ units still enjoying Sec 10AA holidays, or companies claiming large R&D or additional-depreciation deductions have stayed in the old regime for several years and switched later. A modeller must determine which regime applies in each forecast year, whether a switch is planned, and what happens to MAT credit and deferred tax on switching. Getting this wrong shifts the forecast tax line by 8–10 percentage points of PBT.

Core notes

Rate build-up

Under Sec 115BAA a domestic company pays income tax at 22%. The surcharge is a flat 10% irrespective of income level (unlike the slab-based 7%/12% surcharge under the normal regime), and Health and Education Cess is 4% on tax plus surcharge.

\[ 22\% \times (1 + 10\%) \times (1 + 4\%) = 22\% \times 1.10 \times 1.04 = 25.168\% \]

Models usually round to 25.17%. Use the unrounded 25.168% in the inputs sheet and let display formatting round; otherwise DTL computed on large temporary differences will not tie to reported numbers.

Sec 115BAB applied only to companies set up on or after 1 October 2019 that commenced manufacturing on or before 31 March 2024; the window has closed, so new models will rarely use it except for existing beneficiaries.

No MAT

Sec 115JB(5A) provides that MAT does not apply to a company that has exercised the 115BAA option. Consequences:

  • Book profit is irrelevant for tax; you do not need a MAT computation block.
  • MAT credit carried forward under Sec 115JAA cannot be utilised once the company moves to 115BAA. Any MAT credit recognised as an asset (under Ind AS it is usually presented as a deferred tax asset or within income tax assets) must be written off through tax expense in the year the option is exercised or when it becomes clear the credit will not be used.

Conditions of the regime

The option is available only if total income is computed without:

  • Deductions under Sec 10AA (SEZ units), 32(1)(iia) additional depreciation, 32AD (backward-area investment allowance), 33AB, 33ABA (tea/coffee/rubber and site restoration), 35(1)(ii), (iia), (iii) and 35(2AA) (scientific research contributions), 35(2AB) (in-house R&D weighted deduction), 35AD (specified business capex), 35CCC, 35CCD (agricultural extension and skill development);
  • Any deduction under Chapter VI-A Part C (Sec 80-IA, 80-IAB, 80-IB etc.) other than Sec 80JJAA (additional employee cost) and Sec 80M (inter-corporate dividends);
  • Set-off of any loss carried forward or depreciation attributable to the above deductions;
  • Set-off of any loss or allowance for unabsorbed depreciation deemed so under Sec 72A from an amalgamation, where attributable to such deductions;

and depreciation must be computed under Sec 32 in the manner prescribed (i.e., without additional depreciation). Concept c06 develops the disallowance consequences.

Irrevocable option and timing

The company must exercise the option in the prescribed form (Form 10-IC) on or before the due date for furnishing the return under Sec 139(1) for the relevant year. Once exercised for a year it applies to that and all subsequent years and cannot be withdrawn. Failure to satisfy the conditions in a later year renders the option invalid for that year and subsequent years, with the normal provisions applying. There is no deadline for opting in — a company may stay in the old regime and switch in any later year — which is why many companies with MAT credits deferred the switch until the credit was consumed.

Modelling the switch decision

The decision is an NPV comparison. Old regime: tax at ≈ 34.944% (or MAT if higher), less MAT credit utilisation and incentive deductions. New regime: 25.168% with no incentives and lapse of MAT credit. Build both columns for each forecast year:

Old_Tax   = MAX(Normal_Tax_Old, MAT) - MIN(MAT_Credit_Available, MAX(0, Normal_Tax_Old - MAT))
New_Tax   = Taxable_Income_New * 25.168%
Switch_Yr = first year in which cumulative PV(Old_Tax - New_Tax) from that year onward turns positive

Then hard-code the chosen regime with a switch flag on the inputs sheet (Regime = "115BAA" from FY28E), so the forecast is deterministic and auditable.

Deferred tax on switching

Deferred tax is measured at the rate expected to apply when the temporary difference reverses, based on rates enacted or substantively enacted (Ind AS 12.47). When a company decides it will move to 115BAA from a future year, it remeasures the DTL/DTA reversing after that year at 25.168%. In FY 2019-20 many Indian companies booked large one-time deferred tax credits on remeasuring DTLs from 34.944% to 25.168% — a classic distortion of historical ETR (topic pitfall).

Illustration. A company has a DTL on PPE of ₹100 crore measured at 34.944% (temporary difference ≈ ₹286.2 crore). It decides to opt for 115BAA from FY27. Remeasured DTL = 286.2 × 25.168% = ₹72.0 crore; a deferred tax credit of ₹28.0 crore is recognised in P&L (to the extent the original DTL went through P&L). At the same time, ₹40 crore of MAT credit on the balance sheet lapses and is charged to tax expense. Net P&L effect: charge of ₹12.0 crore, wholly non-cash.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • SEZ unit holidays under Sec 10AA: a company with large SEZ profits may rationally stay in the old regime until the holiday expires; model the regime switch at the expiry year.
  • Substantively enacted. In India a rate is generally considered substantively enacted when the Finance Bill is passed by both Houses (before Presidential assent) or when an ordinance is promulgated — 115BAA was introduced by ordinance on 20 September 2019, so September-quarter results already reflected the remeasurement.
  • Surcharge changes. A future Finance Act could change surcharge or cess; keep each component as a separate input so a rate change flows to both current tax and deferred-tax remeasurement.
  • Income-tax Act, 2025 (effective 1 April 2026) re-enacts the concessional regime under a new section number; the rate and conditions are carried over, but verify the mapping before citing sections in client deliverables.
  • Special-rate income (e.g., long-term capital gains at 12.5% under Sec 112 post July 2024, or 20% indexed for earlier periods) is taxed at its own rate even under 115BAA; a model with large treasury gains needs a separate rate.

Key takeaways

  • 22% × 1.10 × 1.04 = 25.168%; store components separately and use the unrounded figure.
  • 115BAA companies are outside MAT; brought-forward MAT credit lapses on opting in.
  • The option requires forgoing specified deductions (incl. additional depreciation and Chapter VI-A Part C other than 80JJAA/80M) and is irrevocable once exercised via Form 10-IC by the Sec 139(1) due date.
  • The opt-in year is a choice; model both regimes, pick the switch year, then fix it as an input.
  • Switching triggers a remeasurement of deferred tax and a write-off of MAT credit — both non-cash, both one-offs to strip out of historical ETR.

Practice Questions

Q1. Rate build-up [L1]

Compute the all-in rate for (a) a company under Sec 115BAA and (b) a company under normal provisions with a 30% base rate and taxable income of ₹50 crore. Show surcharge and cess separately.

Q2. Switch-year analysis [L3]

A company expects taxable income (old regime, after Sec 35(2AB) and 80-IA deductions) of ₹400 crore, ₹450 crore, ₹500 crore and ₹550 crore for FY27E–FY30E; the same income without those deductions would be ₹460 crore, ₹510 crore, ₹560 crore and ₹610 crore. Book profit for MAT equals ₹520 crore, ₹570 crore, ₹620 crore and ₹670 crore. MAT credit available at 1 April 2026 is ₹70 crore. Using 34.944% normal, 17.472% MAT and 25.168% for 115BAA and a 12% discount rate, build a year-by-year comparison and recommend the year to exercise the option.

💡 Hint: Remember MAT credit can only be used to the extent normal tax exceeds MAT in a year, and it lapses on switching.

Q3. Journal entries on opting in [L2]

At 31 March 2026 a company with a DTL of ₹54 crore (measured at 34.944%) and recognised MAT credit of ₹22 crore resolves to opt for 115BAA from FY 2025-26 itself. Pass the journal entries and quantify the impact on FY26 reported ETR if PBT is ₹300 crore.

Q4. Irrevocability judgement [L2]

A company opted for 115BAA in FY 2021-22. In FY 2026-27 it plans an SEZ unit and asks whether it can return to the old regime to claim Sec 10AA. Advise, covering the consequence of claiming a disallowed deduction.

Q5. Interview: why 25.17% in NOPAT [L1]

Explain why a DCF analyst uses 25.17% as the marginal tax rate for an Indian company even when its reported ETR has been 28–30% for three years.
