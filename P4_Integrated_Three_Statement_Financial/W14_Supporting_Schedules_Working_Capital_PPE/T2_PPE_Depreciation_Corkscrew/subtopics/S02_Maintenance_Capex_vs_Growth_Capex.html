Subtopic note

Maintenance Capex vs Growth Capex

Scope: Maintenance capex (≈ D&A or % of opening gross block) vs growth capex (per store/per tonne capacity)

Why it matters

Capex is the bridge between the revenue story and the cash story. Growth assumptions in the Week 13 operating model — new stores, new tonnes of capacity, new towers — are only credible if the model pays for them. Separating capex into maintenance (keeping the existing asset base productive) and growth (adding capacity) lets you tie growth capex to the volume build, test whether maintenance spending is enough to sustain margins, and compute steady-state free cash flow for the terminal value. Equity research and credit analysts ask for this split even though Indian companies rarely disclose it; management commentary ("₹500 crore routine capex plus ₹2,000 crore for the new plant") is the usual source.

Core notes

Maintenance capex

Maintenance capex replaces worn-out assets, refurbishes stores, upgrades IT and meets safety and environmental compliance. Three common approaches:

  1. ≈ Depreciation (D&A) — the assumption that sustaining the asset base costs roughly what it depreciates. Reasonable for mature businesses in moderate inflation; it understates when replacement cost has inflated far above historical cost (old plants depreciated on decades-old cost) and overstates when assets are young.
  2. % of opening gross block — e.g., 2–4% for heavy industry, linked to the asset base being maintained:

\[ \text{Maintenance capex}_t = m \times \text{Gross block}_{t-1} \]

This scales with the installed base, which is economically right, and avoids circularity with depreciation. 3. Per unit — per store refurbishment every \( n \) years (e.g., ₹1.5 crore per store every 7 years), per tonne of installed capacity (₹300–600 per tonne per year for cement), per tower for telecom.

In the terminal year of a DCF, total capex must exceed D&A by enough to fund terminal growth: a rule of thumb is \( \text{Net capex} \approx \text{g} \div \text{RONIC} \times \text{NOPAT} \) for consistency with Week 16 reinvestment logic.

Growth capex

Growth capex is volume-linked and should be driven from the same unit drivers that build revenue:

  • Retail (per store / per sq ft): \( \text{Growth capex}_t = \text{New stores}_t \times \text{Avg sq ft} \times \text{Capex per sq ft} \). Fashion retailers in India spend roughly ₹2,000–4,000 per sq ft on fit-outs for leased stores (indicative; calibrate to disclosures). Owned stores add land and building.
  • Manufacturing (per tonne / per unit capacity): \( \text{Growth capex} = \Delta\text{Capacity} \times \text{Capex per tonne} \), phased over the construction period. Indian cement greenfield capex is often quoted at USD 70–100 per tonne (≈ ₹6,000–8,500 per tonne); brownfield grinding units much less. Steel, chemicals and pharma use capex per tonne or per KL reactor capacity.
  • Asset-light / services: % of incremental revenue or per new employee seat (IT services, ₹2–5 lakh per seat).

The essential discipline: capacity and revenue must reconcile. If the revenue build assumes utilisation of 85% on a capacity that requires a new 2 MTPA plant from FY28, the capex schedule must show that plant being built in FY26–FY27, CWIP building up (s03), and depreciation starting when it is commissioned.

Growth capex FY26 = \( 120 \times 10{,}000 \times 3{,}000 \div 10^7 = 360 \) crore. The opening gross block row is linked from the s01 corkscrew (here assuming all capex is capitalised in the year and no disposals, so each opening = prior opening + prior capex). Compare maintenance capex with the depreciation on the existing layer (s04): if it is far below, the existing estate is being under-invested; if far above, part of it is probably growth.

Phasing and timing

  • Store capex is spent 2–4 months before opening; stores opening in Q1 of FY27 incur capex in Q4 FY26. In annual models, a lead-time switch shifts a % of next year's store capex into the current year.
  • Plant capex is spread over a 2–3 year construction period (e.g., 30/50/20%), accumulates in CWIP, and is capitalised on commissioning.
  • Capital advances to equipment suppliers sit in non-current assets and are part of cash capex.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Disclosure gap: Indian companies rarely split maintenance and growth; use management guidance, analyst meets and the last 3–5 years' capex in years with no capacity additions as a proxy for maintenance.
  • Inflation and replacement cost: in high-inflation or technology-shift industries (telecom 4G → 5G), maintenance capex ≈ D&A underestimates.
  • Leased vs owned stores: for leased stores, the lease itself goes to the Ind AS 116 ROU schedule (s06), not capex; only fit-outs are capex.
  • Regulatory/ESG capex (flue-gas desulphurisation for thermal power, effluent treatment plants) is non-discretionary but does not add capacity — classify as maintenance.
  • Debottlenecking blurs the line: small capex lifting capacity 10% — classify as growth with its own capex per tonne.
  • Terminal value: growth capex should fade to a level consistent with terminal growth; zero growth capex with positive terminal growth is inconsistent.

Key takeaways

  • Split capex into maintenance (sustain the base) and growth (add capacity) even when the company does not disclose it.
  • Maintenance capex ≈ D&A or % of opening gross block or per-unit; choose and document.
  • Growth capex must be driven by the same units that drive revenue — stores, sq ft, tonnes, seats.
  • Phase capex ahead of commissioning; accumulate plant capex in CWIP.
  • Cross-check maintenance capex against D&A, and terminal capex against terminal growth.

Practice Questions

Q1. Growth capex from store additions [L1]

A grocery chain opens 50, 60 and 70 stores in FY26–FY28, each 4,000 sq ft, at fit-out cost of ₹2,500 per sq ft rising 5% a year. 25% of each year's store capex is spent in the prior year because of lead time. Compute growth capex by year with and without the lead-time shift (assume FY29 additions of 80 stores).

Q2. Per-tonne capacity capex for cement [L2]

A cement company with 20 MTPA capacity plans a 4 MTPA greenfield plant commissioning on 1 April 2028 at ₹7,000 per tonne, spent 25% in FY26, 50% in FY27 and 25% in FY28 (by 31 March 2028). Maintenance capex is ₹400 per tonne of installed capacity per year. Revenue build assumes 80% utilisation of total capacity from FY29. Compute total capex FY26–FY29 and explain how the revenue and capex schedules must be linked.

💡 Hint: Maintenance applies to installed capacity at the start of each year; the new plant is not installed until commissioning.

Q3. Maintenance capex method choice [L2]

A 30-year-old refinery has gross block ₹40,000 crore (historical cost), annual depreciation ₹1,100 crore, and engineering estimates of sustaining capex of ₹2,000 crore a year. Compare maintenance capex under the three methods (≈ D&A, 4% of gross block, engineering estimate), and argue which is appropriate for a DCF terminal year.

Q4. Case: revenue without capex [L3]

A model for a QSR chain shows revenue growing from ₹2,400 crore to ₹6,000 crore over five years via 800 new restaurants, while capex is held at ₹150 crore a year (equal to FY25 capex). Write a review comment explaining the inconsistency, how you would rebuild capex, and the expected direction of the change to FCFF and valuation.
