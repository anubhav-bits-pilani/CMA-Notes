Subtopic note

Sanity Checks for the PPE Schedule

Scope: Sanity checks: capex/sales, capex/D&A, fixed-asset turnover, average remaining life

Why it matters

A PPE schedule can be mechanically correct — corkscrews balance, net block stays positive — and still be economically absurd: capex that collapses while revenue doubles, depreciation that falls while the asset base grows, or a fixed-asset turnover that implies the company has discovered a way to sell three times as much from the same factories. Investment committees, lenders and equity research reviewers test PPE forecasts with a short set of ratios, compared against the company's own history and listed peers. Building these ratios as diagnostic rows under the schedule, with conditional-format flags, catches errors before a reviewer does and gives you the language to defend assumptions.

Core notes

1. Capex / sales (capex intensity)

\[ \text{Capex intensity} = \frac{\text{Capex}_t}{\text{Revenue}_t} \]

Reference ranges (indicative, Indian listed companies, vary by cycle): FMCG 2–4%; IT services 2–3%; pharma 5–8%; fashion retail 4–7%; auto OEMs 4–7%; cement 10–20% in expansion years; telecom 20–30%; power and infra much higher during build. Use it in two ways:

  • Trend vs history: a forecast intensity far below the five-year average needs a story (capacity already built, asset-light pivot).
  • Consistency with growth: high revenue growth with low intensity is suspicious unless utilisation has headroom. Compute utilisation alongside: \( \text{Utilisation} = \text{Volume} \div \text{Capacity} \); if utilisation exceeds ~90% without new capacity, revenue or capex is wrong.

2. Capex / D&A

\[ \text{Reinvestment ratio} = \frac{\text{Capex}_t}{\text{D\&A}_t} \]

  • \( > 1 \): the asset base is growing (expected in growth phases).
  • \( \approx 1 \): maintenance mode — steady state with low inflation.
  • \( < 1 \) for several years: the asset base is shrinking — fine for a harvest strategy, not for a company forecasting volume growth.

In the terminal year of a DCF, capex/D&A should be modestly above 1 for a growing company (inflation alone requires this, because replacement cost exceeds historical cost). A terminal capex equal to D&A with 5% perpetual growth is a classic inconsistency.

3. Fixed-asset turnover

\[ \text{FAT} = \frac{\text{Revenue}_t}{\text{Average net block}_t} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\text{Revenue}_t}{\text{Average gross block}_t} \]

Net-block FAT rises mechanically as assets age (net block falls through depreciation), so gross-block FAT is a better measure of physical productivity; report both. Exclude CWIP (not yet productive) and ROU assets (track separately as revenue ÷ ROU for retailers, or compute FAT including ROU to compare with peers that own stores). A forecast FAT that rises steeply means each rupee of assets generates more sales than ever — justified only by price inflation, mix upgrade, utilisation gains or asset-light growth.

Links to ROCE: \( \text{ROCE} = \text{EBIT margin} \times \text{Capital turnover} \), so FAT drives the return story in Phase 2 analysis.

4. Average remaining life and asset age

\[ \text{Average age (years)} \approx \frac{\text{Accumulated depreciation}}{\text{Annual depreciation}} \qquad \text{Remaining life} \approx \frac{\text{Net block} - \text{Residual}}{\text{Annual depreciation}} \]

\[ \text{Asset age ratio} = \frac{\text{Accumulated depreciation}}{\text{Gross depreciable block}} \]

Interpretation:

  • A rising age ratio (e.g., 45% → 65%) signals under-investment — upcoming maintenance capex, efficiency loss, or a looming capacity addition.
  • Remaining life in the forecast should evolve plausibly: if it falls below 2–3 years with flat capex, depreciation is about to fall off a cliff (s04) and the existing asset base is near end of life.
  • Compare total life (age + remaining life) with the Schedule II life for the dominant class; large gaps indicate a modelling error in RUL or depreciation.

Peer benchmarking

Pull capex/sales, FAT and asset age for 3–5 listed peers from annual reports (or databases such as Capitaline, Prowess, ACE Equity). Use multi-year averages, since capex is lumpy. Adjust for business model (owned vs leased stores, outsourced vs in-house manufacturing, captive power).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Lumpy capex: a single mega-project distorts annual ratios; use 3-year rolling averages.
  • Ind AS 116 effects: pre-FY20 historical ratios are not comparable if rent was opex then; restate or compare post-FY20 only.
  • Revaluations or deemed cost resets (Ind AS 101) break age ratios.
  • Inflation and price-led revenue: FAT rises with price even with flat volumes — compare volume-based FAT (tonnes per ₹ crore gross block).
  • Asset-light pivots (outsourced manufacturing, franchise stores) structurally lower capex intensity — acceptable if the revenue build reflects the margin trade-off.
  • Impairments reduce net block and raise FAT artificially.

Key takeaways

  • Build capex/sales, capex/D&A, FAT (net and gross), asset age and remaining life as diagnostic rows with flags.
  • Capex/D&A persistently below 1 contradicts volume growth; terminal capex must exceed D&A for positive growth.
  • Gross-block FAT is a cleaner productivity measure than net-block FAT.
  • Rising asset age and shrinking remaining life signal under-investment or an imminent depreciation cliff.
  • Pair every ratio with utilisation and peer benchmarks; explain deviations explicitly.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute diagnostic ratios [L1]

FY26E: revenue ₹8,400 crore; capex ₹520 crore; D&A ₹410 crore; opening and closing net block ₹3,100 and ₹3,210 crore; opening and closing gross block ₹5,600 and ₹6,050 crore; closing accumulated depreciation ₹2,840 crore; freehold land in gross block ₹250 crore; residual value nil. Compute capex/sales, capex/D&A, FAT on net and gross block, asset age ratio and remaining life.

Q2. Diagnose a forecast [L2]

A steel company's forecast shows volume growing 8% a year for five years with capacity flat at 10 MTPA and FY25 utilisation of 82%; capex/D&A falls from 1.4× to 0.8×; net-block FAT rises from 1.6× to 2.9×. Identify the inconsistencies, the year in which capacity becomes binding, and the changes you would make.

💡 Hint: Compound the volume growth on FY25 output and compare it with capacity each year.

Q3. Terminal-year consistency [L2]

In a DCF, terminal-year NOPAT is ₹1,000 crore, D&A ₹400 crore, capex ₹400 crore, ΔNWC ₹30 crore and perpetual growth 5%. Explain why the capex assumption is inconsistent and propose a method to set terminal capex using a target return on new invested capital of 15%.

Q4. Interview: why does FAT rise as assets age? [L1]

Explain why net-block fixed-asset turnover can rise even with no productivity improvement, and which alternative measure you would show an investment committee.
