Subtopic note

Non-Controlling Interest Corkscrew

Scope: NCI corkscrew: opening + NCI share of PAT − dividends to NCI

Why it matters

Many large Indian groups consolidate listed and unlisted subsidiaries that are not wholly owned — conglomerates with listed subsidiaries, auto groups with partly-owned component companies, pharma companies with JV-turned-subsidiaries, and holding companies such as those in infrastructure or financial services. In their consolidated statements, NCI can be a large share of equity and profit. A consolidated model must split profit and equity between owners of the parent and NCI, because valuation (equity value per share of the parent), EPS (Ind AS 33 uses owners' profit) and the cash flow (dividends to NCI leave the group) all depend on the split. Leaving NCI flat while subsidiaries grow is one of the most common consolidated-model errors.

Core notes

Recognition and presentation

  • Ind AS 110.22: NCI is presented in the consolidated balance sheet within equity, separately from the equity of owners of the parent.
  • Ind AS 110.B94: profit or loss and each component of OCI are attributed to owners of the parent and to NCI; total comprehensive income is attributed to NCI even if this results in a deficit (negative) NCI balance.
  • Schedule III Division II requires the split of profit and of total comprehensive income on the face of the consolidated P&L, and NCI as a separate line within equity.
  • Ind AS 103.19: at acquisition, NCI that is a present ownership interest is measured at fair value or at its proportionate share of identifiable net assets (choice per acquisition); the choice affects goodwill and the NCI opening balance but not the corkscrew mechanics afterwards.

The corkscrew

\[ NCI_t = NCI_{t-1} + \text{PAT}^{NCI}_t + \text{OCI}^{NCI}_t - \text{Div}^{NCI}_t \pm \text{Ownership changes}_t \]

where \(\text{PAT}^{NCI}_t\) is the NCI share of the subsidiaries' profit, \(\text{OCI}^{NCI}_t\) the NCI share of OCI (usually forecast at zero), \(\text{Div}^{NCI}_t\) dividends declared by subsidiaries to minority shareholders, and ownership changes are acquisitions or disposals of stakes without loss of control or capital injections by NCI.

Forecasting the NCI share of PAT

Three approaches, in increasing accuracy:

  1. Ratio method: NCI share = historical NCI/consolidated PAT ratio × forecast consolidated PAT. Quick; acceptable if subsidiaries grow in line with the group.
  2. Segment method: if subsidiaries are separate reported segments, NCI share = Σ (subsidiary PAT × NCI %). Better when subsidiaries grow at different rates.
  3. Entity build: model each material subsidiary (or use its own published forecast if listed) and apply NCI % to its PAT after intercompany eliminations.

Adjust for fair-value adjustments at acquisition (additional depreciation on step-up attributable to NCI if NCI measured at fair value or proportionate share of fair-valued net assets) — material in recent acquisitions.

Dividends to NCI

When a subsidiary declares dividends, the parent's share eliminates on consolidation; the NCI share is a cash outflow of the group, shown in CFF (Ind AS 7.17, as "dividends paid to non-controlling interests"). Model:

\[ \text{Div}^{NCI}_t = \text{Subsidiary dividend}_t \times \text{NCI \%} \]

or as a payout ratio × NCI share of PAT. The same timing lag as for the parent applies: a subsidiary's final dividend is paid after its AGM in the next year.

Changes in ownership without loss of control

Ind AS 110.23: changes in the parent's ownership interest that do not result in loss of control are equity transactions. If the parent buys a further 10% of a subsidiary from NCI, NCI is reduced by its carrying amount of that 10%, cash is paid, and any difference is recognised directly in equity attributable to owners (often retained earnings or a separate reserve). No gain/loss and no goodwill change. If a subsidiary issues shares to outside investors (e.g., a pre-IPO round or IPO of a subsidiary), NCI increases by the share of net assets attributable to new investors, and the difference versus cash received goes to owners' equity.

Model rows

Row Logic
NCI opening prior closing (first year = last actual)
+ NCI share of PAT link to P&L split
+ NCI share of OCI usually 0
− Dividends to NCI subsidiary dividend × NCI %
± Change in ownership / NCI capital infusion input
NCI closing sum
Memo: NCI % (by subsidiary) input
Check: implied NCI share of subsidiary net assets ≈ NCI % × subsidiary equity

Linking to the rest of the model

  • P&L: consolidated PAT − NCI share = PAT attributable to owners → retained earnings (s02) and EPS (s07, c05).
  • BS: NCI line within total equity (c03).
  • CFS: CFO starts from consolidated PBT/PAT (including NCI share); dividends to NCI in CFF.
  • Valuation (Week 16): in an EV-to-equity bridge, deduct the value of NCI (ideally at market value for listed subsidiaries or multiple of NCI share of earnings, not book value) to get equity value attributable to the parent.

Illustration

Group with two subsidiaries: Sub A (70% owned) PAT FY28E ₹200 crore, dividend payout 50%; Sub B (60% owned) PAT ₹50 crore, no dividends. Opening NCI ₹480 crore.

  • NCI share of PAT = 30% × 200 + 40% × 50 = 60 + 20 = ₹80 crore.
  • Dividends to NCI = 30% × (50% × 200) = ₹30 crore (assuming paid in year).
  • Closing NCI = 480 + 80 − 30 = ₹530 crore.

If consolidated PAT is ₹900 crore, owners' PAT = ₹820 crore, which is the figure for retained earnings and EPS.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Negative NCI: loss-making subsidiaries produce negative NCI balances (Ind AS 110.B94); do not floor at zero.
  • Preference shares in subsidiaries held by outsiders: if classified as equity in the subsidiary, they are part of NCI; cumulative preference dividends are deducted before allocating profit to ordinary NCI (Ind AS 110.B95).
  • Loss of control: deconsolidation removes NCI entirely and recognises gain/loss in P&L (Ind AS 110.25) — a discontinuity to model as an event, not through the corkscrew.
  • Put options written to NCI: may require a financial liability for the redemption amount (Ind AS 32.23), with accounting-policy choices for the NCI; check the company's policy before modelling.
  • Ratio method distortion: if a high-growth subsidiary is partly owned, the group ratio understates future NCI share.
  • Standalone models: no NCI; but dividends received from subsidiaries are income of the standalone entity.

Key takeaways

  • NCI sits within equity, separately from owners' equity (Ind AS 110.22).
  • Closing NCI = opening + NCI share of PAT (and OCI) − dividends to NCI ± ownership changes.
  • Only owners' PAT goes to retained earnings and EPS; dividends to NCI are a group cash outflow in CFF.
  • Changes in ownership without loss of control are equity transactions — no P&L gain/loss.
  • NCI can be negative; forecast by subsidiary where growth rates differ.
  • In valuation, deduct NCI at market value rather than book value where possible.

Practice Questions

Q1. Basic NCI corkscrew [L1]

Opening NCI ₹215 crore. Subsidiary (65% owned) PAT ₹140 crore; subsidiary declares and pays dividends of ₹60 crore during the year. Compute NCI share of PAT, dividends to NCI and closing NCI. If consolidated PAT is ₹610 crore, what is PAT attributable to owners?

Q2. Acquisition of additional stake [L2]

The parent owns 60% of a subsidiary whose net assets in the consolidated accounts are ₹900 crore (NCI carrying amount ₹360 crore). On 1 October 2027 it buys a further 15% from NCI for ₹230 crore. Pass the consolidation entry, compute closing NCI at 31 March 2028 if subsidiary PAT for FY28 is ₹120 crore earned evenly, and explain where the difference goes.

💡 Hint: Split the year's NCI share of PAT into the periods before and after the stake change.

Q3. Forecast method choice [L2]

A group's consolidated PAT is ₹1,200 crore, of which NCI ₹96 crore (8%). A 51%-owned digital subsidiary contributes ₹100 crore PAT growing 40% a year; the rest of the group grows 8%. Forecast the NCI share for three years using the ratio method and the segment method (assume all NCI arises from the digital subsidiary), and comment on the difference.

Q4. Valuation bridge [L3]

The parent's EV from a consolidated DCF is ₹18,000 crore. Net debt ₹2,400 crore; book NCI ₹1,100 crore; the 55%-owned listed subsidiary has a market capitalisation of ₹6,000 crore. Explain how you would deduct NCI in the EV-to-equity bridge, compute equity value under book and market approaches, and justify your choice.
