Subtopic note

Buybacks: Capital Reduction, CRR and Shareholder Taxation

Scope: Buybacks: reduction of capital/premium, CRR creation, shareholder-level taxation since 1 Oct 2024 (check latest Finance Act)

Why it matters

Buybacks are a major capital-return tool for cash-rich Indian companies — IT services majors have run repeated tender-offer buybacks worth thousands of crore, and many mid-caps use them to return surplus cash or support the share price. In a model a buyback touches share capital, securities premium, free reserves, the CRR, cash, the share count and EPS simultaneously, and it is constrained by Sec 68, Sec 69, Sec 70 and the SEBI (Buy-Back of Securities) Regulations, 2018. Its tax treatment changed fundamentally from 1 October 2024, shifting the burden from the company to shareholders, which changes both the company's cash cost and shareholders' net returns — a point that comes up in capital-allocation discussions and interviews.

Core notes

Legal framework (Companies Act 2013)

Sources (Sec 68(1)): free reserves, securities premium, or proceeds of an issue of shares or other specified securities — but not proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind of shares.

Key conditions (Sec 68(2)):

  • Authorised by the articles of association.
  • Special resolution of shareholders; a board resolution suffices if the buyback is up to 10% of total paid-up equity capital and free reserves.
  • Buyback not exceeding 25% of the aggregate of paid-up capital and free reserves; for equity shares, not more than 25% of total paid-up equity capital in a financial year.
  • Post-buyback ratio of secured and unsecured debt to paid-up capital and free reserves not exceeding 2:1, unless a higher ratio is prescribed for a class of companies.
  • Only fully paid-up shares; no further buyback offer within one year of the closure of the previous one.
  • Completion within one year of the resolution; shares extinguished and physically destroyed within seven days of completion (Sec 68(7)).
  • No fresh issue of the same kind of shares for six months, except bonus issues and conversion of existing obligations (Sec 68(8)).

Sec 70 prohibits buybacks through subsidiaries or investment companies and by a company in default on deposits, debentures, preference redemption, term loans or dividends (until the default is remedied and three years have passed).

Sec 69 requires that where shares are bought back out of free reserves or securities premium, a sum equal to the nominal value of shares bought back is transferred to the capital redemption reserve.

SEBI Buy-Back Regulations, 2018 (listed companies): tender offer (proportionate, with a reservation for small shareholders), open-market purchase through stock exchanges (phased out — the stock-exchange route is no longer available for buybacks from 1 April 2025 under the 2023 amendments) and book building; public announcement, escrow and timelines; restrictions on promoters' trading during the buyback.

Accounting entries

Under Ind AS 32.33, consideration paid to reacquire own equity is deducted from equity; no gain or loss is recognised in P&L. Transaction costs (merchant banker, legal, stamp duty, exchange fees) are also deducted from equity (net of any tax effect).

Example: buyback of 1.5 crore shares of ₹5 face value at ₹1,200; costs ₹6 crore; premium charged first to securities premium (available ₹900 crore), balance to general reserve.

The order of charging (premium vs free reserves) follows the board's resolution and the company's practice; model it as an input. The CRR is created from the reserve chosen — here general reserve, since premium was exhausted.

Taxation

Up to 30 September 2024 — Sec 115QA: the company paid "additional income-tax" at 20% (plus 12% surcharge and 4% cess, effective 23.296%) on distributed income — buyback consideration minus the amount received by the company on issue of those shares. Shareholders were exempt under Sec 10(34A). The tax was a company cash cost, recorded in equity alongside the buyback (it relates to a transaction with owners) and in CFF.

From 1 October 2024 — Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024:

  • Sec 115QA no longer applies to buybacks on or after that date; the company pays no buyback tax.
  • The entire consideration received by the shareholder is treated as a deemed dividend under Sec 2(22)(f), taxable at the shareholder's applicable rate (slab rates for resident individuals, normal rates for companies) with no deduction for cost.
  • The cost of acquisition of the shares bought back is treated as a capital loss (consideration deemed nil under Sec 46A), which can be set off against capital gains and carried forward under the normal rules.
  • The company deducts TDS on the deemed dividend under Sec 194 (residents) or Sec 195 (non-residents), so the cash paid to shareholders is net of TDS and the TDS is remitted to the government — total company cash outflow equals gross consideration.

Check the latest Finance Act. Buyback taxation has changed twice in five years; before modelling a buyback in FY27 or later, verify whether any later Finance Act, or the Income-tax Act, 2025 (effective 1 April 2026, with renumbered sections), has altered the deemed-dividend treatment or introduced differential treatment for categories of shareholders.

Modelling implications. Under the current regime: (1) the company's cash cost is the gross consideration plus costs, with no company-level tax line; (2) the shareholder's post-tax return depends on their tax profile — for a resident individual in the 30% slab, buyback may be less attractive than a market sale taxed as LTCG at 12.5%, which affects tender participation and the choice between buyback and dividend; (3) promoter participation decisions may change.

Illustration of limit tests

Paid-up equity ₹250 crore; free reserves ₹3,750 crore (for Sec 68, free reserves include the securities premium account, per the Explanation to the section); debt ₹2,100 crore. Proposed buyback ₹1,800 crore.

  • 25% of (paid-up capital + free reserves) = 25% × 4,000 = ₹1,000 crore → proposal fails; maximum ₹1,000 crore.
  • Above 10% (₹400 crore) → special resolution needed.
  • Post-buyback debt-equity with ₹1,000 crore: equity base 4,000 − 1,000 = ₹3,000 crore; 2,100 ÷ 3,000 = 0.70 → passes.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Free reserves definition (Sec 2(43)) excludes unrealised and fair-value gains; FVOCI and revaluation reserves cannot fund a buyback.
  • Buyback vs dividend choice post-October 2024: with both taxed as dividend in shareholders' hands, the buyback's remaining advantages are the capital-loss benefit and flexibility; model shareholders' after-tax outcomes if advising.
  • Settlement after year-end: a buyback approved in March but settled in April affects next year's cash and share count; any liability recognised at year-end must reflect the legal obligation.
  • Treasury shares are not allowed for Indian companies under the Companies Act — bought-back shares are extinguished (unlike ESOP-trust treasury shares in consolidation).
  • Consolidated groups: a subsidiary buying back from NCI is a change in ownership without loss of control — an equity transaction (s06).
  • EPS: reduce weighted-average shares from the date of extinguishment/settlement; buybacks at high P/E can be EPS-dilutive if funded by cash earning high interest.

Key takeaways

  • Buybacks reduce share capital (FV) and securities premium/free reserves (excess); no P&L impact (Ind AS 32.33).
  • Sec 69: transfer the nominal value to CRR when funded from free reserves or premium.
  • Sec 68 limits: 25% of paid-up capital + free reserves (10% with board approval), 25% of equity capital in a year, 2:1 debt-equity, one-year gap.
  • From 1 October 2024, no company-level buyback tax; consideration is deemed dividend for shareholders, with cost as capital loss; company withholds TDS.
  • Always check the latest Finance Act and the Income-tax Act, 2025 mapping before modelling.
  • Feed the share count, cash, capital and reserve schedules together, with limit checks.

Practice Questions

Q1. Journal entries and CRR [L1]

A company buys back 80 lakh shares of ₹10 at ₹650 through a tender offer, charging the premium entirely to securities premium (₹1,200 crore available). Buyback costs are ₹3.2 crore. Pass the journal entries, including the Sec 69 transfer, and state the closing effect on total equity.

Q2. Limit testing [L2]

Paid-up equity capital ₹180 crore; securities premium ₹520 crore; general reserve ₹1,100 crore; retained earnings ₹680 crore; FVOCI reserve ₹90 crore; total borrowings ₹4,400 crore. Determine the maximum buyback under Sec 68, whether board approval suffices for a ₹230 crore buyback, and whether the 2:1 debt-equity condition is met after a buyback at the maximum.

💡 Hint: Exclude reserves that are not free reserves under Sec 2(43), remembering that Sec 68 includes securities premium; test debt-equity on post-buyback capital and free reserves.

Q3. Shareholder tax comparison [L2]

A resident individual in the 30% slab (plus 4% cess; ignore surcharge) holds 10,000 shares bought at ₹400 three years ago. The company offers a buyback at ₹900; the market price is ₹860. Compare the post-tax outcome of tendering into the buyback after 1 October 2024 with selling on the exchange (LTCG at 12.5% above the ₹1.25 lakh exemption, ignoring STT), assuming the shareholder has other capital gains of ₹50 lakh to absorb any capital loss. State what you would verify in the latest Finance Act.

Q4. Model integration [L3]

Specify every row change in the share-count, share capital, securities premium, general reserve, CRR, cash and CFS schedules for a ₹2,000 crore buyback settled on 1 January 2028 at ₹1,600 per share (face value ₹1), and the checks you would add. Compute the effect on FY28 weighted-average shares if opening shares are 75 crore.
