Subtopic note

Optional Cash Sweep by Seniority

Scope: Optional cash sweep: % of excess cash applied by seniority

Why it matters

A cash sweep applies surplus cash, after the revolver is cleared, to prepay term debt — either because a loan agreement requires it (excess cash flow sweep in leveraged and project finance) or because management chooses to deleverage. In Indian practice, sweep clauses are standard in acquisition finance, structured promoter financing and project finance (often tied to DSCR thresholds), and voluntary prepayments are common when companies generate surplus and term loans carry high spreads. The sweep determines how fast leverage falls, which drives covenant headroom, ratings and, in an LBO, equity returns. Done wrong, it sweeps before mandatory obligations, ignores minimum cash or drives a tranche below zero — all classic errors.

Core notes

Where the sweep sits

The sweep is the last step in the waterfall (s03): CFO → capex → mandatory debt → dividends → revolver → sweep. Cash available for sweep is what remains after the revolver change:

\[ \text{Cash for sweep}_t = \max\left(0,\ \text{Cash available}_t - \text{Revolver repayment}_t\right) \]

(If the revolver drew, cash for sweep is zero by construction.)

Sweep amount per tranche

\[ \text{Sweep}_t = \min\left( \text{Cash for sweep}_t \times s,\ \text{Opening}_t + \text{Draws}_t - \text{Mandatory}_t \right) \]

where \( s \) is the sweep percentage (e.g., 50%, 75% or 100% of excess cash; contracts often step down \( s \) as leverage falls), and the cap is the tranche's balance after mandatory repayment — so the sweep can never drive the tranche negative (c04).

Seniority ordering

When several tranches are sweepable, apply cash in order of seniority or contractual priority (senior secured term loans first, then subordinated/mezzanine), or in order of cost if voluntary. Each subsequent tranche receives only the cash remaining after earlier sweeps:

\[ \text{Remaining}_1 = \text{Cash for sweep} \times s \qquad \text{Sweep}_k = \min(\text{Remaining}_k,\ \text{Balance}_k) \qquad \text{Remaining}_{k+1} = \text{Remaining}_k - \text{Sweep}_k \]

NCDs are usually not sweepable: listed debentures are typically non-callable or callable only on call dates with a premium. Flag each tranche as sweepable (1/0) and multiply.

Worked illustration

Cash available after mandatory and dividends ₹420 crore; revolver opening ₹120 crore; sweep 75%.

Step ₹ crore
Revolver repayment (MIN of 120 and 420) 120
Cash for sweep 300
Sweep pool (75%) 225
Term loan A (senior), balance after mandatory 150 → sweep 150
Remaining 75
Term loan B (senior, second priority), balance after mandatory 400 → sweep 75
Remaining 0
NCDs (non-sweepable) 0
Cash retained above minimum (25% of 300) 75

Without the MIN cap on term loan A, the full 225 would be applied and term loan A would close at −₹75 crore.

Contractual excess cash flow (ECF) sweeps

Loan documents define "excess cash flow" precisely — usually EBITDA less taxes, capex, ΔNWC, interest and scheduled principal, sometimes less permitted dividends — and specify a sweep % that steps down with leverage (e.g., 75% above 3.0× net debt/EBITDA, 50% between 2.0–3.0×, 0% below 2.0×). Model the step-down with a lookup:

Sweep_Pct: =IF(Lev_Open>3,0.75,IF(Lev_Open>2,0.5,0))

Use opening leverage to avoid adding another circularity.

Voluntary prepayments

Where there is no contractual sweep, use a management-policy switch: sweep on/off, percentage, and which tranches. Consider:

  • Prepayment premium (deduct from cash, expense to P&L).
  • The opportunity cost: prepaying 9.5% debt vs earning 6.5% on surplus cash — rational to prepay unless cash is needed for capex or M&A.
  • Unamortised upfront fees on the prepaid portion are written off under Ind AS 109 EIR mechanics (accelerated finance cost).

Interaction with mandatory repayments

After a sweep, future scheduled instalments may exceed the remaining balance. The MIN(Opening, Scheduled) cap in the mandatory row (s02) prevents negative balances. If the contract re-amortises the remaining balance over the remaining instalments (pro-rata application), model mandatory repayment as Opening ÷ Remaining instalments instead.

Excel implementation

Cash_for_Sweep:  =MAX(0,Cash_Avail+MIN(0,-Rev_Change))   'after revolver repayment
Pool:            =Cash_for_Sweep*Sweep_Pct*Sweep_On
Sweep_TLA:       =MIN(Pool, TLA_Open+TLA_Draw-TLA_Mand)*TLA_Sweepable
Sweep_TLB:       =MIN(Pool-Sweep_TLA, TLB_Open+TLB_Draw-TLB_Mand)*TLB_Sweepable

Note that Cash_Avail+MIN(0,-Rev_Change) simply deducts the revolver repayment when the change is negative. Check rows: each tranche closing ≥ 0; total sweep ≤ pool; closing cash ≥ minimum.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Sweep before mandatory — wrong order: contractual instalments must be met first, otherwise cash is double-used.
  • Ignoring minimum cash — sweeping all cash leaves no operating buffer; minimum cash must be deducted in cash available.
  • Average-balance interest + sweep creates a strong circularity; sweep in a year reduces interest, which raises cash, which raises the sweep. Use the breaker.
  • Trapped cash in subsidiaries or restricted accounts should not be swept.
  • Sweep percentage in a downturn — ECF may be negative; the MAX(0, …) ensures no "negative sweep".
  • Equity cure rights or dividend restrictions often interact with sweep thresholds in acquisition finance.

Key takeaways

  • Sweep comes last: after mandatory repayment, dividends and revolver repayment, and after minimum cash.
  • Sweep per tranche = MIN(pool remaining, balance after mandatory) × sweepable flag.
  • Apply by seniority/priority; pass remaining cash down the stack; NCDs usually excluded.
  • Contractual ECF sweeps often step down with leverage — use opening leverage.
  • Cap later mandatory repayments at the post-sweep balance.

Practice Questions

Q1. Basic sweep computation [L1]

Cash available after mandatory repayments and dividends is ₹260 crore; opening revolver ₹40 crore; sweep 100%; one sweepable term loan with opening balance ₹500 crore and mandatory repayment ₹100 crore. Compute revolver repayment, sweep, closing term loan and closing cash above minimum.

Q2. Multi-tranche sweep by seniority [L2]

Sweep pool ₹600 crore (75% of excess cash). Tranches after mandatory repayment: senior term loan ₹250 crore, second senior term loan ₹180 crore, mezzanine loan ₹300 crore (sweepable only after senior debt is fully repaid), NCDs ₹500 crore (non-sweepable). Allocate the sweep and compute cash retained. Then repeat for a pool of ₹350 crore.

💡 Hint: Pass the remaining pool down the stack, capping each tranche at its balance.

Q3. Leverage-linked sweep step-down [L2]

An acquisition loan agreement requires a 75% ECF sweep when opening net debt/EBITDA exceeds 3.0×, 50% between 2.0× and 3.0×, and nil below 2.0×. Opening net debt is ₹2,400 crore, ₹1,900 crore and ₹1,350 crore for FY26–FY28, with EBITDA of ₹700, ₹760 and ₹820 crore and excess cash flow of ₹300, ₹340 and ₹380 crore. Compute the sweep each year and explain why opening rather than closing leverage is used.

Q4. Judgement: voluntary prepayment [L3]

A company has ₹800 crore surplus cash earning 6.75%, a prepayable term loan at 9.4% with a 1% prepayment premium and ₹6 crore of unamortised upfront fees, and plans a ₹1,000 crore acquisition in 18 months. Discuss whether to sweep, the P&L and cash effects of prepaying ₹500 crore, and how you would set up the model's sweep switch to evaluate the choice.
