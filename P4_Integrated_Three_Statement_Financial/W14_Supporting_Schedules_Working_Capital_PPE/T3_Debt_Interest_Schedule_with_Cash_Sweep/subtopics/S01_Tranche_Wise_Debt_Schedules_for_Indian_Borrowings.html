Subtopic note

Tranche-Wise Debt Schedules for Indian Borrowings

Scope: Tranche-wise schedules: term loans, NCDs, working-capital/CC limits, commercial paper

Why it matters

Indian corporate balance sheets rarely carry "debt" as one homogeneous line. A typical mid-cap manufacturer has rupee term loans from two or three banks, listed or privately placed non-convertible debentures (NCDs), a fund-based working-capital limit (cash credit, WCDL), perhaps commercial paper backed by unused bank lines, and sometimes external commercial borrowings. Each tranche has its own pricing (floating vs fixed), repayment profile (amortising vs bullet), seniority and security, and covenants. Modelling them separately lets the schedule produce accurate interest, the correct current/non-current split under Schedule III, a meaningful maturity wall for refinancing risk, and a credible cash sweep that respects seniority. Lenders, rating agencies and investment committees read debt that way; so should the model.

Core notes

Generic tranche corkscrew

Every tranche runs the same corkscrew (c01):

\[ \text{Closing}_t = \text{Opening}_t + \text{Drawdowns}_t - \text{Mandatory repayment}_t - \text{Optional prepayment/sweep}_t \]

with its own rows for interest rate, interest expense, fees and current portion. The schedule then sums tranches for total debt, total interest and the Balance Sheet split.

Term loans

  • Lenders: banks and financial institutions (SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis, Bank of Baroda, EXIM for export-linked capex), sometimes via consortium or multiple banking arrangements.
  • Pricing: floating, typically linked to the bank's MCLR (1-year or 6-month) or an external benchmark (repo, T-bill) plus a spread; reset periodically (s06).
  • Tenor and repayment: 5–10 years for corporate capex loans with a moratorium during construction (e.g., 12–24 months), then equal quarterly instalments or a ballooning schedule; project finance loans for infra/renewables 15–20 years with DSCR-sculpted repayments.
  • Security: first charge on fixed assets, second charge on current assets; promoter guarantees for smaller companies.
  • Prepayment: often permitted with or without a small premium (1–2%) — relevant for sweep assumptions.
  • Upfront/processing fees (0.25–1%) are amortised via the effective interest rate under Ind AS 109 (related concept).

Non-convertible debentures (NCDs)

  • Issued publicly or (more commonly) on a private placement basis and listed on NSE/BSE under the SEBI (Issue and Listing of Non-Convertible Securities) Regulations, 2021; rated by CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings, etc.
  • Pricing: usually fixed coupon (annual or semi-annual), sometimes floating; issue spreads over G-secs depend on rating.
  • Repayment: typically bullet at maturity (3–10 years), sometimes staggered redemption or put/call options.
  • Debenture redemption reserve: the 2019 amendment to Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 removed the DRR requirement for listed companies, while retaining an obligation to deposit or invest a percentage of debentures maturing in the year; check the current rule before modelling any DRR transfer in equity.
  • Under SEBI's large-corporate framework, listed entities meeting the size and rating thresholds must raise a minimum share of incremental borrowings through debt securities — relevant for refinancing assumptions for large issuers.

Working-capital limits (CC / OD / WCDL)

  • Cash credit (CC) or overdraft: revolving, drawn and repaid daily, interest on daily outstanding, limit subject to drawing power (inventory and receivables less margins, typically 25%), renewed annually.
  • WCDL: short tenors (7 days to 1 year) within the same sanctioned limit; under RBI's guidelines on the loan system for delivery of bank credit, large borrowers must take a minimum loan component within their working-capital limit.
  • Non-fund-based limits (letters of credit, bank guarantees) are not debt but consume limits and drive margin money; disclose separately.
  • In the model, CC/WCDL behaves as the revolver (s04): it draws when cash is short and repays first when cash is surplus, subject to the sanctioned limit and, optionally, a drawing-power cap:

\[ \text{Available limit}_t = \min\left( \text{Sanctioned limit},\ (1 - m) \times (\text{Inventory}_t + \text{Receivables}_t) - \text{Creditors}_t \right) \]

where \( m \) is the margin (some banks deduct creditors; definitions vary).

Commercial paper (CP)

  • Unsecured money-market instrument, 7 days to 1 year, issued at a discount, rated (typically A1+ for large corporates) under RBI's directions on CP; usually supported by bank lines so that rollover risk is covered.
  • Modelling: treat as a short-term tranche rolled over each year at a CP rate (often below MCLR), with a toggle to replace it with bank funding in a stressed scenario. CP interest is the discount accreted over the tenor.

Other tranches

External commercial borrowings (ECBs, FX-denominated; add hedging cost or FX translation), inter-corporate deposits, promoter loans, and supplier finance treated as debt (t1 s07). Lease liabilities run a separate corkscrew (week cross-cutting item) — never lump them in here.

Layout

Row block Contents
Inputs per tranche Opening balance, rate type and spread, drawdown schedule, repayment profile, maturity, seniority rank, prepayable flag, fee %
Corkscrew per tranche Opening, + draws, − mandatory, − sweep, closing
Interest per tranche Rate, interest (opening or average, with breaker, t4), fees
Totals Total debt, total interest, current portion (next year's mandatory + CP + CC), non-current
Checks Each closing ≥ 0; revolver ≤ limit; maturity ≤ last scheduled repayment

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Moratoriums and step-up schedules — model repayment as a percentage table by year rather than "balance ÷ remaining years".
  • Callable or puttable NCDs — a put date before maturity is the effective maturity for liquidity analysis.
  • Ind AS 109 carrying amount vs face value — the Balance Sheet shows amortised cost net of unamortised fees; the model can carry face value with a separate unamortised-fee line, or carry amortised cost. Be consistent with interest computation.
  • CP without backup lines in a stressed market (as in 2018–19 after the IL&FS default) cannot be assumed to roll; include a rollover-failure scenario.
  • Drawing-power constraints bind in downturns exactly when the revolver is needed, as inventory and receivables shrink.
  • Inter-company debt eliminates on consolidation; model at the consolidated level unless doing standalone covenant tests.

Key takeaways

  • Model each tranche separately with its own corkscrew, pricing, repayment and seniority.
  • Term loans amortise and float on MCLR/EBLR; NCDs are usually fixed-coupon bullets; CC/WCDL is the revolver; CP is a rolled short-term tranche.
  • Working-capital limits are capped by sanctioned limit and drawing power.
  • Lease liabilities and non-fund-based limits stay out of the bank-debt schedule.
  • Build checks for non-negative balances, limit compliance and maturities.

Practice Questions

Q1. Set up a tranche register [L1]

From the following, prepare a tranche input table (balance, rate basis, repayment, maturity, seniority): ₹750 crore term loan from a consortium at 1Y MCLR + 0.50%, 20 equal quarterly instalments starting June 2025; ₹500 crore listed NCDs at 7.95% maturing March 2028 (bullet); CC limit ₹400 crore, ₹120 crore drawn; ₹300 crore CP rolled quarterly at 7.4%.

Q2. Drawing-power cap [L2]

A company's sanctioned CC limit is ₹600 crore. Forecast FY27 inventory ₹520 crore, receivables ₹380 crore and trade creditors ₹290 crore; the bank applies a 25% margin on inventory and receivables and deducts creditors. Compute the drawing power and the effective available limit. If the model's revolver wants to draw ₹500 crore, what happens and how should the schedule respond?

💡 Hint: The effective limit is the lower of sanction and drawing power.

Q3. Commercial paper rollover stress [L2]

Explain how you would model a ₹1,000 crore CP programme in a base case and in a stress case where CP markets freeze for a year, given an undrawn backup bank line of ₹800 crore at MCLR + 1.0% and minimum cash of ₹200 crore. Describe the effect on interest, the revolver and covenants.

Q4. Interview: why not one debt line? [L1]

An interviewer asks why you build tranche-wise debt instead of one "total borrowings" line with an average interest rate. Give four reasons, each linked to a model output.
