Roadmap topic: Debt & Interest Schedule with Cash Sweep: Opening Debt + Drawdowns – Mandatory Amortization – Optional Cash Sweep = Closing Debt; modeling Revolver credit line for cash deficits

Overview

The debt schedule is the financing engine of the three-statement model. It takes the cash generated or consumed by operations and investment (from the working-capital and PPE schedules and the CFS), applies it through a cash waterfall in strict priority — mandatory debt service, dividends, revolver, sweep — and produces closing balances for each tranche, interest expense and income, the CFF lines, the current/non-current split of borrowings and the covenant ratios lenders test. Without it, cash is a plug and leverage is static; with it, the model behaves like a corporate treasury and can answer questions about funding gaps, deleveraging speed, refinancing risk and covenant headroom.

For FP&A, treasury, credit and deal roles in India, the schedule must reflect local instruments and practice: MCLR-, EBLR- and repo-linked bank term loans, fixed-coupon listed NCDs with bullet maturities, cash credit and WCDL limits governed by drawing power, commercial paper rolled under bank backup lines, and lender covenants such as DSCR, interest coverage, Net debt/EBITDA, TOL/TNW and current ratio. Classification follows Ind AS 1 (including the Indian carve-out on post-balance-sheet waivers) and Schedule III; measurement follows Ind AS 109 amortised cost and EIR; and lease liabilities under Ind AS 116 run as a separate corkscrew.

The learning sequence: set up the tranche register and corkscrews (s01); model mandatory amortisation, bullets and capex-linked draws (s02); build the cash waterfall to compute cash available (s03); add the revolver for deficits and first-call repayment of surpluses (s04); then the optional or contractual sweep by seniority (s05); compute interest on floating and fixed tranches and income on cash (s06); and finally covenants and classification (s07). The key concepts capture the corkscrew identity (c01), cash available (c02), the single-cell revolver (c03), the capped sweep (c04), the opening vs average interest choice that creates circularity (c05) and the Indian DSCR/ICR definitions (c06).

Related concepts to study

Interest circularity

→ Week 14 (w14_t4): average-balance interest creates the circularity

Average-balance interest on the revolver and sweepable tranches makes interest depend on closing debt, which depends on cash available, which depends on interest. The next topic this week deals with the loop: Excel iterative calculation settings, a circuit-breaker toggle that switches interest to opening balance or zero, materiality of the difference (≈ Rate × ΔDebt ÷ 2), convergence checks and the recovery procedure when an error gets trapped in the loop. Build the debt schedule with a named Circ switch from the start so that the breaker is a one-cell change, and test every revolver and sweep formula with the breaker both on and off.

EIR, upfront fees and lease liabilities

→ Phase 1 (W1–4): Ind AS 109 EIR and upfront fee amortisation; Ind AS 116 lease liability as separate corkscrew

Under Ind AS 109, borrowings are measured at amortised cost: upfront processing fees and transaction costs are deducted from the initial carrying amount and amortised through the effective interest rate, so P&L finance cost exceeds the cash coupon and the carrying amount accretes to face value. On prepayment via a sweep, unamortised fees are written off. Lease liabilities under Ind AS 116 are financial liabilities too, but they have their own discount rate (IBR), payment profile and new-lease additions driven by store openings; they belong in a separate corkscrew (Opening + New leases + Interest − Lease payments = Closing), excluded from bank-debt covenants unless the agreement includes them.

LBO and acquisition financing

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): LBO and acquisition financing structures

The same mechanics scale up in leveraged buyouts and acquisition finance: multiple senior and subordinated tranches, a revolver, mandatory amortisation on term loan A, minimal amortisation on term loan B, contractual excess-cash-flow sweeps that step down with leverage, and covenants that tighten over time. In India, acquisition finance is constrained by RBI rules on bank lending for share acquisitions (historically restrictive, with recent liberalisation proposals — verify the current position) and is often raised through NBFCs, private credit funds, NCDs or offshore structures. Sponsor returns are highly sensitive to sweep speed, so the s05 logic becomes central in Phase 6.

Credit ratings and rating-linked covenants

Credit ratings (CRISIL/ICRA/CARE) and rating-linked covenant thresholds

Indian rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings, Acuité) rate bank loans, NCDs and CP, and their rationales disclose the metrics that drive ratings: Net debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, DSCR, TOL/TNW, liquidity cushion and refinancing risk. Loan agreements and NCD terms often include rating triggers — a spread step-up on downgrade, a put option or acceleration below a threshold (e.g., below A−), or mandatory prepayment. Build a rating-sensitivity scenario: map forecast ratios to an implied rating band and link the spread on floating tranches, and refinancing rates on maturing NCDs, to that band. SEBI LODR requires disclosure of rating changes by listed issuers.

Common pitfalls

Sweep before mandatory repayment or ignoring minimum cash

Sweeping before mandatory repayments or ignoring minimum cash

The mistake: computing the sweep from cash available before deducting scheduled repayments, or from total cash without deducting the minimum operating balance. The same cash is then used twice (sweep and mandatory), or the company is left with zero operating cash. It happens when the waterfall is built bottom-up or the minimum-cash row is added late. Correct treatment: follow the priority CFO → capex → mandatory debt → dividends → revolver → sweep, and subtract minimum cash inside cash available. Detect it by checking that closing cash ≥ minimum every year, that total uses in the waterfall equal total sources, and that the waterfall closing cash equals CFS and Balance Sheet cash.

Revolver over the limit or never repaid

Revolver draws exceeding the limit or never repaid when cash turns positive

The mistake: a revolver formula that draws the full deficit without the headroom cap, or that only draws and never repays (e.g., =MAX(0,-Cash_Avail) added to the prior balance). The model then shows impossible borrowings or carries expensive revolver debt alongside surplus cash. It happens because modellers write the draw logic first and forget the surplus case, or use the wrong sign. Correct treatment: =MIN(MAX(-Rev_Open,-Cash_Avail),Rev_Limit-Rev_Open) with an effective limit (sanction vs drawing power). Detect it with checks 0 ≤ Rev_Close ≤ Limit, a flag when cash exceeds minimum while the revolver is drawn, and by testing years of strong surplus.

Sweep driving a tranche negative

Sweep driving a tranche below zero because MIN cap is missing

The mistake: sweep = excess cash × sweep % with no cap at the tranche balance. In high-cash years the tranche goes negative — appearing as a negative borrowing (in effect, an asset) — and average-balance interest turns into phantom interest income. It happens because sweeps are tested only in early, cash-tight years. Correct treatment: MIN(pool, Opening − Mandatory) per tranche, pass any remainder to the next tranche in seniority order, and cap later scheduled repayments at the remaining balance. Detect it with a non-negative balance check on every tranche, by stress-testing a high-cash scenario, and by scanning the final forecast years.

Leases lumped into bank debt

Lease liabilities lumped into bank debt at a bank interest rate

The mistake: adding Ind AS 116 lease liabilities to borrowings and computing interest at the bank rate, or letting the revolver and sweep act on lease liabilities. Leases have their own discount rates (IBR at inception), payment schedules and new-lease additions driven by store openings; they cannot be prepaid from a cash sweep; and most bank covenants exclude them. Lumping misstates finance cost, the CFF split between lease principal and interest, covenant ratios and net debt definitions. Correct treatment: a separate lease corkscrew (Opening + New leases + Interest − Lease payments = Closing) linked to the ROU schedule, with lease payments as a committed use in the cash waterfall. Detect it by reconciling finance cost to the notes (interest on borrowings vs on lease liabilities) and checking covenant definitions.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full debt schedule with revolver and sweep [L3]

A specialty chemicals company at 31 March 2025 (₹ crore): cash 180; minimum cash 150; term loan A 600 (16 equal quarterly instalments from Q1 FY26; 1Y MCLR + 0.40%); term loan B undrawn sanction 500 (70% of capex, drawn with spend FY26–FY27, repay 12 equal quarterly instalments from FY29; repo + 2.25%); NCD 400 at 8.10% fixed, bullet in FY28 (refinanced 50% with a new NCD at 8.50%, rest from cash/revolver); CC revolver opening 150, limit 500 (MCLR + 0.75%, commitment fee 0.30% on undrawn); interest income on cash 6.25%. Forecast MCLR 8.85% and repo 5.75% flat. Pre-financing cash flow before interest (CFO before interest + CFI): FY26 −60, FY27 120, FY28 380, FY29 520, FY30 600; capex FY26 400, FY27 350 (already inside CFI); dividends 80 a year rising 10%. Sweep 75% of cash after revolver to term loan A then term loan B. (a) Build the waterfall, tranche corkscrews, revolver and sweep for FY26–FY30 using opening-balance interest. (b) Recompute FY27 with average-balance interest and quantify the difference. (c) Identify any funding gap and its cause.

💡 Hint: Interest paid must be deducted in the waterfall because the pre-financing figure is before interest.

Q2. Covenant test and classification [L2]

For the company in Q1, FY28 forecast: EBITDA ₹780 crore, depreciation ₹220 crore, PAT ₹300 crore, interest on term debt ₹85 crore, total finance cost ₹115 crore. Covenants: Net debt/EBITDA ≤ 2.75×, DSCR ≥ 1.25× (PAT-based, excluding refinanced portion of the bullet), ICR (EBIT) ≥ 3.0×. Using your FY28 closing balances from Q1, compute the ratios and headroom. If DSCR breaches and the bank's waiver arrives on 30 April 2028, explain the 31 March 2028 balance-sheet classification under Ind AS 1 as notified.

Q3. Retailer with leases and a CC limit [L2]

Vastra Retail has bank term debt ₹300 crore (₹100 crore a year amortisation, 9.2%), a CC limit of ₹400 crore (drawing power 75% of inventory plus receivables less creditors), lease liabilities ₹3,000 crore (IBR 9%, payments ₹720 crore a year, new leases ₹860 crore a year) and cash ₹380 crore. FY26 pre-financing cash flow before interest and lease payments is ₹1,050 crore; dividends ₹150 crore; minimum cash ₹250 crore. Inventory ₹1,722 crore, receivables ₹592 crore, creditors ₹2,231 crore. (a) Build the lease corkscrew. (b) Build the waterfall and revolver, testing drawing power. (c) Compute Net debt/EBITDA with and without leases if post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA is ₹2,050 crore.

Q4. Review of a debt tab [L3]

A model you are reviewing has: sweep computed before mandatory repayments; revolver formula =MAX(0,-Cash_Avail) added to the opening balance; no minimum cash; all interest on average balances with iterative calculation permanently on and no breaker; lease liabilities included in "Term loans" at 9.5%; and NCD bullet maturities modelled as equal annual repayments. Write a structured review memo listing each defect, its consequence for the financial statements and covenants, and the corrected design.
