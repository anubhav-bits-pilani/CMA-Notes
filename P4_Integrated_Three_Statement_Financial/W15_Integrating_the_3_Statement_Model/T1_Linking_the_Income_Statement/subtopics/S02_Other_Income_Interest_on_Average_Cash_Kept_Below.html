Subtopic note

Other Income: Interest on Average Cash Kept Below EBITDA

Scope: Other income: interest on average cash/investments, kept below EBITDA

Why it matters

Many Indian listed companies carry large treasury balances — IT services, FMCG, pharma and consumer durables majors routinely hold ₹5,000–50,000 crore of cash, bank deposits, liquid mutual funds and bonds. The income on that treasury is reported in "Other income" on the Schedule III P&L and can be 10–25% of PBT. Two modelling decisions matter. First, how to forecast it: as a yield on the average treasury balance, which creates a circular link with cash. Second, where to show it: below EBITDA, so that operating margins are not flattered by treasury yield and so that valuation can treat cash separately in the EV bridge. Get either wrong and the model double-counts cash value in a DCF or presents an EBITDA margin that no broker or credit analyst would recognise.

Core notes

What sits in Other income

Schedule III Division II requires "Other income" as a separate line after Revenue from operations, with note disclosure of:

  • Interest income (on bank deposits, loans given, bonds, income-tax refunds), with financial assets at amortised cost shown separately.
  • Dividend income (from equity investments; dividends from subsidiaries eliminate on consolidation).
  • Net gain on sale/fair-valuation of investments (liquid and debt mutual funds at FVTPL — these are the dominant treasury product for Indian corporates since the 2023 removal of indexation for debt funds reduced their tax edge but not their liquidity appeal).
  • Other non-operating income: rental income on investment property, profit on sale of PPE (net), foreign-exchange gains (where not operating), liabilities written back.

For forecasting, split the historical other income into treasury-linked items (interest, dividends, MF gains — driven by the cash/investment balance) and other items (rental, scrap of non-operating nature, one-offs). Treasury-linked items are driven by a yield; recurring other items by a simple growth or hold-flat assumption; one-offs (profit on sale of land, write-backs) are set to zero.

Yield on average balance

\[ \text{Interest income}_t = r_t \times \frac{\text{Cash}_{t-1} + \text{Cash}_t}{2} + y_t \times \frac{\text{Inv}_{t-1} + \text{Inv}_t}{2} \]

where \( r_t \) is the pre-tax yield on cash and bank deposits, \( y_t \) the pre-tax yield on current/non-current investments, and \( \text{Cash} \), \( \text{Inv} \) the BS closing balances. Typical Indian yields (FY26 context): 6–7% on bank FDs and liquid funds, 7–7.5% on high-grade bonds; check the RBI repo rate trajectory and the company's own disclosed yield (other income ÷ average treasury). Calibrate: if FY26A interest + MF gains were ₹420 crore on an average treasury of ₹6,000 crore, the implied yield is 7.0%.

Using the average balance is the economically correct choice because cash accumulates through the year. It also creates a circular reference: interest income → PBT → PAT → CFO → closing cash → average cash → interest income. Week 14 (Managing Interest Circularity Safely) set up the circuit breaker; here the P&L simply links the result from the Debt/Treasury schedule:

Debt!F60  =IF(Breaker=1,0,Inputs!F30*AVERAGE(BS!E12,BS!F12))   ' interest on cash
Debt!F61  =Inputs!F31*AVERAGE(BS!E14:F14)                         ' yield on investments (if not held flat)
IS!F20    =Debt!F60+Debt!F61+Costs!F50                            ' Other income = treasury + other recurring

When the breaker is on (=1), interest income is zero (or switched to opening-balance interest), the loop is cut, and errors can be flushed. Alternative non-circular choice: interest on opening cash only — acceptable, simpler, slightly understates income for cash-generating companies; disclose the choice on the assumptions sheet.

Why below EBITDA

The Schedule III face does not show EBITDA, but every Indian broker, rating agency (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE) and management presentation computes it. Practice is to compute EBITDA excluding other income because:

  1. Operating performance. Treasury yield is a function of capital structure and policy, not of operations; including it would let a cash-rich company show higher margins simply by not paying dividends.
  2. Valuation consistency. In EV/EBITDA and DCF, cash and investments are added in the EV-to-equity bridge. Including their income in EBITDA would count the same value twice.
  3. Peer comparability. A leveraged peer's interest cost sits below EBITDA; symmetric treatment of the treasury peer's income keeps margins comparable.

The model therefore runs: EBITDA → less D&A → EBIT → plus Other income → less Finance costs → PBT. Some companies (and SEBI-format quarterly results) present "Total income = Revenue + Other income" at the top; the model can show that memo, but EBITDA must be defined ex-other income on the sheet (c05).

Cash flow consequences

Interest income is part of PAT, but under Ind AS 7 (for non-financial entities) interest received and dividends received are investing cash flows (Ind AS 7.33 permits operating or investing; Indian practice for non-financial entities is investing). In a PBT-start Indian CFS, interest income is deducted in the CFO reconciliation and the cash received is shown in CFI. If the model assumes all interest income is received in cash in the year (no accrued interest receivable movement), CFI interest received = P&L interest income. Fair-value gains on FVTPL mutual funds are non-cash until redemption: they increase the investment balance, so they are deducted in CFO and appear nowhere in CFI until the units are sold.

Worked illustration

Opening cash ₹1,200 crore; closing cash ₹1,800 crore (from the CFS, after convergence); current investments held flat at ₹2,000 crore; yield on cash 6.5%, on investments 7.2%; other recurring income ₹15 crore.

\[ \text{Other income} = 6.5\% \times 1{,}500 + 7.2\% \times 2{,}000 + 15 = 97.5 + 144 + 15 = 256.5 \]

If investments are held flat, the ₹144 crore yield must be either cash interest (received, CFI inflow) or MF fair-value gain (non-cash, increases investments — in which case investments cannot be held flat). Decide which, and make the BS and CFS consistent with that choice.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Negative or near-zero cash with a revolver: interest income should be on positive cash only; the revolver bears interest in finance costs. Use MAX(0, …) on average cash.
  • Minimum cash held in restricted accounts (other bank balances, margin money, unpaid dividend accounts) earns interest but is not "cash and cash equivalents" under Ind AS 7; it may still be included in the treasury base for income.
  • Income-tax refund interest (Sec 244A) is lumpy and should not be forecast.
  • Holding investments flat while forecasting MF gains creates a BS/CFS mismatch — a classic imbalance equal to the gain.
  • Treasury in subsidiaries with NCI: consolidated other income includes the NCI's share; the NCI line picks it up through PAT allocation.
  • Tax rate on treasury income is the same corporate rate under Sec 115BAA (25.168%); MF gains are taxed on redemption, creating a small DTL on unrealised FVTPL gains.

Key takeaways

  • Split historical other income into treasury-linked, recurring other and one-off; forecast each differently.
  • Interest income = yield × average treasury balance; this creates a circularity that runs through the Week 14 breaker.
  • Keep other income below EBITDA (EBIT + Other income − Finance costs = PBT) for operating and valuation consistency.
  • Interest and dividends received are CFI inflows for non-financial entities; FVTPL gains are non-cash until redemption.
  • Every treasury yield assumption must agree with how the investment balance moves on the BS.

Practice Questions

Q1. Calibrate a treasury yield [L1]

An IT services company reports FY26A other income of ₹3,100 crore, of which ₹2,650 crore is interest and MF gains and ₹450 crore is forex gains on hedges and miscellaneous items. Cash and bank balances plus current investments were ₹38,000 crore at 31 March 2025 and ₹42,000 crore at 31 March 2026. Compute the implied treasury yield and state which part of other income you would forecast at zero.

Q2. Circular vs opening-balance interest [L2]

Opening cash is ₹500 crore. Pre-interest CFO + CFI + CFF for FY27E is ₹+300 crore, the yield on cash is 7% and t = 25.168%. Assume all interest is received in cash in the year and there are no other cash effects of interest income. Set up the equation for closing cash using average-balance interest, solve for interest income algebraically, and compare with the opening-balance method.

💡 Hint: Closing cash = 800 + interest × (1 − t); interest = 7% × average of 500 and closing cash.

Q3. EBITDA definition dispute [L2]

A management presentation shows FY26A "EBITDA" of ₹1,940 crore including other income of ₹310 crore; a broker shows ₹1,630 crore. Revenue is ₹9,800 crore. Compute both margins, explain which one your model should use, and how you would label the sheet so the dashboard is not misread.

Q4. MF gains and the BS check [L3]

Your model forecasts other income of ₹180 crore, of which ₹120 crore is fair-value gain on liquid mutual funds held at FVTPL that are not redeemed. Current investments are held flat at ₹1,500 crore on the BS, and the CFS treats all other income as received in cash (CFI). Predict the size and direction of the balance-check difference, explain why, and list two alternative consistent treatments.
