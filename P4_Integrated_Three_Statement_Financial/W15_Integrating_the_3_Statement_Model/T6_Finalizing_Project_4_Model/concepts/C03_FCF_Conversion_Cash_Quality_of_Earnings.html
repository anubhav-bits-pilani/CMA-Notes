Key concept

FCF Conversion: Cash Quality of Earnings

Concept: FCF conversion = FCF ÷ PAT (or ÷ EBITDA)

Statement

\[ \text{FCF conversion}_{\text{PAT}} = \frac{\text{FCF}}{\text{PAT}} \qquad \text{FCF conversion}_{\text{EBITDA}} = \frac{\text{FCF}}{\text{EBITDA}} \]

where a common practitioner definition of free cash flow is:

\[ \text{FCF} = \text{CFO} - \text{Capex (incl. CWIP and intangibles)} - \text{Lease payments (principal + interest)} \]

CFO is cash flow from operating activities after tax paid (Ind AS 7). Capex is purchases of PPE and intangibles net of disposal proceeds (state whether net or gross). Lease payments are deducted so that post-Ind AS 116 FCF is comparable with pre-Ind AS 116 history, where rent sat within CFO. PAT and EBITDA are as defined on the dashboard.

Explanation

Why it matters: profit is an accounting measure, while FCF is what is available to repay debt, pay dividends or reinvest. Conversion shows how much of each rupee of profit becomes free cash. Consistently high conversion (around 80–100% of PAT for asset-light businesses) signals earnings quality. Persistently low conversion signals working capital absorption, heavy capex or aggressive revenue recognition, which is a classic Phase 2 earnings-quality flag.

Which denominator:

  • FCF ÷ PAT is used by equity investors. It compares cash with the profit attributable to shareholders. Since FCF here is after interest (interest paid may sit in CFF for Indian non-financial companies under Ind AS 7.33, so adjust FCF for interest paid if you want an "equity" FCF), state the treatment.
  • FCF ÷ EBITDA (sometimes called cash conversion) is used by credit analysts and PE investors. It shows how much operating profit survives tax, capex and working capital. Typical ranges are 30–60% for capital-intensive and 60–90% for asset-light businesses.

Definitional pitfalls:

  • Interest classification. Indian companies usually show interest paid in CFF. Then CFO − capex is effectively a pre-interest (FCFF-like) number. If the denominator is PAT (post-interest), deduct interest paid for consistency, or note the mismatch.
  • Lease payments. Under Ind AS 116 both principal and interest are typically in CFF (Ind AS 7.17(e) for principal; interest per the entity's policy), so CFO rises mechanically compared with pre-2019 periods. Deduct lease payments to keep the ratio comparable.
  • Acquisitions. Usually excluded from capex and shown separately.
  • Growth capex vs maintenance capex. A company in a capex cycle has low conversion by design. Show conversion on maintenance capex as well (if estimable), or explain on the dashboard.
  • Loss years and near-zero PAT. The ratio becomes negative or explodes. Show "n.m."

Interpretation in forecasts: forecast conversion well above 100% for several years usually means capex is below depreciation (the asset base is shrinking) or working capital releases are not sustainable. That is a sanity-band flag (c05).

Illustration

Bhima Foods Ltd (₹ crore):

Item FY26E FY27E FY28E
PAT 300 340 380
EBITDA 560 620 690
CFO (after tax, interest in CFF) 470 450 590
Capex (190) (260) (200)
Lease payments (30) (32) (34)
FCF 250 158 356
FCF ÷ PAT 83% 46% 94%
FCF ÷ EBITDA 45% 25% 52%

FY27E dips because capex rises (a new line) and working capital absorbs cash (CFO falls despite higher EBITDA). The dashboard commentary should say so. If FY28E conversion instead showed 140%, the reviewer would look for an unwinding of working capital or capex falling below D&A.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute conversion [L1]

Kosi Textiles Ltd FY27E (₹ crore): PAT 210, EBITDA 420, CFO 330 (interest paid of 45 shown in CFF), capex 160, disposal proceeds 10, lease payments 25. Compute FCF (state whether capex is net of disposals), FCF ÷ PAT and FCF ÷ EBITDA. Then compute an "equity-consistent" FCF that deducts interest paid, and its conversion on PAT.

Q2. Interpreting a trend [L2]

A forecast shows FCF ÷ PAT of 95%, 110%, 128%, 135% and 142% for FY26E–FY30E, with capex/D&A falling from 1.4x to 0.8x and receivable days falling from 60 to 42. Explain what drives the trend, whether it is plausible for a growing manufacturer, and which assumptions you would challenge.

💡 Hint: Compare capex with depreciation and look at the direction of working capital.

Q3. Ind AS 116 comparability [L2]

A retailer's FY19 (pre-Ind AS 116) FCF ÷ EBITDA was 40%. Its FY26E ratio, computed as (CFO − capex) ÷ EBITDA, is 62%. Rent of ₹180 crore is now replaced by lease payments of ₹185 crore in CFF, and EBITDA rises by ₹180 crore. Explain why the two ratios are not comparable, and restate the FY26E ratio on a comparable basis, given FY26E post-Ind AS 116 EBITDA of ₹900 crore.
