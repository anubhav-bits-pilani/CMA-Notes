Subtopic note

Reading the Pattern of the Balance Check Across Years

Scope: Pattern reading: constant, growing, or alternating difference across years

Why it matters

The balance check row is a time series, and its shape is diagnostic evidence. A difference that appears once and then stays flat is a different kind of error from one that grows every year or flips sign. Before touching any cell, reading the pattern tells you whether you are looking for an opening-balance break, a recurring flow omission, a driver-scaled error or a circularity/timing problem. In practice this shortlists the candidates from about 60 BS and CFS lines to perhaps five, which is how experienced reviewers find errors in minutes. In interviews, saying "the difference grows by a constant amount every year, so I'm looking for a recurring flow of that amount that is missing from the CFS" shows you understand that the BS is a stock and the CFS is a flow.

Core notes

The key idea: the check is a stock, its first difference is a flow

The BS check in year \( t \) is the cumulative effect of every unmatched movement up to and including year \( t \):

\[ \text{Check}_t = \text{Check}_{t-1} + e_t \]

where \( e_t \) is the net error generated by that year's flows. So the year-on-year change in the check row isolates the error created in each year:

\[ e_t = \text{Check}_t - \text{Check}_{t-1} \]

Always add a helper row "Δ check" under the check row. Most patterns are easier to read on the Δ row.

Pattern 1: Constant difference

FY26E FY27E FY28E FY29E FY30E
Check 14.00 14.00 14.00 14.00 14.00
Δ check 14.00 0 0 0 0

A single error of 14.00 arises in the first year and never recurs. Likely causes:

  • An opening balance on a schedule not linked to FY25A closing (for example, the opening term loan typed as 500 while FY25A closing is 514).
  • CFS opening cash ≠ FY25A BS cash.
  • A one-off flow in FY26E only (a one-time capex, a single equity issue, a one-off lease addition) missing from or mis-signed in the CFS.
  • A historical error carried forward. Check that FY25A balances (s01).

If the constant difference starts in a later year (0, 0, 14, 14, 14), the error is a one-off flow in that later year, for example a scheduled bullet repayment or a CWIP capitalisation in FY28E.

Pattern 2: Growing by a constant increment (linear)

FY26E FY27E FY28E FY29E FY30E
Check 6.00 12.00 18.00 24.00 30.00
Δ check 6.00 6.00 6.00 6.00 6.00

A flow of 6.00 is missing or mis-signed every year, and the item itself is flat. Candidates are fixed annual flows: a constant ESOP charge, straight-line amortisation of an intangible added back on the P&L but not deducted on the BS, a fixed annual term-loan instalment, a constant dividend, or a fixed lease principal.

Pattern 3: Growing at an increasing rate (driver-scaled)

FY26E FY27E FY28E FY29E FY30E
Check 4.00 8.40 13.24 18.56 24.42
Δ check 4.00 4.40 4.84 5.32 5.86

The annual error grows at 10%. That points to an item that scales with a driver growing at 10% (revenue, COGS or capex). Look at working-capital movements, depreciation on a growing asset base, ΔDTL on growing timing differences, or tax. Divide the Δ check by the driver to find the ratio. For example, if Δ check ÷ revenue is a constant 0.33%, search for an item that is 0.33% of revenue.

Pattern 4: Alternating or oscillating difference

FY26E FY27E FY28E FY29E FY30E
Check 5.00 −3.00 7.00 −2.00 6.00

Sign flips have three common causes:

  1. Circularity not converged. Interest on average balances with iteration on. The numbers usually also change on F9. Switch the breaker off first (s05).
  2. Sign error on a movement that changes direction. If receivables rise in some years and fall in others, a ΔNWC line with the wrong sign produces errors of \( 2 \times \Delta \) that flip with the direction of the movement. Compare Δ check with \( 2 \times \Delta \) of each WC line.
  3. Timing mismatch. The CFS uses this year's movement while the BS uses last year's (or the CFS uses the change computed against the wrong column). The error equals the difference between consecutive movements, which can flip sign.

Pattern 5: Error that appears only in a scenario

The model balances in Base but not in Bear. Many links work because a value is zero in Base: revolver draws, tax losses, dividend floors, negative-cash interest. If the pattern is clean only in Base, look at the logic that activates only under stress (see Topic 5).

Combining pattern with size

Pattern reading gives the type of error. Size (s03) gives the candidate line. Use both together. A Δ check of 6.00 every year, combined with an ESOP expense of exactly 6.00 in the P&L, is a strong hypothesis. A Δ check of 6.00 combined with a constant lease principal repayment of 3.00 points to a sign error on the lease principal (2 × 3.00).

Illustration: decomposing a mixed pattern

Krishna Cement Ltd check row: 2.50, 9.30, 16.10, 22.90, 29.70. Δ check: 2.50, 6.80, 6.80, 6.80, 6.80. That is a recurring error of 6.80 starting in FY27E, plus a one-off 2.50 in FY26E (2.50 is not 6.80). Possible story: FY26E has a one-off opening-link error of 2.50 and a recurring 6.80 error that starts in FY27E. Or the recurring item began in FY26E at a partial-year amount (for example, a new loan's interest for part of the year). Check whether any FY26E-start item has a 2.50 first-year amount and 6.80 full-year amount.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Two errors with opposite signs can produce a check that looks clean in some years. For example, +6 recurring and −6 one-off in FY28E gives 6, 12, 12, 18, 24. When the Δ row has an odd value, suspect a second error.
  • Errors that net to zero in one scenario. A ΔDTL omission of +4 and an ESOP omission of −4 cancel in Base. Testing other scenarios (Topic 5) breaks the coincidence.
  • Large constant error equal to opening cash. If CFS opening cash is blank in FY26E, the check equals minus the opening cash in every year, a clear constant pattern.
  • Exponential divergence. A check that doubles every year (2, 4, 8, 16) usually means a schedule references its own prior-year error. For example, interest on cash where cash includes the error, or a corkscrew opening that references closing plus a flow twice.
  • Do not over-interpret decimals in a circular model. Fix circularity first. Unconverged iteration produces noise in the third decimal place that looks like a pattern but is not one.

Key takeaways

  • Add a Δ check row. The check is a stock, and its first difference shows the error created in each year.
  • Constant check → one-off or opening-balance error. Linear growth → recurring flat flow error. Accelerating growth → driver-scaled item.
  • Alternating signs → circularity, a sign error on a line that changes direction, or a timing/column mismatch.
  • Errors that appear only in some scenarios sit in logic that is dormant in Base (revolver, losses, floors).
  • Combine pattern (type of error) with size (s03) to get a short list of candidates before opening any schedule.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify five check rows [L1]

For each check row (FY26E to FY30E, ₹ crore), state the pattern type and the class of error you would look for. (a) 0, 0, 25, 25, 25. (b) 3.6, 7.2, 10.8, 14.4, 18.0. (c) 1.0, 2.1, 3.3, 4.6, 6.1. (d) −4.2, 3.8, −5.0, 4.4, −3.6. (e) −182, −182, −182, −182, −182 where FY25A BS cash is ₹182 crore.

Q2. Driver-scaled error [L2]

Revenue for Tapti Electricals Ltd is ₹2,000, ₹2,300, ₹2,645, ₹3,042 and ₹3,498 crore for FY26E–FY30E. The check row is 0, 36.99, 79.52, 128.47, 184.68. (a) Compute the Δ check row. (b) Test whether Δ check is a constant percentage of revenue or of the change in revenue. (c) Convert the ratio you find into days on a 365-day year. (d) Name the working-capital item and the error type consistent with your finding if trade receivables are forecast at 45 days of revenue on a 365-day year.

💡 Hint: Compare Δ check with Δ revenue, not with revenue itself.

Q3. Mixed pattern decomposition [L2]

The check row for Sabarmati Chemicals Ltd is 4.0, 4.0, 11.5, 19.0, 26.5. A new 7-year term loan of ₹52.5 crore is drawn on 1 April FY28E with equal annual principal repayments starting in FY28E. (a) Decompose the row into a one-off component and a recurring component, stating the start year of each. (b) Propose a specific hypothesis for each component and the cell you would inspect to confirm it.

Q4. Interview: why does my check flip sign? [L3]

An interviewer says: "My check row reads 2.4, −1.8, 3.1, −0.9 and the numbers change slightly every time I press F9. Talk me through your approach." Structure a 2-minute verbal answer covering the causes of oscillation, the order of tests, and how you would prove you had fixed the root cause rather than the symptom.
