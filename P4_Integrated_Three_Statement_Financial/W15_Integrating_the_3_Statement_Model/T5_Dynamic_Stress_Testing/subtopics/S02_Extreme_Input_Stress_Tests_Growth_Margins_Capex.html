Subtopic note

Extreme Input Stress Tests: Growth, Margins, Capex and WC Days

Scope: Extreme inputs: zero/negative growth, margin collapse, 3× capex, DSO/DIO shocks

Why it matters

Base, Bull and Bear are designed to be plausible, so they rarely push the model into its corners. Extreme inputs are deliberately implausible: revenue growth of −40%, EBITDA margin of −5%, capex at three times plan, DSO up by 60 days. Their purpose is not to forecast. They prove that every formula behaves sensibly at the boundaries. If the model balances and gives logically valid outputs (no negative inventory, no negative tax, no depreciation beyond cost, no division errors) under extreme shocks, you can trust it under any realistic input a user might try. These shocks also answer real downside questions. A COVID-type revenue collapse (many Indian discretionary companies saw Q1 FY21 revenue fall 50–90% year on year), a commodity margin squeeze (Indian steel, cement and paint companies saw sharp EBITDA compression in FY22–FY23), or a working-capital crunch when a large customer delays payment.

Core notes

Design: an extreme-input override layer

Do not overwrite scenario inputs. Add a stress layer on the inputs sheet:

Stress_On      = 0/1 switch
Growth_Shock   = additive shock to growth (e.g. -0.40)
Margin_Shock   = additive shock to EBITDA margin (e.g. -0.15)
Capex_Mult     = multiplier on capex (e.g. 3)
DSO_Shock      = additive days (e.g. +45)
DIO_Shock      = additive days (e.g. +30)

Live_Growth_Final = Live_Growth + Stress_On * Growth_Shock
Live_Capex_Final  = Live_Capex * IF(Stress_On = 1, Capex_Mult, 1)

One switch returns the model to the clean scenario, and each shock can be run alone (one-at-a-time) or combined.

Shock 1: Zero and negative growth

What to test: growth = 0%, −20%, −50%, and −100% (revenue to zero) in FY26E.

What should happen:

  • Working capital releases cash as receivables and inventory fall (a CFO inflow). If the release shows as an outflow, the ΔNWC formula is direction-blind, for example =-ABS(Closing-Opening) or =-MAX(0,Closing-Opening). Such formulas give the right answer while working capital only grows, so Base, Bull and Bear can all balance with the error in place.
  • Fixed costs (employee cost, rent-like costs, fixed overheads), if modelled as fixed or semi-fixed, cause EBITDA to fall more than revenue (operating leverage).
  • Days-based formulas divide by revenue or COGS. Historical DSO calculations are safe, but forecast-day outputs (such as "implied DSO" KPI rows) produce #DIV/0! at zero revenue. Wrap KPI rows with IFERROR(…, 0) or IF(Revenue=0, 0, …). Never wrap calculation rows in IFERROR, because it hides real errors.
  • Growth below −100% produces negative revenue. Add a data validation lower bound of −100% on growth inputs, or floor revenue at zero.

Shock 2: Margin collapse

What to test: EBITDA margin −10 to −20 percentage points, including negative EBITDA.

What should happen:

  • PBT becomes negative. Current tax must be zero, losses go into the carry-forward corkscrew, and DTA recognition follows the switch (s04, c01).
  • Dividends are floored at zero (s05).
  • ICR and DSCR go below 1.0x or negative. Covenant flags must fire (s06). Net debt/EBITDA becomes meaningless when EBITDA ≤ 0, so show "n.m." or flag it as a breach directly (=IF(EBITDA<=0, "Breach", ND/EBITDA)).
  • Margin collapse driven by COGS: if COGS exceeds revenue, inventory based on DIO × COGS rises even as revenue falls. Check that this is intended.

Shock 3: 3× capex

What to test: capex multiplier of 3 in all years, or a single-year 3× spike.

What should happen:

  • Cash falls and the revolver draws within its limit. Any shortfall beyond the limit must be flagged, not silently funded (s03).
  • Depreciation rises with a lag consistent with your convention (full-year, half-year or capitalisation-based). If capex goes into CWIP with delayed capitalisation, depreciation should not jump in the same year.
  • DTL rises if tax depreciation (WDV under the IT Act) exceeds book depreciation (SLM under Schedule II of the Companies Act). Check that ΔDTL flows into CFO and the BS DTL.
  • Net debt/EBITDA rises sharply and may breach the covenant.

Shock 4: DSO and DIO shocks

What to test: DSO +30 to +60 days, DIO +30 days, DPO −30 days (suppliers tighten terms), separately and combined.

What should happen:

  • A large one-year CFO outflow in the shock year equal to the increase in working capital. For example, revenue of ₹3,650 crore with DSO up 30 days means receivables rise by 3,650 × 30 ÷ 365 = ₹300 crore.
  • If the shock is permanent, no further outflow occurs after the first year beyond normal growth. A recurring outflow suggests the model uses cumulative days or references the wrong year.
  • The revolver may draw. In the reversal case (DSO falls back), the revolver repays.

Extreme-case test grid

Run each shock one at a time with Base underneath, then a combined "perfect storm":

Test Growth Margin Capex DSO/DIO Checks to confirm
E1 0% Base Base Base BS check, WC no movement except inflation
E2 −50% FY26E Base Base Base WC release inflow, KPI rows no errors
E3 Base −15 pp Base Base Tax ≥ 0, loss c/f, dividends ≥ 0

Capture results in the same automated format as the scenario check table (s01).

Illustration

Kaveri Consumer Ltd: FY25A revenue ₹5,000 crore, COGS ₹3,000 crore, DSO 40 days, DIO 60 days and DPO 50 days on COGS. Under E2 (growth −50%), FY26E revenue is ₹2,500 crore and COGS ₹1,500 crore. Receivables fall from 548 to 274 and inventory from 493 to 247, releasing ₹520 crore of cash. Payables fall from 411 to 205, consuming ₹206 crore. The net release is ₹314 crore, before the operating loss. Suppose the WC schedule computes each asset movement as =-ABS(Closing-Opening). The ₹520 crore release then appears as a ₹520 crore outflow, a 2X error of ₹1,040 crore on the asset lines. If the payables line uses the equivalent =ABS(…), it books the ₹206 crore decrease as an inflow, a further 2X error of ₹412 crore in the opposite direction. Neither error showed in Base, Bull or Bear, because working capital grew every year in those cases and ABS returned the correct sign. Only a direction-changing stress exposes them.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Depreciation beyond NBV. At very low capex with straight-line depreciation on opening gross block, NBV can go negative in later years. Cap depreciation at opening NBV plus additions: =MIN(Dep_Formula, NBV_Open + Additions).
  • Negative inventory or receivables from additive day shocks (DSO −60 on a base of 40). Floor days at zero in the live input.
  • Interest income on negative cash. If cash goes negative without a revolver, a formula Cash × Rate produces negative interest income. Treat negative cash as an overdraft at the overdraft rate, or flag it and stop.
  • Employee costs at zero revenue. Fixed costs continue. Ensure cost drivers are not all percentages of revenue, or a revenue collapse will show an implausibly stable margin.
  • Extreme inputs and circularity. Large negative cash plus average-balance interest can diverge. Check convergence on the dashboard (s07). If it diverges, review the revolver logic before blaming the inputs.
  • Do not "fix" extreme results by capping inputs silently. Validation limits are fine, but they must be visible (data validation messages or a flag), never hidden MAX/MIN clamps inside calculation rows.

Key takeaways

  • Extreme inputs test formula robustness, not forecasts. The model must balance and stay logically valid at the boundaries.
  • Build a stress override layer with one master switch. Never overwrite scenario inputs.
  • Revenue declines reverse the direction of WC movements and expose direction-blind ΔNWC formulas (ABS, MAX(0,…)) that growth cases hide.
  • Margin collapse tests tax floors, loss corkscrews, dividend floors and covenant flags. Capex and WC shocks test the revolver limit and shortfall flag.
  • Protect KPI rows from division errors, cap depreciation at NBV, floor days at zero, and never wrap calculation rows in IFERROR.

Practice Questions

Q1. Design the stress layer [L2]

Write the inputs and live-formula logic for a stress layer covering growth, EBITDA margin, capex, DSO, DIO and DPO shocks, with a single on/off switch and floors that prevent negative revenue and negative days. Use named ranges, and explain how a reviewer would know at a glance that stress mode is on.

Q2. DSO shock arithmetic [L2]

Revenue of Mandakini Electricals Ltd is forecast at ₹7,300 crore (FY26E) and ₹8,030 crore (FY27E). Base DSO is 55 days. In the stress case DSO rises to 90 days in FY26E and stays at 90. Minimum cash is ₹150 crore, opening cash is ₹420 crore and the revolver limit is ₹400 crore (undrawn). Base CFO before WC changes is ₹520 crore in FY26E. Capex is ₹380 crore and other financing flows are nil. (a) Compute the incremental receivables in FY26E and FY27E compared with Base. (b) Determine whether the revolver draws in FY26E and by how much, assuming no other changes. (c) State what you expect to see in FY27E and why.

💡 Hint: Separate the one-off step change in days from the growth-driven increase.

Q3. Margin collapse checklist [L2]

Under a −15 percentage point margin shock, Shivalik Paints Ltd's EBITDA becomes −₹80 crore in FY27E. List every model output you would inspect in FY27E and FY28E, the expected behaviour of each, and the formula pattern that guarantees that behaviour. Cover tax, DTA, dividends, covenants, revolver, interest and KPI rows.

Q4. Hidden sign error [L3]

A reviewer says: "Your model balanced in Base, Bull and Bear, but under E2 (−50% growth) the check is −312 in FY26E." Explain why a sign error could survive all three scenarios but fail under E2. Describe how you would confirm the hypothesis in two steps, and how you would prevent this class of error in future models.
