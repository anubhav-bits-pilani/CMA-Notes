Roadmap topic: Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) Derivation: NOPAT (EBIT × (1 – t)) + D&A – Capex – ΔNWC; aligning mid-year discounting convention

Overview

Free cash flow to the firm is the cash the operating business generates after paying operating taxes and making the investments needed to sustain and grow it — before any payment to lenders, preference holders or shareholders. It is the numerator of the enterprise DCF: discount FCFF at WACC and you get enterprise value. For an FP&A or valuation role in India, FCFF is also the language of capital allocation (what capex earns), of impairment testing under Ind AS 36 (value in use is essentially a pre-tax FCFF DCF of a CGU), of Rule 11UA valuations for share issues and of every sell-side DCF target price.

The derivation looks simple — NOPAT plus D&A minus capex minus ΔNWC — but each term carries judgement. NOPAT needs an operating EBIT stripped of other income and exceptional items, taxed at the marginal 115BAA rate rather than the historical ETR. D&A, capex and ΔNWC must be linked from the Project 4 schedules so that FCFF reconciles to the cash flow statement. Ind AS 116 forces a basis choice: treat leases as debt (post-116) or rent as opex (pre-116), and carry that choice consistently into WACC, the bridge and comps. Timing matters too: the valuation date rarely falls on 31 March, so a stub period and the mid-year convention set the discount exponents.

The learning sequence is: (1) build NOPAT correctly (s01, c01); (2) add the reinvestment lines from schedules (s02); (3) fix the lease basis (s03, c06); (4) extend the horizon until steady state using a fade (s04), checking that growth is funded via the reinvestment identity (c05); (5) set the valuation date, stub and mid-year exponents (s05, c04); (6) confirm that FCFF rather than FCFE is the right framing, and compute FCFE as a cross-check where useful (s06, c03); and (7) reconcile FCFF from PAT (c02). The output of this topic — a row of FCFFs with discount factors — feeds the WACC topic (t2), the terminal value (t3) and the EV bridge (t4).

Related concepts to study

Weeks 14–15: schedules and CFS feed FCFF

→ Weeks 14–15: schedules and CFS supply every FCFF line

The integrated model built in Weeks 14–15 is the data engine for the DCF. Revenue and cost schedules give EBIT; the fixed-asset corkscrew gives D&A and capex (including CWIP spend); the working-capital schedule gives NWC by line from DSO/DIO/DPO; the tax schedule gives the cash tax profile; the debt schedule gives interest and net borrowing for FCFE. The cash flow statement then acts as a reconciliation: CFO + after-tax interest − capex should approximate FCFF once non-operating items are removed. If the DCF sheet contains any typed number in a forecast column, the link between operating model and valuation has broken — scenario switches will not flow to value.

Phase 1: Ind AS 116 lessee accounting

→ Phase 1 (W1–4): Ind AS 116 lessee accounting

Ind AS 116 requires lessees to recognise a ROU asset and a lease liability at the PV of lease payments discounted at the rate implicit in the lease or, more commonly, the incremental borrowing rate. ROU depreciation and interest on the liability replace rent in the P&L; payments split into interest and principal in the CFS. Short-term and low-value exemptions, variable payments linked to sales, and remeasurement on modifications all affect what is capitalised. For FCFF, the accounting determines which lines must be adjusted: post-116 requires ROU additions as reinvestment and lease liabilities in the bridge; pre-116 requires reversing ROU depreciation and lease interest and deducting cash rent. Knowing where Indian annual reports disclose these numbers (the leases note and the CFS financing section) is essential.

Phase 6: capital budgeting NPV and M&A valuation

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): capital budgeting NPV and M&A valuation

Project NPV is a FCFF DCF at project level: incremental after-tax operating cash flows, capex, working capital and salvage, discounted at a project-specific WACC. The same rules apply — exclude financing flows, use marginal tax, include working capital release at end of life, and do not double-count interest. In M&A, the target's standalone FCFF is the starting point, with synergies (cost and revenue) and integration costs layered on as separate FCFF streams, each with its own risk profile. Phase 6 extends this topic's mechanics to acquisition premiums, synergy valuation and accretion/dilution — so a robust FCFF build now is a prerequisite.

ICAI Valuation Standards and Rule 11UA DCF

ICAI Valuation Standards 2018 and Rule 11UA DCF for unlisted share valuation

ICAI Valuation Standards 2018 (VS 101–103, 201–202, 301–303) govern valuations by ICAI members and registered valuers under the Companies (Registered Valuers and Valuation) Rules, 2017. VS 103 recognises the income approach (DCF) alongside market and cost approaches and requires reasoning for the approach chosen. Rule 11UA of the Income-tax Rules permits a merchant banker's DCF for fair market value of unquoted equity shares for Sec 56(2)(viib) (angel tax — abolished from AY 2025-26 by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, but historic cases remain) and Sec 56(2)(x)/50CA transactions, and the FEMA pricing guidelines also accept DCF for share issues to non-residents. Such reports are scrutinised for unrealistic projections — tax tribunals have rejected DCFs where projections diverged widely from actuals — so documented assumptions, a consistent FCFF build and a defensible terminal value are practical necessities.

Common pitfalls

Deducting interest or using PAT/EBITDA as FCFF

Deducting interest in FCFF or using PAT/EBITDA as FCFF

The mistake: FCFF is computed from PAT (after interest) without adding back after-tax interest, or EBITDA is discounted directly as if it were free cash flow. Why it happens: corporate "free cash flow" definitions in board packs often start from CFO, which in Indian practice may include interest paid; EBITDA is the headline number everyone quotes. Correct treatment: FCFF is pre-financing — start from operating EBIT and tax it at the marginal rate (c01), or add back Interest × (1 − t) to PAT (c02). Deduct taxes, capex and ΔNWC from EBITDA. Interest is reflected in WACC; deducting it in FCFF too understates value, while discounting EBITDA overstates it massively. Detection: check that the FCFF row has no reference to the finance-cost line except in the PAT-route cross-check; compare FCFF/EBITDA conversion with peers (typically 30–70% for Indian manufacturers).

Historical ETR or other income in EBIT

Taxing EBIT at historical ETR or leaving other income in EBIT

The mistake: NOPAT uses the three-year average effective tax rate, and EBIT includes treasury income from cash and investments. Why it happens: reported "EBIT" in data terminals often includes other income; ETR is easy to compute. Correct treatment: use the marginal rate (25.17% under 115BAA, or a converging schedule if old-regime incentives persist) and construct operating EBIT that excludes other income, exceptional items and associate share. Non-operating assets producing that income are added at value in the bridge. Detection: compare the DCF's EBIT row with revenue minus operating expenses; check whether cash in the bridge and interest income in EBIT coexist; look for a tax rate row that differs from the named marginal-rate input.

Ignoring new lease additions post-116

Post-116 FCFF ignoring new lease additions, overstating value for lease-heavy retailers like Trent

The mistake: the analyst uses post-116 EBITDA (rent excluded), adds back ROU depreciation, excludes lease payments — but forgets that new stores require new leases, i.e. ROU additions. Why it happens: ROU additions are non-cash in the CFS and appear only in the leases note; the capex line from the CFS excludes them. For a fast-expanding retailer such as Trent (Westside, Zudio) adding hundreds of stores a year, the omission can overstate FCFF by 30–100% and EV by a similar order. Correct treatment: forecast ROU additions from the store roll-out (new stores × average lease liability at inception, plus renewals/remeasurements) and deduct them as reinvestment; in the terminal year, ROU additions ≥ ROU depreciation. Alternatively, switch to pre-116 and deduct full rent. Detection: compare forecast ROU asset growth with store-count growth; check that the lease liability corkscrew has additions; compare post-116 and pre-116 valuations — a gap above ~5% signals a problem.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated FCFF build for a mid-cap manufacturer [L3]

A Nifty Midcap 150 auto-ancillary company provides (₹ crore): FY26E–FY30E revenue 5,000 / 5,600 / 6,250 / 6,900 / 7,500; EBITDA margin 15% throughout; D&A 4% of revenue; other income 1% of revenue (all from treasury investments); maintenance capex equal to D&A × 1.1; growth capex 300 / 350 / 250 / 200 / 150; NWC at 18% of revenue (FY25 revenue 4,500 with NWC at 18%); marginal tax 25.17%; WACC 11.8%; valuation date 30 September 2025, with H1 FY26 actual FCFF of ₹110 crore. (a) Build FY26E–FY30E FCFF. (b) Compute stub FCFF (actual-based) and mid-year discount factors. (c) Compute PV of explicit FCFF. (d) Compute the reinvestment rate and ROIC in FY30 given invested capital of ₹4,300 crore at 31 March 2029, and comment on whether 6% terminal growth is funded.

💡 Hint: For (d), compare RR × ROIC with the terminal growth rate you would like to use.

Q2. Lease-heavy retailer: two bases [L3]

An apparel retailer's FY27E figures (₹ crore): post-116 EBITDA 2,200; PPE D&A 260; ROU depreciation 420; lease interest 190; cash lease payments 560; ROU additions 700; capex 480; ΔNWC 90; tax 25.17%. Lease liabilities at valuation date ₹3,100 crore; borrowings ₹400 crore; cash ₹900 crore. (a) Compute FCFF on both post-116 and pre-116 bases. (b) State the bridge deductions under each. (c) Explain how WACC differs between the bases. (d) An analyst used post-116 FCFF without ROU additions and pre-116 bridge — estimate the direction and approximate scale of the error in a single-year context.

Q3. Review memo on a Rule 11UA DCF [L2]

A merchant banker's DCF for a private SaaS company (for a share issue) shows: FCFF = EBITDA − tax on PBT − capex; tax at the FY25 ETR of 9% in perpetuity (due to brought-forward losses); a five-year horizon with revenue growth of 35% in the final year; terminal growth 8%; year-end discounting from 31 March 2025 although the valuation date is 15 January 2026. Write a review memo listing each issue, why it matters, and the corrected treatment.

Q4. FCFF vs FCFE choice and reconciliation [L2]

A listed cement company with net debt of ₹6,000 crore plans to repay ₹1,200 crore per year for five years. FY27E FCFF is ₹1,500 crore, interest ₹480 crore, tax 25.17%. (a) Compute FY27E FCFE. (b) Explain whether FCFF or FCFE is the better primary method and why. (c) Describe what must change in Ke each year if you insist on FCFE.

Q5. Interview drill [L1]

Answer in short form: (a) Why is interest excluded from FCFF? (b) Why use 25.17% rather than the ETR? (c) What does the mid-year convention assume, and what is the stub exponent for a 31 December valuation date? (d) What happens to FCFF when a negative-working-capital company grows?
