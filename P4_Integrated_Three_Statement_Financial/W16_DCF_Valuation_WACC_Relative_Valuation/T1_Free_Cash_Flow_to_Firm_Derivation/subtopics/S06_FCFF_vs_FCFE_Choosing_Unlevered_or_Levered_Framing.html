Subtopic note

FCFF vs FCFE: Choosing Unlevered or Levered Framing

Scope: FCFF vs FCFE choice; unlevered vs levered framing

Why it matters

Every DCF must answer one question before any number is typed: whose cash flows are we discounting? FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) is the cash available to all capital providers — lenders, preference holders and shareholders — before any financing flows. FCFE (free cash flow to equity) is what remains for ordinary shareholders after interest, debt repayments and new borrowings. The choice determines the discount rate (WACC vs cost of equity), the output (enterprise value vs equity value) and the bridge. Using FCFF with Ke, or FCFE with WACC, is a conceptual error that invalidates the valuation. In practice, industrial and consumer companies are valued on FCFF; banks, NBFCs and insurers on FCFE or dividend/residual-income models. Interviewers routinely ask candidates to defend the choice.

Core notes

Definitions

\[ \text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}(1-t) + \text{D\&A} - \text{Capex} - \Delta\text{NWC} \]

\[ \text{FCFE} = \text{FCFF} - \text{Interest} \times (1-t) + \text{Net borrowing} \]

where net borrowing = new debt raised − debt repaid (and, if preference capital exists, preference dividends and redemptions are also deducted to get FCFE to ordinary equity).

Unlevered (FCFF) framing

  • Cash flow is computed as if the firm had no debt: interest is not deducted, so there is no tax shield in the cash flow.
  • The benefit of debt (interest tax shield) and its cost are captured in WACC, which weights after-tax cost of debt and cost of equity.
  • Output: enterprise value (value of operating assets). Equity value = EV + non-operating assets − debt and debt-like claims.
  • Strength: independent of the year-by-year financing plan; capital structure enters only through target weights. Robust when leverage is stable or targeted.

Levered (FCFE) framing

  • Cash flow is after all debt-related flows, so it depends on the financing plan (debt schedule).
  • Discount rate is cost of equity Ke, which must reflect the leverage implied by the debt plan (relevered beta).
  • Output: equity value directly; no debt deduction in the bridge (but still add non-operating assets not reflected in FCFE).
  • Strength: natural for financial institutions where debt is raw material, not financing; also for highly structured deals (LBO equity IRR).

Choosing between them

Situation Preferred Why
Industrial/consumer company with stable or target leverage FCFF + WACC Separates operating value from financing; consistent with EV multiples
Bank, NBFC, HFC, insurer FCFE + Ke (or DDM/residual income) Interest is operating; "debt" is deposits/borrowings used to generate spread
Leverage changing sharply (LBO, deleveraging after IPO) FCFE with year-specific Ke, or APV Constant-weight WACC becomes inaccurate
Real-estate SPV with project-specific debt FCFE often used Debt is non-recourse and tied to project cash
Minority equity stake valuation Either; FCFE closer to investor's claim Must still be consistent

Consistency triad

The cross-cutting rule for this week:

  • FCFF ↔ WACC ↔ EV
  • FCFE ↔ Ke ↔ Equity value

If both are done correctly, with leverage in the FCFE debt plan consistent with the WACC target weights, equity value from FCFE ≈ EV from FCFF less net debt. In practice they diverge because (i) the debt plan in FCFE rarely tracks the WACC target ratio exactly, and (ii) Ke in FCFE is usually held constant while leverage changes. A divergence of a few percent is acceptable; larger gaps signal inconsistency in leverage assumptions.

Worked illustration

FY27E (₹ crore): EBIT 900; tax 25.17%; D&A 220; capex 380; ΔNWC 70; interest 120; new borrowings 200; repayments 150.

\[ \text{FCFF} = 900 \times 0.7483 + 220 - 380 - 70 = 443.5 \]

\[ \text{FCFE} = 443.5 - 120 \times 0.7483 + (200 - 150) = 443.5 - 89.8 + 50 = 403.7 \]

FCFF of ₹443.5 crore is discounted at WACC (say 11.8%); FCFE of ₹403.7 crore at Ke (say 14.5%).

Why PAT and EBITDA are not FCFF

PAT is after interest and includes non-cash items and non-operating income; EBITDA is pre-tax and pre-reinvestment. Neither is a free cash flow. Discounting EBITDA at WACC ignores taxes, capex and working capital — a common error flagged in t1's pitfalls.

Excel implementation

Build FCFF as the primary output and FCFE as a memo row below it, linked to the debt schedule:

DCF!F20  =DCF!F18-Debt!F25*(1-Tax_Marginal)+(Debt!F12-Debt!F13)   ' FCFE memo
DCF!F21  =1/(1+Ke)^DCF!F8                                         ' Ke discount factor for FCFE

Showing both lets the reviewer see whether the debt plan is plausible (e.g. if FCFE is negative in years when the company pays dividends, the plan implies further borrowing).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Preference shares and NCI. In FCFF, they are claims deducted in the bridge. In FCFE to ordinary equity, preference dividends are deducted from cash flow; NCI share of subsidiary FCFE must be removed (or NCI deducted at value).
  • Cash build-up. If FCFE is retained rather than paid out, Ke-based value implicitly assumes reinvestment at Ke; for cash-rich companies, model payout or treat excess cash separately.
  • Negative FCFE in a capex-heavy phase implies equity infusion; ensure the dilution is captured (new shares at fair value do not dilute value per share if priced fairly).
  • Interest income. Excluded from FCFF; in FCFE, include it only if the cash generating it is not separately added.
  • APV (adjusted present value) separates unlevered value (discounted at \( K_u \)) from the PV of tax shields — useful when leverage varies but rarely used in Indian sell-side.

Key takeaways

  • FCFF is pre-financing and discounted at WACC to give EV; FCFE is post-financing and discounted at Ke to give equity value.
  • Interest tax shield sits in WACC for FCFF, and in the cash flow for FCFE — never in both.
  • Use FCFF for operating companies; FCFE/DDM/residual income for banks, NBFCs and insurers.
  • FCFE depends on the debt plan; FCFF depends only on target weights.
  • PAT and EBITDA are not free cash flows.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute FCFF and FCFE [L1]

FY28E (₹ crore): EBITDA 1,450; D&A 310; tax 25.17%; capex 520; ΔNWC 95; interest expense 160; debt raised 300; debt repaid 220; preference dividend 12. Compute FCFF, FCFE to ordinary equity, and state the discount rate for each.

Q2. Framework selection [L2]

Recommend FCFF or FCFE (or an alternative) for each, with justification: (a) a listed housing finance company; (b) a cement company that will reduce net debt/EBITDA from 3.5× to 0.5× over five years; (c) an FMCG company with zero debt and ₹3,000 crore cash; (d) a real-estate SPV with a construction loan repayable from flat sales.

Q3. Reconciling FCFF and FCFE values [L3]

Your FCFF DCF gives EV ₹18,000 crore and net debt is ₹4,000 crore. Your FCFE DCF gives equity value ₹12,600 crore. The FCFE debt plan reduces debt to zero by year 4, while WACC uses a target D/(D+E) of 25%. Explain the likely source of the gap and how you would make the two methods consistent.

💡 Hint: Consider what the debt plan implies for leverage versus the constant Ke and target weights.

Q4. Interview: discount EBITDA? [L1]

An intern discounts forecast EBITDA at WACC and calls the result enterprise value. List every item that is missing and explain the direction of bias.

Q5. Bank valuation [L2]

Explain why FCFF is inappropriate for a private-sector bank, and describe what the "reinvestment" in an FCFE model for a bank represents in terms of regulatory capital (CRAR under RBI Basel III norms).
