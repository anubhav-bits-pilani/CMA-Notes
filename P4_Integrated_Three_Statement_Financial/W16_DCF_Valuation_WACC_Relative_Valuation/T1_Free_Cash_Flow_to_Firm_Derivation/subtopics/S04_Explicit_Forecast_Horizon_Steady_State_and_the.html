Explicit Forecast Horizon, Steady State and the Fade Period

Scope: Explicit forecast horizon to steady state (5–10 years) with fade period

Why it matters

A DCF has two parts: an explicit forecast period where each year's FCFF is modelled, and a terminal value that capitalises everything after. The terminal value is only valid if the business is in steady state at the end of the explicit period — constant growth, stable margins, stable return on capital and a reinvestment rate consistent with growth. If the explicit period stops while the company is still growing at 20% with expanding margins, the terminal value either assumes that growth forever (absurd) or truncates it abruptly (an artificial cliff). For Indian growth companies — consumer, specialty chemicals, financial platforms, renewables — the natural horizon is often 10 years rather than the three-statement model's 5, with a fade period that converges growth and returns toward long-run levels. Investment committees and valuation peer reviewers routinely ask: "Why does your forecast stop here, and what does year 6 look like?"

Core notes

Defining steady state

A firm is in steady state when, from that point on, it can be modelled as a growing perpetuity:

  • Revenue growth ≈ long-run nominal growth (for India, typically 5–7% INR nominal in perpetuity — see t3).
  • EBIT margin stable at a sustainable level (competitive equilibrium; not peak-cycle).
  • ROIC on new investment (RONIC) stable, converging toward or modestly above WACC.
  • Reinvestment rate consistent with \( g = \text{Reinvestment rate} \times \text{RONIC} \) (c05).
  • Capex ≥ D&A, NWC growing with revenue, tax at marginal rate, no MAT/holiday effects.
  • Capital structure at target.

Choosing the horizon length

Company profile Typical explicit horizon Rationale
Mature, low-growth (utilities, staples incumbents, PSU oil marketing) 5 years Already near steady state; 3-statement model horizon suffices
Moderate growth with visible capex cycle (cement, auto ancillaries, steel) 5–7 years Capture current capacity cycle until utilisation stabilises
High-growth (retail roll-outs, specialty chemicals, platforms, renewables IPPs) 10 years (sometimes 15) Growth materially above GDP must fade before TV
Finite-life assets (toll roads, PPA-backed power plants, mines) Asset life; no Gordon TV Concession/licence ends; use salvage or zero terminal value

The rule of thumb: extend the horizon until the year-on-year changes in growth, margin and ROIC are small; never stop just because the operating model has five columns.

Two-stage build: detailed years + fade years

A practical structure links the detailed Project 4 three-statement forecast (years 1–5) to a simpler fade module (years 6–10) on the DCF sheet:

  1. Years 1–5 (detailed): FCFF lines linked from the integrated model — revenue build, margins, capex schedule, NWC days.
  2. Years 6–10 (fade): drive only a handful of variables: revenue growth, EBIT margin, tax rate, and reinvestment (either sales-to-capital ratio or reinvestment rate). Each fades linearly (or geometrically) from the year-5 value to the terminal value.

Linear fade formula for a driver \( x \) from year 5 value \( x_5 \) to terminal value \( x_T \) over \( N \) fade years:

\[ x_{5+k} = x_5 + \frac{k}{N} \times (x_T - x_5), \quad k = 1, \dots, N \]

Reinvestment in fade years can be set by a sales-to-capital ratio (Damodaran's approach): reinvestment = Δrevenue ÷ (sales-to-capital), which keeps growth funded. Alternatively, reinvestment = NOPAT × \( g \div \text{RONIC} \).

Competitive advantage period (CAP)

The fade reflects economics: excess returns (ROIC > WACC) attract competition. The period over which a company sustains excess returns — the competitive advantage period — determines how long growth adds value. Companies with strong moats (Asian Paints' distribution, Pidilite's brand in adhesives) justify longer CAPs and higher terminal RONIC; commodity businesses fade fast. A transparent fade makes the CAP assumption visible instead of burying it in the terminal value.

Illustration

A specialty-chemicals company has FY30E (year 5) revenue growth of 18%, EBIT margin 19%, and terminal assumptions of 6% growth and 16% margin. With a five-year fade (FY31–FY35):

Year FY31 FY32 FY33 FY34 FY35
Growth 15.6% 13.2% 10.8% 8.4% 6.0%
EBIT margin 18.4% 17.8% 17.2% 16.6% 16.0%

FY36 (the year after the explicit horizon) then grows at 6% with a 16% margin, and Gordon TV is applied to that normalised FY36 FCFF. Had the analyst stopped at FY30 and applied 6% growth to FY30 FCFF, the valuation would ignore five years of above-GDP growth (understating value) — or, if they used a 10% "compromise" g in perpetuity, it would overstate it.

Excel implementation

DCF!K5  =J5+($P$5-$J$5)/Fade_Years          ' Growth fades from year-5 value to terminal g
DCF!K6  =J6+($P$6-$J$6)/Fade_Years          ' EBIT margin fade
DCF!K8  =K7*K6                               ' EBIT = Revenue x margin
DCF!K12 =(K7-J7)/Sales_to_Capital            ' Reinvestment in fade years
DCF!K13 =K8*(1-Tax_Marginal)-K12             ' FCFF in fade years

The anchored $J$5 (year-5 value) and $P$5 (terminal value) keep the step constant as the formula is copied right across the fade columns. Add a check that year-10 growth equals terminal g exactly; if Fade_Years is changed without changing the number of fade columns, the check will fail and flag it.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Cyclical businesses. Ending the explicit period at a cyclical peak (steel, sugar, cement realisations) inflates TV. Normalise to mid-cycle margins in the terminal year, or extend the horizon to complete a cycle.
  • Loss-making growth companies. Ten years may be needed for margins to reach steady state; the near-term negative FCFFs are genuine and must not be "smoothed".
  • Policy-driven businesses. PLI-benefited manufacturers: incentives typically last five or six years; fade margin down after the scheme ends.
  • Debt and capital structure during fade. If the company is de-leveraging, a constant WACC is an approximation; for significant changes, use year-specific WACC (cumulative discount factors) or APV.
  • Too long a horizon without a fade module simply extends spreadsheet guesswork; precision beyond year 5 is illusory, so keep fade drivers few and explicit.

Key takeaways

  • The explicit horizon must end in steady state — stable growth, margins, ROIC and reinvestment.
  • Mature firms: 5 years; growth firms: 10 years with a fade; finite-life assets: asset life without Gordon TV.
  • Build years 1–5 from the integrated model and years 6–10 as a driver-based fade on the DCF sheet.
  • Fade growth, margin and reinvestment linearly toward terminal values; check the final year hits them exactly.
  • The fade makes the competitive advantage period explicit instead of hiding it in the TV.

Practice Questions

Q1. Fade schedule build [L1]

Year-5 revenue growth is 22% and EBIT margin is 14%; terminal growth is 5.5% and margin 12%. Construct the year-6 to year-10 growth and margin schedule using a linear fade and write the Excel formula for the year-7 growth cell.

Q2. Choosing the horizon [L2]

For each of the following, recommend an explicit horizon and terminal approach, with reasons: (a) a 25-year toll road concession starting FY22; (b) a mature PSU oil marketing company; (c) a listed retail chain growing stores at 25% per year; (d) a solar IPP with 25-year PPAs.

Q3. Sales-to-capital reinvestment [L2]

Year-5 revenue is ₹6,000 crore. Revenue grows 14%, 12%, 10%, 8% and 6% in years 6–10. EBIT margin is constant at 15%, tax 25.17%, sales-to-capital ratio 2.0. Compute reinvestment and FCFF for years 6–10 and the implied RONIC in year 10 given year-10 growth.

💡 Hint: Reinvestment = change in revenue ÷ sales-to-capital; implied RONIC = g ÷ reinvestment rate.

Q4. Cyclical-peak terminal year [L3]

A steel company's FY30E EBITDA margin is 24% (cycle peak); 10-year average is 16%. The analyst applies Gordon growth to FY30 FCFF. Write a critique and propose two alternative structures that produce a defensible terminal year.

Q5. Interview: why 10 years? [L1]

Explain to a CFO in under 150 words why your DCF of their high-growth company uses a 10-year horizon when their board-approved business plan covers only five years.
