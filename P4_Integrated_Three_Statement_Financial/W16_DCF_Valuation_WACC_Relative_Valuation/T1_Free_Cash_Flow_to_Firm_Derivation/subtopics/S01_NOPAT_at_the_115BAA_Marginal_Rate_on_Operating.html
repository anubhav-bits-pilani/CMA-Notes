Subtopic note

NOPAT at the 115BAA Marginal Rate on Operating EBIT

Scope: NOPAT at marginal tax rate (25.17% under 115BAA), EBIT excluding other income

Why it matters

NOPAT (net operating profit after tax) is the starting line of every FCFF forecast. Each rupee of error here is capitalised into the terminal value, so a mistake of 2 percentage points in the tax rate or ₹50 crore of treasury income left inside EBIT can move enterprise value by 3–8%. Equity research reports, fairness opinions under SEBI regulations, Rule 11UA valuation reports and internal impairment tests (Ind AS 36 value in use) all start from an operating profit taxed at a rate the business will actually pay on its marginal operating rupee. Reviewers — auditors testing an Ind AS 36 model, investment committees, ICAI-registered valuers' peer reviewers — look first at two things: what is inside EBIT, and which tax rate was applied.

Core notes

What NOPAT represents

NOPAT is the after-tax profit the operating business would earn if it had no debt and no non-operating assets. It is deliberately "unlevered": interest expense is excluded, so the interest tax shield is not counted in the cash flow (it is captured in WACC through \( K_d \times (1 - t) \)). It is also "pure operating": income from cash, treasury investments and non-core assets is excluded, because those assets are added separately in the EV-to-equity bridge.

\[ \text{NOPAT} = \text{EBIT}_{\text{operating}} \times (1 - t_{\text{marginal}}) \]

where \( \text{EBIT}_{\text{operating}} \) is revenue from operations less operating expenses (including depreciation and amortisation), before finance cost, other income and exceptional items; and \( t_{\text{marginal}} \) is the statutory rate expected on incremental operating profit.

Building operating EBIT from a Schedule III P&L

Schedule III Division II does not present an "EBIT" line, so it must be constructed:

Line (Schedule III) Treatment for operating EBIT
Revenue from operations (incl. other operating revenue — scrap, export incentives, PLI) Include
Other income (interest on FDs, dividend, MF gains/fair value changes, rental from investment property, profit on sale of PPE, liabilities written back) Exclude by default; reclassify only items that are genuinely operating and recurring
Cost of materials, purchases, changes in inventories Include
Employee benefits expense (incl. ESOP cost) Include (ESOP cost is a real economic cost; do not add it back for valuation purposes unless dilution is separately modelled)
Other expenses Include; strip one-offs
Depreciation and amortisation (incl. ROU depreciation post-116) Include
Finance costs (incl. interest on lease liabilities, unwinding of discount on provisions) Exclude
Exceptional items (Ind AS 1 disclosure of material items) Exclude from the forecast base; assess whether "exceptional" is really recurring
Share of profit of associates/JVs (consolidated) Exclude; associates are added in the bridge at value

Why exclude other income? For many NSE companies, other income is largely yield on surplus cash — e.g. IT services, FMCG and many mid-caps carry large treasury books. If interest on ₹2,000 crore of liquid funds sits inside EBIT, it is capitalised at the WACC multiple and the cash itself is added in the bridge — the same asset is counted twice. The test: will the asset generating this income be added separately in the bridge? If yes, exclude the income.

Items that can stay in operating EBIT: export incentives classified in other income by some companies (RoDTEP, if recurring and linked to sales), interest on trade receivables/ delayed-payment charges for a utility, rental income where property leasing is a core business. Document each reclassification.

The marginal tax rate under 115BAA

Section 115BAA of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (introduced by the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019) allows domestic companies to pay tax at 22% plus a 10% surcharge and 4% health and education cess, giving:

\[ 22\% \times 1.10 \times 1.04 = 25.168\% \approx 25.17\% \]

Companies opting for 115BAA forgo most deductions/incentives (e.g. Sec 80-IA/80-IC area-based exemptions, additional depreciation, Sec 35(2AB)) and are outside MAT (Sec 115JB does not apply). Section 115BAB (new manufacturing companies, 15% base, ~17.16% effective) has a sunset — manufacturing had to commence by 31 March 2024 — so it is relevant only to companies that qualified. Companies remaining in the old regime face 30% base (34.944% with 12% surcharge and cess for income above ₹10 crore) or 25% base for smaller turnover thresholds, plus MAT at 15% base.

For valuation of a typical Indian listed company, the default marginal rate is 25.17%, because:

  • Most large companies have migrated or will migrate once old incentives/MAT credit are exhausted.
  • The option once exercised is irrevocable, so the long-run (terminal) rate converges to 25.17%.

Where a company is still in the old regime with MAT credit or tax holidays, model a tax rate schedule: actual expected cash tax rate in explicit years, converging to 25.17% when incentives expire or the company switches. Note that the terminal-year rate must be the long-run statutory rate.

Marginal vs effective vs cash tax rate

Rate Definition Use
Statutory marginal (25.17%) Rate on the next rupee of taxable income NOPAT in steady state; \( K_d(1-t) \); beta unlevering
Effective tax rate (ETR) Tax expense ÷ PBT from the P&L Diagnostic only — distorted by exempt dividends, deferred tax remeasurement, prior-year items, non-deductible ESOP/CSR
Cash tax rate Taxes paid (CFS) ÷ PBT Explicit-year adjustment where timing differences are large (e.g. accelerated tax depreciation)

Historical ETR is a poor proxy: a company whose FY25 ETR was 18% because of a one-off deferred tax credit on regime switch will be overvalued if 18% is perpetuated. Conversely, non-deductible CSR spending (Sec 37 Explanation 2) pushes the ETR above 25.17%; strictly, the operating tax should include that permanent difference — a refinement worth noting if CSR is material.

Deferred taxes

Deferred tax arising from tax depreciation exceeding book depreciation means cash taxes are below NOPAT tax for a growing capex-heavy firm. Two acceptable approaches: (i) compute operating taxes on EBIT at 25.17% and add the increase in net deferred tax liability (operating portion) as a cash-flow adjustment; (ii) simply tax EBIT at the marginal rate (simpler, conservative). Whatever is chosen, the terminal year should not carry a perpetual deferral benefit.

Worked illustration

FY26E P&L of a Pune-based auto-component manufacturer (₹ crore): Revenue from operations 4,800; operating expenses before D&A 4,032; D&A 192; other income 86 (of which interest on FDs 70, profit on sale of land 16); finance cost 54.

\[ \text{EBIT}_{\text{op}} = 4{,}800 - 4{,}032 - 192 = 576 \]

\[ \text{NOPAT} = 576 \times (1 - 0.2517) = 431.0 \]

Had the analyst used reported "EBIT" including other income (662) and the FY25 ETR of 21%, NOPAT would be 523.0 — 21% higher, and the FD income would be double-counted with cash in the bridge.

Excel implementation

DCF!F10  =IS!F8-IS!F15-IS!F17          ' Operating EBIT = Revenue - Opex - D&A
DCF!F11  =Inputs!$C$20                  ' Marginal tax rate 25.17% (named Tax_Marginal)
DCF!F12  =DCF!F10*(1-DCF!F11)           ' NOPAT
DCF!F13  =IS!F25/IS!F23                 ' Memo: ETR, for diagnostics only

Keep the tax rate as a named input (Tax_Marginal) on the inputs sheet, with a row-wise override for explicit years where the regime differs.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Operating losses / carried-forward losses. If EBIT is negative in early years, NOPAT tax should be zero (not a negative tax) unless losses can be offset; model a tax-loss corkscrew (8-year carry-forward for business loss under Sec 72) and apply tax only when cumulative losses are absorbed. Unabsorbed depreciation carries forward indefinitely.
  • SEZ units / old-regime holidays. A company with Sec 10AA units may have a lower cash rate for a few years; model it explicitly and step up.
  • Foreign subsidiaries. In consolidated valuations, blend rates by geography (e.g. 21% US federal plus state); a single 25.17% applied to global EBIT is wrong for a company with 60% overseas profit.
  • Treasury-heavy businesses. For NBFCs, banks and insurers, interest income is operating — FCFF/NOPAT is not the right framework; use FCFE/dividend discount or residual income.
  • Other income that is operating. Scrap sales and export incentives misclassified into other income should be reclassified into revenue — check the notes to accounts.

Key takeaways

  • NOPAT = operating EBIT × (1 − marginal tax rate); it is unlevered and excludes non-operating income.
  • Operating EBIT must be constructed from Schedule III: exclude other income, finance cost, exceptional items and associate share.
  • 25.17% (22% × 1.10 × 1.04) is the default long-run marginal rate for domestic companies under 115BAA.
  • Use ETR only as a diagnostic; model a converging tax schedule if old-regime incentives or losses exist.
  • Excluding treasury income from EBIT is the mirror image of adding cash in the bridge — keep both consistent.

Practice Questions

Q1. Deriving the 115BAA rate [L1]

Show the computation of the effective rate for a domestic company opting for Sec 115BAA, and contrast it with the old-regime maximum rate for a company with total income above ₹10 crore. State why the 115BAA rate is the preferred terminal-year rate in a DCF.

Q2. Constructing operating EBIT [L2]

A Chennai FMCG company reports for FY25 (₹ crore): revenue from operations 6,200 (including scrap sales 40 and PLI incentive 60); other income 310 (interest on deposits 190, fair value gain on liquid MFs 75, rent from investment property 25, liabilities written back 20); cost of materials 3,410; employee cost 520; other expenses 1,180 (including a one-time VRS cost of 45); D&A 210; finance cost 35; exceptional item (gain on sale of brand) 140; tax expense 395. Compute operating EBIT, NOPAT at 25.17%, and the reported ETR. Explain each inclusion/exclusion.

💡 Hint: Decide line by line whether the related asset will be valued separately in the bridge; treat the VRS cost as non-recurring.

Q3. Tax schedule for a company still in the old regime [L2]

A company has unutilised MAT credit of ₹180 crore and expects taxable profit of ₹400 crore per year at the old rate of 34.944%, with MAT at 17.472%. Management plans to switch to 115BAA once MAT credit is utilised. Design the rows of a tax-rate schedule in Excel for FY26–FY32 and describe how the NOPAT tax rate should evolve to the terminal year.

Q4. Reviewer challenge on ETR [L3]

An analyst's DCF of a listed IT services mid-cap applies the three-year average ETR of 23.4% to EBIT that includes ₹420 crore of treasury income on ₹6,000 crore of cash and investments; the bridge also adds the ₹6,000 crore. Write the review note you would send, quantifying the direction of the errors and specifying the corrections.

Q5. Negative EBIT start-up [L2]

A D2C brand forecasts EBIT of ₹-80, ₹-30, ₹20, ₹90 and ₹160 crore for FY26–FY30 with ₹50 crore of brought-forward business losses at 31 March 2025. Explain how you would model NOPAT year by year, including the loss corkscrew and the eight-year carry-forward constraint.
