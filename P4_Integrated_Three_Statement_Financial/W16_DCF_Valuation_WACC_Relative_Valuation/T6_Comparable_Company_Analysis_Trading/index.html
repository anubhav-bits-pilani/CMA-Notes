Roadmap topic: Comparable Company Analysis (Trading Comps): Computing EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, P/E, P/B, and EV/Sales across 5 Indian listed peers + Football Field valuation chart

Overview

A DCF tells you what a business is worth on your assumptions. Trading comps tell you what the market is paying today for similar businesses. Comparable company analysis selects a set of listed peers and builds each one's market capitalisation and enterprise value on a consistent basis. It computes valuation multiples on historical (LTM) and forward (FY+1, FY+2) metrics, summarises them statistically, and applies the median or interquartile range to the target's metrics to derive an implied valuation range. Precedent transactions add the price paid for control in past deals. The football field brings everything together: the DCF ranges, the comps, the precedents and the 52-week trading range, plotted against the current market price.

For FP&A, equity research, corporate development and transaction-advisory roles in India, comps are a daily tool. They are used in IPO pricing (the SEBI ICDR "basis for issue price" section compares the issuer's multiples with listed peers), in fairness opinions, in ESOP and Rule 11UA valuations (as a cross-check), in board papers for acquisitions, and in investor-relations benchmarking. Comps look simple: price ÷ earnings. In practice they are full of traps. The common ones are:

  • Ind AS 116 basis mismatches (EV with leases ÷ EBITDA before leases)
  • other income left in earnings
  • exceptional items
  • standalone versus consolidated numbers
  • stale share counts
  • outliers that wreck the mean

The learning sequence runs as follows:

  1. Peer selection by business model, size, growth and margins (s01).
  2. Market data and EV build from NSE prices and the latest balance sheet (s02), using one EV definition (c01).
  3. Time-consistent metrics. LTM and forward figures, calendarisation and consensus (s03), with the LTM formula (c02).
  4. The multiples themselves (s04), with equity-level and firm-level definitions (c03) and numerator–denominator consistency (c04).
  5. Normalisation for Ind AS 116, exceptional items, other income and the consolidated basis (s05).
  6. Statistics and outlier treatment (s06).
  7. Precedent transactions and SEBI SAST pricing (s07).
  8. The football field (s08), with the chart build mechanics (c05).

Related concepts to study

Peer benchmarking and normalisation

→ Phase 2 (W5–8): peer benchmarking and metric normalisation

Phase 2 ratio analysis taught you to compute margins, returns and leverage for a peer group and to normalise reported numbers. That meant stripping exceptional items, separating other income, and adjusting for Ind AS 116 and for accounting-policy differences. Comps reuse that work directly. The "spread" table, showing peers' growth, EBITDA margin, ROCE and net debt/EBITDA, is what justifies why one peer deserves a premium multiple and another a discount. Without the benchmarking, the median multiple is just an average of businesses that may not be comparable. Strong comps sheets show operating benchmarks next to the multiples, so readers can see the relationship between quality and valuation.

Exit multiple sourced from comps

→ Week 16 (w16_t3): exit multiple sourced from comps

The exit-multiple terminal value in topic t3 takes its EV/EBITDA from this comps sheet. Two links matter. First, the basis must match. If terminal EBITDA is post-Ind AS 116, the multiple must be a post-116 EV/EBITDA. Second, the maturity must match. The terminal year represents a steady-state business, so the exit multiple should reflect mature, slower-growth peers or long-run averages, not today's high-growth multiples. Linking the exit multiple cell to Comps!Median_Mature (with an override and a documented rationale) keeps the DCF and comps consistent, and it lets Grid 2 (topic t5, s03) centre on a market-anchored base.

Precedents, control premium and synergies

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): precedent transactions, control premium, synergies

Trading comps price minority stakes in liquid markets. Precedent transactions price control, and so include a premium that reflects synergies, the ability to change strategy, and competitive tension in the auction. Phase 6 covers how acquirers justify that premium through synergy NPV and how it is split between buyer and seller. In this week's football field, precedents typically sit above trading comps. Explaining that gap is part of triangulation. In Indian listed deals, SEBI SAST open-offer pricing creates a regulatory floor that shapes observed premiums.

Power Query data pulls and charting

→ Phase 3 (W9–12): Power Query for NSE/BSE data pulls and charting

Comps data goes stale quickly. Power Query can import NSE bhavcopy files (end-of-day prices for all listed securities), shareholding-pattern data and your own consensus extracts. You can refresh them into a staging table that feeds the comps sheet through lookups keyed on the NSE symbol. This replaces manual copy-paste and its staleness risk. Phase 3 charting skills feed the football field directly (c05). Power BI can also host a refreshable comps dashboard with slicers by sector and size.

Common pitfalls

Mismatched Ind AS 116 bases

Post-116 EV (with leases) divided by pre-116 EBITDA, or mixed bases across peers

The mistake: EV includes lease liabilities, but the EBITDA used is pre-116 (after rent). Or some peers' EBITDA is taken from data providers that strip leases while others use reported post-116 figures.

Why it is serious: for retailers, QSR chains and airlines, the gap is large. Lease liabilities can be 20–40% of EV, and rent 30–50% of reported EBITDA. Mixing bases can move a multiple by several turns.

Correct treatment: choose one basis and build every peer on it. Post-116: EV includes leases and EBITDA is as reported. Pre-116: EV excludes leases and EBITDA is after cash rent.

Detection: a "basis" column per peer, plus a check that the lease liabilities in EV are zero exactly when a pre-116 EBITDA is used.

Equity multiples on firm-level metrics

Equity multiples applied to firm-level metrics (P/E on EBITDA)

The mistake: dividing market cap by EBITDA or sales ("market cap/sales"), or applying a peer P/E to the target's EBIT, and then deducting debt anyway.

Why it happens: P/E is the most familiar multiple, and some databases publish "market cap/EBITDA".

Correct treatment: pair EV with metrics before interest (Sales, EBITDA, EBIT). Pair equity value with metrics after interest and minority (PAT attributable to owners, book value attributable to owners) (c04).

Detection: for each multiple, ask whether the numerator's claim holders are paid from the denominator.

Outliers, stale data and basis mismatches

Means distorted by outliers; stale prices or share counts; standalone metrics vs consolidated EV

The mistake: a single peer at 120× P/E pulls the mean far above the median. Prices are a week old, or the share count predates a QIP or bonus. Standalone PAT is divided by a market cap that values the consolidated group.

Correct treatment:

  • prefer the median and quartiles, and mark multiples that are negative or above a cap as n.m.
  • refresh prices and shares to a single date
  • use consolidated financials throughout, with owners' share of PAT for P/E

Detection: a mean-vs-median gap flag, a "price date" column, and a "consolidated/standalone" column per peer.

Practice Questions

Q1. Five-peer comps build for an auto-component target [L3]

You are valuing Shakti Auto Components (FY27E EBITDA ₹ 1,150 crore, EBIT ₹ 860 crore, PAT attributable ₹ 516 crore, book equity attributable ₹ 3,900 crore, diluted shares 22.36 crore, net claims in the bridge ₹ 1,110 crore after adding non-operating assets). Five NSE peers (illustrative data, all consolidated, post-116, FY27E, ₹ crore):

Peer CMP (₹) Diluted shares (cr) Debt + leases Cash & liquid inv. NCI EBITDA EBIT PAT (owners) Book equity (owners)
A 1,240 8.0 900 300 50 1,050 800 520 3,400
B 560 30.0 2,100 400 0 1,900 1,350 820 5,800
C 3,150 2.5 150 700 0 690 560 470 2,600
D 210 55.0 3,600 250 300 1,500 900 360 4,100
E 880 12.0 600 200 20 780 600 400 2,900

(a) Compute the EV, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, P/E and P/B for each peer, and the mean, median, Q1 and Q3. (b) Identify any outliers and justify treating them as n.m. or keeping them. (c) Derive Shakti's implied price range from the EV/EBITDA IQR and the P/E IQR. (d) Compare the result with the DCF price of ₹ 550 and a CMP of ₹ 405, and explain the divergences.

💡 Hint: Compute EV once with the c01 recipe and reuse it for all three EV multiples.

Q2. Retail comps on two Ind AS 116 bases [L3]

For a value-fashion retailer peer set (choose four NSE-listed retailers from s01 and assume illustrative data), design a comps sheet that shows EV/EBITDA on both post-116 and pre-116 bases. List the data needed for each peer (lease liabilities, lease interest, ROU depreciation, cash rent). Write the formulas for both bases. Explain why an owned-store model like DMart's will look relatively cheaper on post-116 EV/EBITDA than on pre-116.

Q3. Precedents and SEBI SAST floor [L2]

An acquirer agrees to buy a 45% promoter stake in an NSE-listed specialty chemicals company at ₹ 1,450 per share. Over the preceding 60 trading days the VWAP was ₹ 1,180. The highest price the acquirer paid in the past 26 weeks was ₹ 1,300, and its 52-week VWAP of purchases was ₹ 1,220. The shares are frequently traded. Determine the minimum open-offer price under SAST Regulation 8, the control premium over the unaffected price (60-day VWAP) and the implied deal EV/EBITDA (EBITDA LTM ₹ 420 crore, net debt ₹ 300 crore, 12 crore diluted shares). Explain how you would use this deal in a precedents table.

Q4. Football field and triangulation memo [L3]

Using a DCF Gordon grid range of ₹ 471–659 (WACC ±100 bp at base g), a DCF exit-multiple range of ₹ 504–595, trading-comps EV/EBITDA IQR of ₹ 430–610, precedents of ₹ 560–720, and a 52-week range of ₹ 352–468 with CMP at ₹ 405, specify the football field chart (series, order, colours, CMP line). Write a 200-word triangulation memo explaining the divergences and recommending a valuation range.
