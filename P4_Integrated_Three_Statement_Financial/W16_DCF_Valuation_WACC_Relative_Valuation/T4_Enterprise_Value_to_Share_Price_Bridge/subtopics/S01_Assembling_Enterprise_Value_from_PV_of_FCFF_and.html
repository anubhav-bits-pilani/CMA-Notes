Subtopic note

Assembling Enterprise Value from PV of FCFF and Terminal Value

Scope: EV = Σ PV(FCFF) + PV(TV)

Why it matters

Enterprise value is the hand-off point between the DCF engine (FCFF, WACC, terminal value) and the equity bridge. Every downstream number depends on it: implied share price, upside, implied multiples and the football field. In review, the EV cell is where you check that the discount factors, the stub fraction and the terminal-value timing are consistent. In FP&A work, the same summation drives capital-budgeting NPV and the "value created" metrics used in long-range plans. A clean EV block, one that shows each component and its share of total, also lets you see immediately when terminal value dominates the answer.

Core notes

Definition

Enterprise value is the present value at the valuation date of all future free cash flows available to all capital providers (debt, lease, preference and equity), discounted at WACC:

\[ EV_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{FCFF_t}{(1+WACC)^{e_t}} + \frac{TV_n}{(1+WACC)^{e_{TV}}} \]

where \(FCFF_t\) is free cash flow to firm in forecast period \(t\), \(e_t\) is the discounting exponent for that period (it depends on the stub and the mid-year convention), \(TV_n\) is the terminal value at the end of the explicit horizon, and \(e_{TV}\) is the exponent used for the terminal value.

EV is an operating value. It contains nothing for surplus cash, liquid investments, associates or idle land, because their income was excluded from EBIT and from FCFF. That is why the bridge adds them later.

Components

Component What it captures Typical share of EV for an Indian mid-cap
Σ PV(FCFF), explicit period Value from the 5–10 year forecast 25–45%
PV(TV) Value of all cash flows beyond year n 55–75%

A terminal-value share above roughly 75–80% signals that the explicit forecast does not reach steady state. It is not a mistake as such, but it should be disclosed and stress-tested.

Discounting exponents

These are the exponents for a valuation date that is a fraction \(s\) of a year before the first forecast year-end (the stub), with the mid-year convention:

Period Cash flow used Exponent \(e_t\)
Stub (valuation date → 31 March) \(s \times FCFF_{FY1}\) (or actual remaining) \(s/2\)
First full year \(FCFF_{FY2}\) \(s + 0.5\)
Second full year \(FCFF_{FY3}\) \(s + 1.5\)
Terminal value \(TV_n\) End-year view: \(s + N\); mid-year view (Gordon on mid-year flows): \(s + N - 0.5\), where \(N\) = number of full forecast years

Practitioners differ on TV timing. One view keeps Gordon TV consistent with mid-year flows by discounting it at the last cash flow's mid-year exponent. The other discounts at the end-year exponent and accepts a small conservatism. An exit-multiple TV is a point-in-time price at the end of year n and is discounted at the full end-year exponent. Whichever view you take, state it in the model and apply it consistently. The topic t3 notes cover this in detail.

Worked illustration

Shakti Auto Components has valuation date 30 September 2026, so \(s = 0.5\). WACC is 12.0%. The model forecasts to FY31.

₹ crore Stub FY27 (H2) FY28 FY29 FY30 FY31
FCFF 380 820 900 980 1,050
Exponent 0.25 1.0 2.0 3.0 4.0
Discount factor 0.9721 0.8929 0.7972 0.7118 0.6355
PV 369 732 718 698 667

Σ PV(FCFF) = ₹ 3,184 crore. With \(g = 5.5\%\), \(TV_{FY31} = 1{,}050 \times 1.055 \div (0.12 - 0.055)\) = ₹ 17,042 crore. Discounting at the end-year exponent of 4.5 gives a factor of \(1.12^{-4.5} = 0.6005\), so PV(TV) = ₹ 10,234 crore. Discounting at the mid-year exponent of 4.0 gives ₹ 10,830 crore. Using the end-year figure, EV = 3,184 + 10,234 = ₹ 13,418 crore, and TV is 76% of EV.

The ₹ 596 crore gap between the two TV timings shows why the convention matters and why it must be stated.

Excel implementation

Put the timing row and discount factors on the DCF sheet, driven by named inputs, so the sensitivity tables in topic t5 flex correctly.

Stub fraction  s:      =YEARFRAC(Val_Date, FY1_End, 1)
Exponent (stub):       =s/2
Exponent (full yrs):   =s + (COLUMN()-COLUMN($F$10)) + 0.5   'F10 = first full year column
Discount factor:       =1/(1+WACC)^F12
PV of FCFF:            =F11*F13
Sum of PV:             =SUMPRODUCT(E11:I11, E13:I13)
PV of TV:              =TV/(1+WACC)^TV_Exponent
EV:                    =Sum_PV_FCFF + PV_TV
TV % of EV:            =PV_TV/EV

An alternative for irregular dates is =XNPV(WACC, values, dates), using a cash-flow date column set at the mid-point of each period. XNPV discounts the first value at the first date, so put a zero cash flow on the valuation date as the anchor. XNPV uses actual/365 day counts. Small differences from the exponent approach are expected and should be documented.

Presentation

The valuation summary should show, in order:

  1. Σ PV(FCFF)
  2. PV(TV)
  3. EV
  4. TV % of EV
  5. Implied EV/EBITDA (LTM and FY+1)

Showing implied multiples next to EV catches errors before the bridge.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Negative early FCFF (capex-heavy expansion) is fine. These flows reduce EV, but check that the terminal year reflects the payoff from that capex.
  • Valuation date after the first forecast year-end. Re-base the timeline. Never discount actual historical cash flows.
  • Cash flows by quarter or half-year for project-type businesses. Use XNPV with mid-period dates instead of annual exponents.
  • Mixed TV methods. If you present both Gordon and exit-multiple TVs, show two EVs, not an average hidden in one cell.
  • Currency. EV in INR requires INR FCFF and INR WACC. A USD-denominated export business still needs consistent conversion (topic t2, c06).
  • Circularity. If WACC weights are derived from the DCF's own equity value, EV becomes circular. Use a target capital structure, or a controlled iteration with a breaker.

Key takeaways

  • EV is the PV of operating cash flows to all capital providers. It excludes non-operating assets by construction.
  • Exponents must reflect the stub and the mid-year convention. The TV exponent is a stated judgement.
  • TV usually dominates Indian DCFs. Show TV % of EV and investigate anything above about 75–80%.
  • Keep WACC, stub fraction and TV inputs as single named cells on the DCF sheet so data tables flex.
  • Show implied EV/EBITDA next to EV as an immediate reasonableness check.

Practice Questions

Q1. EV from a five-year forecast with stub [L1]

Valuation date is 31 December 2026. FY ends 31 March. WACC is 11.0% and g is 5.0%. FCFF (₹ crore) is: Q4 FY27 stub 140; FY28 610; FY29 660; FY30 720; FY31 770. Using the mid-year convention, compute the exponents, Σ PV(FCFF), TV at FY31 (Gordon), PV(TV) under both end-year and mid-year exponent views, EV, and TV % of EV.

💡 Hint: The stub fraction is 0.25, so the first full-year exponent is 0.75.

Q2. XNPV vs exponent method reconciliation [L2]

Rebuild Q1 using XNPV with a zero anchor on the valuation date and cash-flow dates at the mid-point of each period. Explain why the result differs slightly from the exponent approach, and which assumptions (day count, mid-point definition) drive the difference.

Q3. Terminal-value dominance review [L2]

A draft model for a capital-goods company shows TV at 88% of EV, with explicit FCFF negative in the first three years because of a greenfield plant. List the review questions you would raise. Describe two model changes (horizon extension, fade period, terminal normalisation) that could make the EV more defensible, and say what each does to TV %.

Q4. Interview: why not just use EBITDA × multiple? [L1]

An interviewer asks why you assemble EV from discounted FCFF rather than applying a peer EV/EBITDA multiple directly. Give a structured answer covering what each approach captures, when they should converge, and why you would still present both.
