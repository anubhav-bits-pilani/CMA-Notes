Roadmap topic: Terminal Value Methods: Gordon Growth Model (perpetuity growth rate capped below nominal GDP) vs Exit Multiple Method (Terminal EBITDA × EV/EBITDA) + implied growth sanity check

Overview

The terminal value captures everything a business is worth beyond the explicit forecast — and in Indian DCFs that is usually 60–85% of enterprise value. Two methods dominate. The Gordon Growth (perpetuity growth) method capitalises a normalised steady-state FCFF at \( \text{WACC} - g \); it is intrinsic and transparent, but very sensitive to \( g \) and WACC. The exit multiple method applies a market EV/EBITDA multiple to terminal EBITDA; it is market-anchored and favoured in PE and M&A, but it imports today's market sentiment into a valuation meant to describe a mature business years from now. Neither is complete alone. Practitioners compute both and reconcile them using the implied growth in the exit TV and the implied multiple in the Gordon TV.

For FP&A, equity research and transaction roles, this topic is where valuation judgement is most visible. Investment committees and auditors challenge the perpetuity growth rate (is it below long-run nominal GDP and consistent with the risk-free rate?), the exit multiple (is it a mature-state multiple, or a 50× consumer multiple perpetuated?), the terminal-year normalisation (steady margins, capex ≥ D&A, NWC growing with revenue), and the economic coherence of growth and reinvestment (the value-driver formula). Mechanics matter too: under the mid-year convention, Gordon and exit TVs are discounted with different exponents.

The learning sequence: build the Gordon TV on a normalised year-n+1 FCFF (s01, c01) → choose g with INR inflation, real growth and caps (s02) → normalise the terminal year (s05) → link g, RONIC and reinvestment via the value-driver formula and report TV share of EV (s06, c02) → build the exit-multiple TV with a mature-state multiple (s03, c03) → reconcile via implied g and implied multiple (s04, c04) → discount both correctly under mid-year (s07, c05).

Related concepts to study

Week 16 trading comps supply the exit multiple

→ Week 16 (w16_t6): comps supply the exit multiple

The exit multiple must come from a properly constructed comparable set: the same peers used for beta, with EV defined identically to the DCF bridge (diluted market cap + debt + leases if post-116 + preference + NCI − cash − non-operating investments) and EBITDA normalised for Ind AS 116 basis, exceptional items and other income on a consolidated basis. The comps produce current LTM and forward medians and quartiles; for a terminal value, the analyst then adjusts toward a mature-state level. Keeping the exit multiple as a linked driver from the comps sheet (with a selection switch) ensures the football field, sensitivity grids and DCF tell a consistent story.

Phase 2: ROIC and competitive advantage period

→ Phase 2 (W5–8): ROIC and competitive advantage period

Phase 2's ROIC analysis — NOPAT over invested capital, decomposed into margin and capital turnover — supplies the RONIC assumption for the value-driver formula. The competitive advantage period (CAP) is the length of time a company can earn ROIC above WACC before competition erodes it. A long CAP justifies a longer explicit/fade period and a terminal RONIC modestly above WACC; a short CAP suggests RONIC ≈ WACC at the terminal year. Indian examples: entrenched distribution networks (paints, adhesives, FMCG) support long CAPs; commodity chemicals and new-entrant-exposed sectors shorter ones. Linking TV to ROIC analysis converts "g" from a guess into an economic argument.

Damodaran three-stage models

Damodaran three-stage models (high growth → transition → stable)

Damodaran's three-stage FCFF model has a high-growth phase (company-specific growth, high RONIC, high reinvestment), a transition phase in which growth, RONIC, beta and leverage converge linearly to stable-state values, and a stable phase valued with Gordon. In INR, stable growth is capped at the stripped risk-free rate, beta converges toward 1, and RONIC toward WACC (or a modest premium). This is precisely the explicit + fade + terminal structure in t1/s04. Its advantages: it avoids the "cliff" from high growth straight into a perpetuity and makes each convergence assumption auditable. Damodaran's free spreadsheets (fcffginzu) implement it and are useful templates to compare against your model.

Phase 6: synergies and terminal assumptions in M&A

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): synergy and terminal assumptions in M&A

In M&A, terminal value assumptions drive both standalone value and synergy value. Cost synergies are often perpetuated in TV; revenue synergies are riskier and usually faded or excluded. Using the acquirer's high trading multiple as the exit multiple for the target inflates value; using a precedent transaction multiple at exit double-counts the control premium. SEBI SAST open-offer pricing (t6) and fairness opinions require the valuer to justify terminal assumptions separately for standalone and synergy cash flows. Phase 6 will reuse the reconciliation tools here — implied growth, implied multiples, TV share — on deal models.

Common pitfalls

g at or above WACC, or near nominal GDP

g ≥ WACC, or g set near India's ~10–11% nominal GDP in perpetuity

The mistake: perpetuity g of 8–10% because "India's nominal GDP grows at 10–11%", or a sensitivity grid that includes g ≥ WACC. Why it happens: strong current Indian growth and a desire to justify high market prices. Correct treatment: g must be below long-run (decades-ahead) nominal GDP growth, typically 4–6% in INR for most companies; within Damodaran's cap g ≤ Rf (≈ 4.4% stripped, ≈ 6.5% full G-Sec, consistent with the Ke convention); built from inflation (≈ 4%) plus modest real growth. Extend the explicit/fade period if the company has a long runway. Detection: TV/FCFF\(_{n+1}\) above 25–30×; implied multiple far above mature peers; formula errors or negative TVs in grids; g input above the Rf input.

Terminal capex below D&A with positive growth

Terminal capex below D&A while assuming positive growth

The mistake: the terminal year has capex ≤ D&A (often because the explicit forecast ends after a capex cycle) while g is 5–6%. Why it happens: the final explicit year is copied forward; capex guidance from management covers only the near term. Correct treatment: with positive nominal growth, capex must exceed D&A (replacement at inflated cost plus growth investment); set net reinvestment = NOPAT\(_{n+1}\) × g ÷ RONIC (value-driver), and ΔNWC = NWC% × ΔRevenue. Detection: a check row showing terminal capex/D&A < 1, or implied RONIC (g ÷ reinvestment rate) above ~30–40%; FCFF\(_{n+1}\) ≈ NOPAT\(_{n+1}\) despite positive g.

Perpetuating elevated Indian consumer multiples

Perpetuating today's elevated Indian consumer multiples (50×+ EBITDA) as exit multiple

The mistake: applying today's peer median of 50–70× EV/EBITDA (common for Indian paints, adhesives, retail, QSR and consumer durables leaders) to year-10 EBITDA. Why it happens: the comps sheet shows it as "the market multiple"; it yields valuations close to the current price, which feels validating. Correct treatment: today's multiple prices in decades of above-GDP growth; at the terminal year the company should be in steady state, deserving a mature-state multiple — derived from the Gordon-implied multiple, mature global peers (often 15–20× for quality consumer franchises) or a faded multiple. Detection: implied perpetuity growth of the exit TV above 7–8% or close to WACC; exit TV more than 1.5–2× the Gordon TV; TV share of EV above 90%.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full TV build and reconciliation [L3]

An Indian manufacturing company's FY30E (n = 5) data (₹ crore): revenue 9,000; EBITDA 1,620; D&A 360; capex 330; ΔNWC −30 (one-off inventory release); tax rate 22% (last year of a SEZ benefit); NWC 15% of revenue; net fixed assets 3,600. Assumptions: WACC 11.8%; g 5%; RONIC 14%; peer current EV/EBITDA 18×; mature-state 11×; valuation date 31 March 2025; mid-year convention. (a) Build a normalised FY31 FCFF (state each normalisation). (b) Compute Gordon TV (plain and value-driver). (c) Compute exit TVs at 18× and 11×. (d) Compute implied g for each exit TV and the implied multiple of the Gordon TV, aligned in time. (e) Discount each TV correctly and recommend a TV with reasons.

💡 Hint: Put all TVs at the end of FY30 before comparing; discount Gordon at 4.5 and exit at 5.

Q2. Defending terminal assumptions in a Saturday case [L3]

You have valued a listed Indian auto-component maker with a Gordon TV at g = 5.5% (TV share 72%) and an exit multiple of 12× (implied g 6.4%). The panel challenges: (i) "Why not 8% — India grows faster?"; (ii) "Why is your exit multiple half the current peer median?"; (iii) "Your TV is 72% of EV — isn't the DCF just a guess?". Prepare structured answers with supporting calculations you would show.

Q3. Review of a consumer-brand DCF [L2]

A sell-side DCF of a paints company uses a 5-year horizon, exit multiple 55× FY30 EBITDA, discounts the exit TV at 4.5 years, and shows TV share of 93%. Write a review note identifying each issue, quantifying the timing error at WACC 11%, and proposing a revised structure (horizon, fade, TV method and cross-checks).

Q4. Value-driver sensitivity grid [L2]

NOPAT\(_{n+1}\) ₹1,800 crore; WACC 11.5%. Build a grid of TV for g ∈ {4%, 5%, 6%} and RONIC ∈ {11.5%, 15%, 20%, 30%}. Interpret the pattern and state which assumptions you consider defensible for (a) a cement company and (b) a leading adhesives company.

Q5. Interview round [L1]

Answer briefly: (a) Why must g be below WACC? (b) What is the Damodaran cap on g and why? (c) Why does the exit TV get discounted at n but the Gordon TV at n − 0.5 under mid-year? (d) What does TV share of EV tell you? (e) What is RONIC and why set it near WACC?
