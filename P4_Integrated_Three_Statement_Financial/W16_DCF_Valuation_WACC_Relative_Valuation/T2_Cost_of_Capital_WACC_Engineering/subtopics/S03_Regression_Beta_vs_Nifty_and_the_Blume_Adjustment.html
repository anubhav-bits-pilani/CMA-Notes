Subtopic note

Regression Beta vs Nifty and the Blume Adjustment

Scope: Beta: regression of stock vs Nifty 50/Nifty 500 (2-year weekly or 5-year monthly returns); Blume adjustment

Why it matters

Beta scales the ERP into a company-specific equity premium. Between a beta of 0.7 (typical of staples) and 1.3 (capital goods, metals), Ke differs by about 4.4 percentage points at a 7.3% ERP — more than any other single input variation. Beta is also the most "computable" input, which tempts analysts to treat a terminal-screen number as fact. A reviewer will ask: which index, which frequency, which window, raw or adjusted, and does it make sense versus peers? This subtopic covers how to estimate a regression beta for an NSE-listed stock properly and when to replace it with a peer-based bottom-up beta (s04).

Core notes

The regression

Beta is the slope coefficient of the market model:

\[ R_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta \, R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_t \]

\[ \beta = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)} \]

where \( R_{i,t} \) is the stock's return and \( R_{m,t} \) the market index return over period \( t \). Returns may be simple (\( P_t / P_{t-1} - 1 \)) or log returns; strictly they should be total returns (including dividends), but price returns are standard practice for beta because dividends barely affect covariance.

Choice of market index

Index Composition When to use
Nifty 50 50 large-caps, ~60%+ of free-float market cap Large-cap stocks; most common in sell-side
Nifty 500 Top 500 by market cap (~93–95% of market cap) Broader proxy for the market portfolio; better for mid/small caps
BSE Sensex / BSE 500 30 / 500 stocks on BSE Equivalent alternatives; use BSE for BSE-only listings

CAPM's market portfolio should be as broad as possible, which favours the Nifty 500. Whatever is chosen, use the same index for all peers and state it. Betas against Nifty 50 and Nifty 500 usually differ by less than 0.05 for large-caps but can diverge for mid-caps.

Frequency and window

Convention Observations Pros Cons
2-year weekly ~104 Recent business mix and leverage; enough points Weekly noise; day-of-week effects
5-year monthly 60 Smooths noise; common academic/data-vendor convention (Bloomberg's default is 2-year weekly) Business may have changed (merger, deleveraging); old regime
1-year daily ~250 Many observations Non-synchronous trading biases beta toward zero for illiquid stocks

Daily data are discouraged for anything but very liquid large-caps because of non-synchronous trading: an illiquid stock's price reacts to market news with a lag, depressing measured covariance. Weekly (Wednesday-to-Wednesday, avoiding Friday/Monday effects) or monthly returns mitigate this.

Check the regression statistics: R² (low R² means high idiosyncratic risk, not necessarily a bad beta), the standard error of beta (a beta of 0.9 with SE 0.25 has a 95% interval of roughly 0.4–1.4), and any structural breaks (merger, demerger, major acquisition).

Blume adjustment

Empirically, betas regress toward 1 over time (Blume, 1971/1975). The Blume (Bloomberg "adjusted beta") formula:

\[ \beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.67 \times \beta_{\text{raw}} + 0.33 \times 1.0 \]

Rationale: firms with extreme betas tend to become more average as they diversify and mature; also, estimation error means extreme estimates are more likely to be overshoot. The adjustment is a crude Bayesian shrinkage toward the prior mean of 1. Vasicek's adjustment generalises it, weighting by the estimation error of each beta relative to the cross-sectional dispersion.

When to apply: when using a single company's regression beta for forward-looking Ke, many practitioners apply Blume. When using a median of unlevered peer betas (s04), the averaging already reduces estimation error; applying Blume on top is a choice that should be stated. Do not apply Blume twice (e.g. on Bloomberg's already adjusted beta).

Worked illustration

Weekly returns, 104 weeks, for a listed paints company vs Nifty 500: Cov = 0.000412, Var(Nifty 500) = 0.000520.

\[ \beta_{\text{raw}} = \frac{0.000412}{0.000520} = 0.792; \quad \beta_{\text{Blume}} = 0.67 \times 0.792 + 0.33 = 0.861 \]

R² = 0.31, SE(β) = 0.11. The 95% interval is roughly 0.57–1.01, so a peer cross-check is still useful.

Excel implementation

B3:B107   weekly closing prices of stock (Wednesdays)
C3:C107   weekly closing prices of Nifty 500
D4        =B4/B3-1                                      ' stock return
E4        =C4/C3-1                                      ' index return
H2        =SLOPE(D4:D107,E4:E107)                        ' raw beta
H3        =COVARIANCE.S(D4:D107,E4:E107)/VAR.S(E4:E107)  ' same, check
H4        =RSQ(D4:D107,E4:E107)                          ' R-squared
H5        =INDEX(LINEST(D4:D107,E4:E107,TRUE,TRUE),2,1)  ' SE of beta
H6        =0.67*H2+0.33                                  ' Blume-adjusted beta

Note the argument order of SLOPE(known_y's, known_x's): stock first, index second. Reversing it returns a meaningless number. Pull prices via Power Query from NSE historical data downloads, aligning dates with a merge on the date column so that missing trading days in one series don't shift the other.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Recently listed companies (less than two years of data): use peer betas; a 6-month beta is meaningless.
  • Illiquid / low free-float stocks (PSU subsidiaries with 75% promoter holding, SME-platform stocks): betas biased toward zero — use bottom-up peer beta.
  • Corporate actions: use adjusted prices for splits/bonuses; demergers create discontinuities — begin the window after the event.
  • Conglomerates (Reliance, ITC, L&T): the regression beta is a blend of segments; for sum-of-the-parts, use segment-specific peer betas.
  • Market-index heavyweights: a stock with a large index weight (e.g. HDFC Bank, Reliance) partly regresses on itself; Nifty 500 dilutes this.
  • Negative or near-zero betas (gold financiers in crises) are rarely forward-looking; use sector betas.

Key takeaways

  • Beta = Cov(stock, market) ÷ Var(market); estimate with weekly (2-year) or monthly (5-year) returns against Nifty 50 or Nifty 500.
  • Avoid daily data for illiquid stocks; check R², standard error and structural breaks.
  • Blume: 0.67 × raw + 0.33, a shrinkage toward 1; apply once and say so.
  • =SLOPE(stock, index) — order matters; cross-check with COVARIANCE.S/VAR.S.
  • A single-stock regression beta should always be cross-checked against a peer-based beta.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute and adjust [L1]

Monthly returns over 60 months give Cov(stock, Nifty 50) = 0.00285 and Var(Nifty 50) = 0.00228. Compute raw beta and Blume-adjusted beta, and write the Excel formulas you would use if the return series sat in D2:D61 (stock) and E2:E61 (index).

Q2. Window and frequency choice [L2]

A specialty chemicals company completed a large debt-funded acquisition 20 months before the valuation date, raising D/E from 0.1 to 0.6. Its 5-year monthly beta is 0.85 and its 2-year weekly beta (which includes 20 post-acquisition months) is 1.12. Discuss which estimate is more relevant, what other evidence you would gather, and how unlevering/relevering (s04) might reconcile the two.

💡 Hint: Think about which window reflects the current business mix and capital structure.

Q3. Illiquid stock [L2]

A small-cap industrial stock trades on fewer than 60% of days, with a 1-year daily beta of 0.38 (R² 0.04) and a 2-year weekly beta of 0.71 (R² 0.12). Peers' median relevered beta is 1.05. Recommend the beta for Ke and explain the statistical reasons.

Q4. Build exercise [L3]

Describe a Power Query + Excel workflow to (a) download two years of weekly prices for five NSE peers and the Nifty 500, (b) align dates, (c) compute raw and Blume betas with R² and standard errors, and (d) present a beta summary table that updates when the valuation date input changes.
