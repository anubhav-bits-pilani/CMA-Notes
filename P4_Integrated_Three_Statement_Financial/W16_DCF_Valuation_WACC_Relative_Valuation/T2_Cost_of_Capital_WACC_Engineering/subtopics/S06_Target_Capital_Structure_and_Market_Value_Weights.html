Subtopic note

Target Capital Structure and Market-Value Weights

Scope: Target capital structure: peer median D/(D+E), management target, market-value weights

Why it matters

WACC weights tell the model how much of the firm's financing comes from debt (cheaper, tax-advantaged) and how much from equity (expensive). A 10-point change in D/(D+E) shifts WACC by roughly 40–60 bp for a typical Indian company — and also changes the relevered beta, so the effect is partly offsetting. The conceptual question is which capital structure: today's, the company's book-value structure, its management target, or an industry norm? Because a DCF values the business over decades, the weights should reflect the long-run target structure expected to prevail, measured in market values. Reviewers flag book-value weights immediately, and investment committees ask why the WACC weights differ from the debt schedule's trajectory.

Core notes

Why market-value weights

WACC is the opportunity cost of capital that investors require today. Investors' claims are worth their market values: equity at market capitalisation, debt at market value (approximated by book for most investment-grade debt). Book equity in Indian balance sheets can diverge massively from market value — Asian Paints trades at well over 10× book; a PSU bank may trade below book. Using book weights for a high P/B company overstates the debt weight and so lowers WACC artificially, because cheaper after-tax debt is over-weighted; for a low P/B company the bias runs the other way.

\[ w_D = \frac{D}{D + E}, \quad w_E = \frac{E}{D + E} \]

with \( E \) = diluted market cap and \( D \) = market value of interest-bearing debt (plus lease liabilities if post-116, s07; plus preference capital if any, with its own cost).

Three candidate structures

1. Current market-value structure. Today's D/(D+E). Appropriate when the company is at or near its long-run target and leverage is stable. Weakness: momentary — a share price rally lowers \( w_D \) and changes WACC.

2. Management target. Stated in board-approved capital-allocation policies, earnings call guidance ("net debt/EBITDA below 1.5×", "net debt-free by FY27"), rating-agency commitments or debt covenants. Appropriate when credible and consistent with the debt schedule. Convert a leverage target to a D/(D+E) weight: e.g. net debt/EBITDA of 1.5× at an EV/EBITDA of 12× implies D/(D+E) ≈ 1.5/12 = 12.5% (ignoring cash differences).

3. Peer median. Median market D/(D+E) of the comparable set used for beta. Appropriate for unlisted targets, for acquisition valuations (a buyer will impose an optimal structure), and as a cross-check when management targets are vague. Because the unlevered beta came from the same peers, relevering at the peer median is internally consistent.

Decision framework

Situation Recommended weights
Listed, mature, leverage stable Current market weights, cross-checked to peer median
Listed, deleveraging/levering with credible plan Management target (and consider year-specific WACC if transition is long)
Unlisted target / IPO Peer median, adjusted for company specifics
Acquisition (buyer's perspective) Buyer's target structure for the asset (control value)
Distressed Current structure inappropriate; use APV or restructured target

Consistency requirements

  • Beta relevering must use the same target D/E (\( D/E = w_D / w_E \)) as the WACC weights.
  • Kd must reflect the rating consistent with the target leverage — a company moving from 10% to 40% debt will not keep its AAA spread.
  • Debt schedule vs weights: if the forecast shows debt falling to zero while WACC assumes 25% debt, the valuation implicitly assumes a re-levering in the terminal period. That is acceptable (WACC reflects the long-run financing policy) but must be stated; otherwise use a time-varying WACC.
  • Cash: WACC weights generally use gross debt, consistent with deducting gross debt and adding cash in the bridge; some practitioners use net debt for weights — choose one and align it with cash-adjusted betas.

Circularity of market weights

Equity value is the DCF output, and WACC depends on equity value — a circularity if you use the DCF's own implied equity value for weights. Avoid by using the current market cap (listed), a target ratio (preferred), or iterate deliberately with a circularity breaker.

Worked illustration

Listed specialty chemicals company: market cap ₹24,000 crore; borrowings ₹2,800 crore; book equity ₹6,500 crore; management target net debt/EBITDA 1.0× (EV/EBITDA 16× → D/(D+E) ≈ 6%); peer median D/(D+E) 9%.

Basis D/(D+E)
Book 2,800 ÷ (2,800 + 6,500) = 30.1%
Current market 2,800 ÷ (2,800 + 24,000) = 10.4%
Management target ≈ 6%
Peer median 9%

With Ke = 13.0% and after-tax Kd = 6.6%, WACC is 11.1% on book weights versus 12.3% on current market weights — a 120 bp difference. A defensible choice is 8–10% (between target and peer median), giving WACC of about 12.4% after relevering beta at that target.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Net-cash companies (much of Indian IT/FMCG): target D/(D+E) may be ~0; WACC ≈ Ke. Do not impose peer leverage the company has rejected for years without explicit justification.
  • Promoter-pledged / group-leverage structures: holding-company debt is outside the entity; do not include it in the operating company's weights.
  • Preference capital and hybrid instruments (CCPS in startups): include as a separate weight with their own cost, or treat CCPS as equity on an as-converted basis.
  • Regulated businesses (power transmission under CERC norms of 70:30 debt:equity) — the regulatory normative structure is a natural target.
  • Market value of debt below book (distressed bonds): use market value for weights.

Key takeaways

  • Use market-value weights reflecting the long-run target capital structure.
  • Candidates: current market, management target, peer median — choose by situation and document.
  • Book weights are wrong for valuation, especially for high P/B Indian companies.
  • Relever beta and set Kd consistently with the chosen target leverage.
  • Avoid circularity by using market cap or a target ratio, not the DCF's own output.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute weights and WACC [L1]

Market cap ₹45,000 crore; borrowings ₹9,000 crore; book equity ₹18,000 crore; Ke 12.8%; pre-tax Kd 8.6%; tax 25.17%. Compute WACC on book and market weights and explain which is correct.

Q2. Convert a leverage target [L2]

Management guides to "net debt/EBITDA of 2.0× through the cycle". The company's EV/EBITDA is 9×, cash is negligible, unlevered beta is 0.85, Rf (Damodaran) 4.4%, ERP 7.3%, pre-tax Kd at the corresponding rating 9.0%, tax 25.17%. Derive the target D/(D+E), relevered beta and WACC.

💡 Hint: Net debt/EBITDA ÷ EV/EBITDA approximates D/EV; then convert to D/E for relevering.

Q3. Weights vs debt schedule [L3]

Your Project 4 debt schedule repays all borrowings by FY29, yet your WACC uses a 20% target debt weight. The investment committee asks whether this is inconsistent. Prepare a reasoned answer, describing an alternative time-varying WACC structure and its effect on the discount factors.

Q4. Unlisted target in a buyout [L2]

A PE fund buys an unlisted pharma CDMO and plans 45% debt funding at acquisition, falling to 25% in five years. Peers' median D/(D+E) is 12%. Discuss which weights to use for (a) the fund's investment-case DCF and (b) a fair value opinion for minority shareholders, and why they may differ.
