Subtopic note

Risk-Free Rate from the 10-Year Benchmark G-Sec

Scope: Risk-free rate: 10-year benchmark G-Sec yield (FBIL/CCIL/RBI) on valuation date

Why it matters

The risk-free rate is the anchor of every discount rate: it enters Ke directly (CAPM), it is the base over which corporate credit spreads are added for Kd, and it caps the perpetuity growth rate in the Damodaran framework (t3). A 50 bp change in Rf moves WACC by roughly 40–50 bp and, for a typical Indian company valued at 12% WACC with 6% terminal growth, moves EV by 6–9%. Valuation reviewers check three things: the instrument (10-year benchmark Government of India security), the source (FBIL, CCIL or RBI data), and the date (the valuation date, not a stale month-end or an average chosen to suit the answer). In Indian practice, the Rf is also the most common point of currency inconsistency — using a US Treasury yield with INR cash flows is still seen in reports.

Core notes

What a risk-free rate must satisfy

A risk-free rate for discounting must be (i) free of default risk, (ii) free of reinvestment risk over the horizon, and (iii) in the same currency as the cash flows. For INR cash flows, the natural candidate is the yield on Government of India rupee securities (G-Secs). Practitioners take the 10-year benchmark because:

  • It is the most liquid point on the Indian sovereign curve (it dominates NDS-OM secondary-market trading volumes).
  • Its duration roughly matches the weighted average timing of DCF cash flows for a going concern.
  • Market betas and ERPs (Damodaran and others) are conventionally estimated relative to long-term government yields.

Longer tenors (14-, 30- or 40-year G-Secs) can be used for long-life infrastructure assets, but must then be used consistently with an ERP estimated relative to the same tenor.

Sources and data conventions

Source What it publishes Use
FBIL (Financial Benchmarks India Ltd) Daily G-Sec par yield curve and valuation prices for G-Secs, used by banks/MFs for mark-to-market Authoritative benchmark-yield source; FBIL is RBI-recognised administrator
CCIL (Clearing Corporation of India) Trade data from NDS-OM, benchmark yields and the CCIL tenor-wise yield curve Traded yield on the valuation date
RBI (DBIE database, Weekly Statistical Supplement, Handbook) Historical yields, weighted average yields on auction Historical series, cross-check
NSE / Bloomberg / Refinitiv Market quotes (e.g. "IN10YT") Convenient; confirm against FBIL/CCIL

The 10-year benchmark is a specific security that RBI designates by issuing a new 10-year paper periodically (e.g. the 6.79% GS 2034 was benchmark during 2024, succeeded by the 6.33% GS 2035 in 2025). The yield, not the coupon, is the Rf. Yields are quoted on a semi-annual compounding basis; strictly, convert to annual effective:

\[ r_{\text{annual}} = \left(1 + \frac{y_{\text{semi}}}{2}\right)^2 - 1 \]

At 6.50%, the annual effective rate is 6.61%. Many practitioners ignore this 10 bp difference; it should be applied consistently (cash-flow discounting is annual).

Spot vs normalised Rf

  • Spot (valuation-date) yield — the default under ICAI Valuation Standards and in transaction valuations: value is a point-in-time market estimate and should use market inputs as at the valuation date.
  • Normalised Rf — some valuers use a 3- or 6-month average, or a longer-term normalised yield, when the spot yield is distorted (e.g. after a policy shock). If used, justify it, and remember that the ERP and Rf must be estimated on a consistent basis (an implied ERP computed with spot yield is inconsistent with a normalised Rf).

For context (date-sensitive, verify on the valuation date): the 10-year G-Sec traded roughly between 6.2% and 7.2% during 2024–2025, falling as RBI cut the repo rate from 6.50% to 5.50% in 2025 and as India's inclusion in global bond indices (JP Morgan GBI-EM from June 2024) added foreign demand.

Is the G-Sec yield truly risk-free?

India's sovereign rating is investment grade but not AAA (Moody's Baa3; S&P upgraded to BBB in August 2025; Fitch BBB−). Damodaran argues that the INR G-Sec yield contains a default spread and should be stripped to get a "true" Rf (t2/c05). Two internally consistent approaches:

  1. Local-market approach: Rf = full G-Sec yield; ERP = a local or mature-market ERP without a separate country risk premium (because country risk is partly in Rf).
  2. Damodaran approach: Rf = G-Sec yield − India sovereign default spread; ERP = mature-market ERP + India CRP.

Mixing the two — full G-Sec yield plus a CRP-loaded ERP — double-counts country risk (topic pitfall). This choice must be documented on the WACC tab.

Worked illustration

Valuation date 14 November 2025. FBIL par yield for the 10-year tenor: 6.52% (semi-annual). Annual effective: \( (1 + 0.0652/2)^2 - 1 = 6.63\% \). If the valuer adopts the Damodaran approach and India's sovereign default spread is 2.10%, the true INR Rf = \( 6.52\% - 2.10\% = 4.42\% \).

Excel implementation

WACC!C5  =6.52%                         ' 10Y G-Sec yield on valuation date (input, blue)
WACC!D5  "FBIL par yield 14-Nov-2025"   ' Source note beside every market input
WACC!C6  =(1+C5/2)^2-1                  ' Annual effective (optional)
WACC!C7  =IF(Rf_Method="Damodaran",C5-Sov_Default_Spread,C5)   ' Rf used

Keep a source/date column next to every market input on the WACC tab; reviewers expect it.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Valuation date on a holiday — use the last available trading day's yield.
  • Very long-life assets (40-year hydro concession) — consider the 30- or 40-year G-Sec, and an ERP consistent with that maturity.
  • USD cash flows (export-oriented IT, shipping) — use a US Treasury yield and USD ERP, or convert flows to INR; never mix (t2/c06).
  • State Development Loans (SDLs) trade 20–50 bp above G-Secs and are not the Rf.
  • T-bill yields are short-term and inappropriate for a long-horizon DCF (except in a term-structure approach).
  • Regulated returns (CERC tariff regulations specify their own return on equity) — a separate regime; do not confuse regulatory RoE with market Ke.

Key takeaways

  • Rf for INR flows = 10-year benchmark G-Sec yield on the valuation date, from FBIL/CCIL/RBI.
  • Use the yield, not the coupon; optionally convert semi-annual to annual effective.
  • Spot yield is the default; normalisation requires justification and consistent ERP.
  • Decide whether country risk sits in Rf or in ERP — never both.
  • Document source and date beside the input on the WACC tab.

Practice Questions

Q1. Convert and select [L1]

On the valuation date the FBIL 10-year par yield is 6.38% (semi-annual), the 91-day T-bill yields 5.45%, and the 30-year G-Sec yields 7.05%. For a DCF of an FMCG company with a 10-year explicit horizon, select the Rf, convert it to an annual effective rate, and explain why the other two are rejected.

Q2. Double-counting test [L2]

A report uses Rf = 6.6% (10-year G-Sec), beta 1.1, and ERP = 4.3% mature market + 2.9% India CRP. India's sovereign default spread is 2.0%. Compute Ke as reported, and Ke under each of the two internally consistent approaches. Comment on the size of the double count.

💡 Hint: Under the local-market approach, drop the CRP; under the Damodaran approach, strip the default spread from Rf.

Q3. Spot vs average [L3]

Six weeks before the valuation date, the 10-year yield spiked from 6.9% to 7.4% after a fiscal shock, then settled at 7.25% on the valuation date. The client asks you to use the 12-month average of 6.95% to "remove volatility". Draft your response, referencing what the valuation standards expect and the consistency requirements with ERP.

Q4. Source documentation [L1]

List the data you would record on the WACC tab for the risk-free rate (instrument, ISIN, source, date, compounding, adjustment) and explain why each matters for a reviewer.
