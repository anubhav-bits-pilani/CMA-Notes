Roadmap topic: Cost of Capital (WACC) Engineering: Indian 10-Yr G-Sec Risk-Free Rate, Equity Risk Premium, Unlevering & Relevering Peer Betas, Pre/Post-Tax Cost of Debt, Target Capital Structure

Overview

WACC is the discount rate that converts FCFF into enterprise value. It is the weighted blend of what equity holders and lenders require, at the company's long-run target capital structure, in the same currency and inflation regime as the cash flows. Because the terminal value is a perpetuity at \( \text{WACC} - g \), small changes in WACC have large valuation effects: at a 12% WACC and 6% growth, a 50 bp increase in WACC reduces terminal value by about 8%. The topic is therefore less about plugging numbers into a formula and more about engineering each input so it is sourced, dated, internally consistent and defensible in front of an auditor's valuation specialist, an investment committee or a tax officer reviewing a Rule 11UA report.

For roles in Indian FP&A, equity research, transaction advisory and corporate development, the practical skill set is: pull the 10-year G-Sec yield from FBIL/CCIL on the valuation date; choose and pair an ERP (Damodaran India total ERP with a stripped Rf, or a local-market ERP with the full G-Sec); estimate regression betas against Nifty 50/500, adjust them, and build a peer asset-beta table unlevered at market D/E and 25.17% tax; derive a marginal Kd from NCD yields, rating spreads or a synthetic rating; set market-value target weights; and, if FCFF is post-Ind AS 116, include lease liabilities in debt everywhere.

The learning sequence follows the formula: Rf (s01, c05) → ERP (s02) → beta (s03, c04) → peer unlevering/relevering (s04, c02) → Ke (c01) → Kd (s05) → weights (s06) → leases (s07) → WACC (c03), with currency conversion (c06) as the consistency check that ties INR rates to INR cash flows.

Related concepts to study

Phase 2: peer selection and leverage ratios

→ Phase 2 (W5–8): peer selection and leverage ratios

The beta table is only as good as the peer set. Phase 2 trained the criteria — business model, end-market, size, growth, margin structure, geography — and the leverage metrics (D/E, net debt/EBITDA, ICR) used in credit analysis. In WACC engineering these skills are reused directly: peers' market D/E and cash balances drive unlevering; ICR drives the synthetic rating; peer median D/(D+E) is a candidate target structure. Peer sets for beta and for trading comps (t6) should normally be the same list, so that the exit multiple, the asset beta and the target leverage are drawn from one coherent benchmark group.

Phase 6: hurdle rates and acquisition discount rates

→ Phase 6 (W21–24): hurdle rates in capital budgeting; acquisition discount rates

A company's WACC is the right hurdle only for projects with average risk and financed at the target structure. Phase 6 adapts it: a new-geography or new-technology project needs a divisional/project beta from pure-play peers; a project with dedicated non-recourse financing may be valued on FCFE or APV. For acquisitions, the discount rate reflects the target's risk (its asset beta) and the financing structure the buyer intends — not the acquirer's own WACC. Using the acquirer's lower WACC to value a riskier target is a classic source of overpayment. The mechanics built here — unlever, relever, weight — are exactly what Phase 6 applies.

CAPM assumptions, implied ERP and the Fama-French critique

CAPM assumptions, implied ERP, Fama-French critique

CAPM assumes mean-variance investors, a frictionless market, a single period, unlimited borrowing/lending at Rf and a market portfolio of all risky assets. Empirically, the security market line is flatter than CAPM predicts, and Fama-French (1992, 1993) showed that size and book-to-market explain returns beyond beta; later models add profitability and investment (five-factor) and momentum (Carhart). Indian studies find similar anomalies, but factor premia are unstable and rarely used in Indian valuation practice. Implied ERP (from Nifty 50 prices and expected cash flows) is the market-based alternative to historical ERP, and Damodaran's method is itself implied. Know the critiques to defend CAPM as a convention, not a law.

RBI policy transmission to the G-Sec curve and spreads

RBI policy rate transmission to G-Sec curve and corporate spreads

The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee sets the repo rate; liquidity operations (VRR/VRRR, OMOs, CRR changes) steer the overnight rate around it. Transmission to the 10-year G-Sec is partial: the long end also reflects fiscal supply (central and state borrowing calendars), inflation expectations, global yields and foreign flows (amplified since India's inclusion in JP Morgan's GBI-EM index from June 2024 and subsequent index inclusions). Corporate bond spreads widen when liquidity tightens or credit events occur (IL&FS 2018 widened AA/A spreads sharply). For WACC, this means Rf and Kd move together but not one-for-one, and a valuation done soon after a policy move must use valuation-date market yields rather than assume full pass-through.

Common pitfalls

Double-counting country risk or mixing currencies

Raw G-Sec yield plus full CRP-loaded ERP double-counts country risk; or USD ERP with INR flows

The mistake: Ke = 10-year G-Sec yield (which contains India's default spread) + β × Damodaran India total ERP (which adds a CRP built from that same default spread). Or the analyst uses US Treasury Rf and a US/mature ERP while discounting INR cash flows. Why it happens: each input is individually "correct" and well-sourced, so the combination isn't questioned. Correct treatment: pair Damodaran's total ERP with G-Sec less default spread (c05), or use the full G-Sec with an ERP that has no separate CRP. Keep all rates in INR for INR flows; convert using inflation differentials (c06) if starting from USD rates. Detection: Ke above ~14% for an average-beta Indian large-cap usually signals the double count; check the WACC tab for an "Rf method" note and that Rf and ERP sources match.

Book or current weights when a target is intended

Book-value or current weights when a target structure is intended

The mistake: WACC weights use balance-sheet book equity and debt, or today's market weights for a company in transition, while the stated intention is a long-run target structure. Why it happens: book values are on the trial balance and easy to link; current market weights look "objective". Correct treatment: use market-value weights at the long-run target (management target, peer median, or current if already at target), relever beta at the same D/E and set Kd at the rating consistent with that leverage (s06). Detection: compare \( w_D \) with D ÷ (D + market cap); high-P/B companies with book-weight WACC will show debt weights several times their market weight; check that the relevering D/E equals \( w_D / w_E \).

Unvalidated raw beta from illiquid or short data

Raw beta from illiquid stock or short window without peer cross-check

The mistake: using a 1-year daily beta of 0.35 for a thinly traded small-cap, or a 6-month beta for a recent IPO, directly in Ke. Why it happens: terminals display a beta for every ticker; the low number produces a pleasingly high valuation. Correct treatment: use 2-year weekly or 5-year monthly returns; check R², standard error and trading frequency; build a peer asset-beta table and relever (s04); apply Blume consistently. If the company's own beta is unreliable, rely on the peer beta. Detection: R² below ~0.1, wide standard error, beta far from the peer median, low traded-days ratio, or listing history shorter than the window.

Practice Questions

Q1. Full WACC build for an Indian manufacturer [L3]

Valuation date 14 November 2025. Data: 10-year G-Sec 6.52% (FBIL); Damodaran January update: mature ERP 4.33%, India default spread 2.10%, India CRP 2.92%. Subject: listed auto-component maker, market cap ₹18,500 crore, borrowings ₹2,600 crore, lease liabilities ₹300 crore, cash ₹900 crore; 2-year weekly raw beta 1.32 (R² 0.21, SE 0.19). Five peers (levered β, market D/E incl. leases, cash/firm value): (1.05, 0.12, 0.04), (0.95, 0.05, 0.10), (1.20, 0.35, 0.02), (0.88, 0.08, 0.12), (1.40, 0.60, 0.01). Unrated; normalised EBIT ₹1,150 crore; finance cost ₹170 crore (incl. lease interest ₹25 crore). Management target net debt/EBITDA 1.0×; EV/EBITDA 11×. Model is post-116. (a) Compute the stripped Rf and India ERP. (b) Unlever, cash-adjust and take the median peer beta; relever at your chosen target. (c) Compute Blume beta for the subject and compare. (d) Derive Kd via synthetic rating (use the ICR table in s05; A-rated spread 3.0%, A− 3.5%, AA 1.4%). (e) Compute WACC and present a one-page input sheet with sources.

💡 Hint: Convert the leverage target to D/(D+E) before relevering; keep the debt definition lease-inclusive throughout.

Q2. Review of a fairness-opinion WACC [L2]

A draft WACC shows: Rf 6.6% (G-Sec), ERP 7.3% (Damodaran India), beta 0.42 (1-year daily, illiquid stock), CSRP 3% "for size", Kd 7.1% (book interest ÷ average debt), weights on book values (D 45%, E 55%), lease liabilities excluded although FCFF is post-116. List every issue, its direction of bias, the correct treatment and the data you would request.

Q3. Currency consistency for an IT exporter [L2]

A US parent computes USD WACC 8.9% for its Indian captive. Indian management computes INR WACC 12.6% from G-Sec inputs. Expected inflation India 4.5%, US 2.3%. (a) Convert the USD WACC to INR. (b) Explain the remaining gap and which inputs to investigate. (c) If cash flows are in INR, which rate would you use and why?

Q4. Rate shock sensitivity [L2]

Following an RBI rate cut, the 10-year G-Sec falls 40 bp and AA spreads widen 15 bp. For a company with βL 1.05, ERP 7.25% (Damodaran, stripped Rf), target D/(D+E) 25%, Kd previously 8.1%, tax 25.17%, compute the change in Ke, Kd and WACC, assuming the default spread is unchanged. Discuss whether you would expect ERP to change.

Q5. Interview round [L1]

Answer briefly: (a) Why market-value weights? (b) Why unlever peer betas? (c) Why does Hamada include (1 − t)? (d) When is a company-specific premium defensible? (e) What is the Blume adjustment and why does it exist?
