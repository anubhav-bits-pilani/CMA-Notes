Key concept

Damodaran INR Risk-Free Rate: Stripping the Sovereign Default Spread

Concept: Damodaran INR Rf = 10-yr G-Sec yield − India sovereign default spread

Statement

\[ R_{f,\text{INR}} = y_{\text{G-Sec, 10yr}} - \text{DS}_{\text{India}} \]

where \( y_{\text{G-Sec, 10yr}} \) is the yield on the 10-year Government of India benchmark security on the valuation date and \( \text{DS}_{\text{India}} \) is India's sovereign default spread — in Damodaran's dataset, the spread associated with India's Moody's local-currency rating (Baa3) from his rating-to-spread table, or a CDS-based spread where he uses one.

Explanation

A risk-free rate must carry no default risk. Government of India rupee bonds are considered very safe locally, but India's sovereign rating is below AAA; global investors price some default risk into the yield. Damodaran's position: the INR G-Sec yield = true INR risk-free rate + default spread. To get the "true" Rf, subtract the default spread. Country risk is then reintroduced explicitly through the CRP in the ERP (t2/s02), scaled by equity-to-bond volatility and by beta.

Why this matters: it is the pairing rule. Damodaran's India total ERP (mature ERP + CRP) is designed to be added to the stripped Rf. Adding it to the full G-Sec yield counts the default spread twice — once in Rf and once (scaled up) in CRP. The two consistent configurations are:

Configuration Rf ERP
Damodaran G-Sec − default spread Mature ERP + India CRP
Local market Full G-Sec ERP without separate CRP (local implied/historical/survey)

Consequences:

  • The stripped Rf (around 4.3–4.6% in 2025) is also Damodaran's ceiling for the terminal growth rate in INR (t3/s02) — a key reason Indian valuations using his framework end up with g of 4–5%.
  • Kd for companies is still the full G-Sec yield plus corporate spread (rating spreads are quoted over G-Sec); alternatively stripped Rf + country default spread + company spread.
  • The default spread changes with ratings; an upgrade by Moody's would lower DS and raise the stripped Rf (while lowering CRP).

Illustration

Valuation date: 14 November 2025. 10-year G-Sec yield 6.52%; Damodaran India default spread (Baa3) 2.10% (illustrative; take the current table).

\[ R_{f,\text{INR}} = 6.52\% - 2.10\% = 4.42\% \]

With India ERP 7.25% and βL 1.00: Ke = 11.67%. Using full G-Sec with the same ERP gives 13.77% — 210 bp higher, entirely due to double-counting the default spread.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute and pair [L1]

G-Sec yield 6.95%; default spread 2.25%; mature ERP 4.6%; CRP 3.1%; βL 0.88. Compute Ke under the Damodaran configuration and under a mistaken mix (full G-Sec + total ERP), and state the size of the error.

Q2. Rating upgrade scenario [L2]

Suppose Moody's upgrades India to Baa2 and Damodaran's default spread falls from 2.10% to 1.70%, with the volatility ratio at 1.35 and G-Sec unchanged at 6.5%. Compute the change in stripped Rf, CRP, total ERP and Ke for a stock with βL 1.20, and explain why the net effect on Ke depends on beta.

💡 Hint: Stripped Rf rises by the fall in spread; CRP falls by the fall in spread × volatility ratio, which is then multiplied by beta.

Q3. Terminal growth implication [L2]

Your client's DCF uses g = 7% with a WACC built on the Damodaran stripped Rf of 4.4%. Explain why this combination is internally inconsistent in Damodaran's framework and what the two coherent alternatives are.
