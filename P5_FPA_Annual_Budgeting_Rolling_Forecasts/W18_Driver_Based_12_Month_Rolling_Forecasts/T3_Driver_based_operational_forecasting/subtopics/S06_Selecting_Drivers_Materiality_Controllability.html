Subtopic note

Selecting Drivers: Materiality, Controllability, Predictability, Data

Scope: Selecting drivers: materiality, controllability, predictability, data availability (Pareto 80/20)

Why it matters

A driver-based model can be ruined by too many drivers as surely as by too few. Every driver needs data, an owner and a monthly update; every driver adds estimation error; and a model with 60 drivers is too slow for a monthly rolling cycle and too complex for anyone to trust. The discipline of selecting a small set of key drivers — and treating everything else with simple ratios — is what separates practical FP&A models from academic ones. It is also the step most often skipped, which is why the topic's pitfalls (unsourceable drivers, unowned drivers, false precision) are so common.

Core notes

The four selection criteria

  1. Materiality — how much does the driver move the outcome? Measure as the EBITDA (or cash) impact of a realistic change in the driver (c05: ∂EBITDA/∂driver × plausible Δ). Rank drivers; the top 15–20% usually explain about 80% of outcome variance (Pareto 80/20).
  2. Controllability — can management influence it? Controllable drivers (price, marketing spend, headcount, store openings) support accountability and action planning; uncontrollable ones (commodity prices, FX, monsoon, GDP) are still modelled but treated as assumptions or scenarios with finance/treasury ownership, not managerial targets.
  3. Predictability — can it be forecast with useful accuracy? Measured historically by coefficient of variation, forecastability against a naive baseline, or the error of past forecasts. Unpredictable but material drivers belong in scenarios and R&O rather than point forecasts.
  4. Data availability — is it measured reliably, monthly, with enough history, in a system finance can access? If not, the driver collapses into manual overrides within a few cycles.

Scoring framework

Score each candidate driver 1–5 on each criterion and weight materiality highest:

Candidate driver Materiality (×3) Controllability Predictability Data Weighted score Decision
Footfall per store 5 3 3 4 25 Key driver
Conversion % 5 4 3 4 26 Key driver
ATV 4 4 4 5 25 Key driver
Cotton price 4 1 2 5 20 Assumption + scenario
Store electricity kWh 1 3 4 2 12 Ratio (% of sales or per store)
Staff uniform cost 1 4 5 3 15 Run-rate

The thresholds are judgemental; the value is in making the choice explicit and reviewable.

Pareto analysis in practice

  1. List candidate drivers with their estimated EBITDA impact for a plausible move (e.g. ±1 standard deviation of historical change, or ±10%).
  2. Sort descending; compute cumulative share of total absolute impact.
  3. Select drivers up to roughly 80% cumulative share, then add any driver required for compliance or contractual reasons.
  4. For the remaining tail, use simple rules: % of revenue, per-unit norms, run-rate, contractual schedule.
=SORTBY(Drivers,Impact,-1)
=SCAN(0,SORT(Impact,,-1),LAMBDA(a,x,a+x))/SUM(Impact)

The SCAN formula produces the cumulative share for the Pareto line.

Ownership and the driver register

For each key driver, record: definition and unit; source system and report; owner (business) and reviewer (finance); update frequency and due date in the cycle; history length; forecast method (owner input, statistical, contractual); sensitivity rank; and accuracy track record. Review the register at each AOP cycle — materiality and data availability change as the business evolves (a new channel, a new commodity exposure).

Illustration

A D2C apparel brand lists 18 candidate drivers. Estimated FY EBITDA impact of a plausible move (₹ crore): sessions 9.0, conversion 8.0, AOV 6.0, return rate 4.5, marketing CPC 3.5, discount depth 3.0, cotton yarn price 2.5, freight per order 1.5, PG mix 0.6, and nine others totalling 1.4. Total 40.0. Cumulative share: top 3 = 57.5%, top 5 = 77.5%, top 6 = 85%. Select the top 6 as key drivers with owners (growth head, category head, CX head), treat cotton yarn price as a procurement assumption with a scenario, and model freight and PG via per-order and % rules.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Material but unpredictable (e.g. commodity prices, FX): don't pretend to forecast precisely; use forward curves or consensus, and run scenarios.
  • Controllable but immaterial (e.g. travel): keep as a spend-control line, not a driver.
  • Proxy drivers: when the ideal driver is unavailable (true footfall), use a proxy (bills, mall footfall indices) and document the bias.
  • Correlated drivers: two highly correlated drivers (sessions and marketing spend) may be redundant; model the causal one.
  • Organisational politics: owners may resist being named for drivers they do not fully control; distinguish forecast ownership (providing the best estimate) from target accountability.
  • Over-reliance on history: materiality based on past variance may miss new risks (e.g. a new regulation); add judgement-based drivers where needed.

Key takeaways

  • Select drivers by materiality, controllability, predictability and data availability — materiality first.
  • Pareto: typically 15–20% of candidate drivers explain about 80% of outcome variance.
  • Material but uncontrollable or unpredictable drivers become assumptions, scenarios or R&O items.
  • The tail is modelled with simple ratios, norms or run-rates.
  • Maintain a driver register with definitions, sources, owners, update timing and accuracy.
  • Revisit driver selection every AOP cycle.

Practice Questions

Q1. Pareto selection [L2]

Candidate drivers and EBITDA impact of a plausible move (₹ crore): volume 22, realisation 18, coal price 14, power consumption per tonne 6, freight lead distance 5, packing bag price 3, labour productivity 2, spares 2, insurance 1, other 7 drivers totalling 4. Compute cumulative shares, identify the set covering at least 80%, and classify each selected driver by controllability.

💡 Hint: Sort first, then accumulate; remember to include the "other" group in the total.

Q2. Scoring matrix design [L2]

Design a scoring matrix for selecting drivers for a hospital chain (bed count, occupancy, ALOS, ARPOB, payer mix, doctor fees, consumables), score each on the four criteria with justification, and decide which become key drivers.

Q3. Driver register in Excel [L2]

Specify the columns of a driver register table and write formulas that flag (a) drivers not updated in the current cycle, (b) drivers with no owner, and (c) drivers whose last six months' MAPE exceeds 15%.

Q4. Case: driver overload [L3]

A logistics company's forecast model has 52 drivers, of which the finance team overrides 30 each month because data arrive late. Propose a redesign, explaining how you would use the four criteria and Pareto analysis, what you would do with the dropped drivers, and how you would convince operational managers.
