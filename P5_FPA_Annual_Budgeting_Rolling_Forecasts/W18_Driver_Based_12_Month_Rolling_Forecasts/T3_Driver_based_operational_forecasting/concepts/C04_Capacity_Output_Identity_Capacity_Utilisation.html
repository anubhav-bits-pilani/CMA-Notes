Key concept

Capacity Output Identity: Capacity × Utilisation × Yield

Concept: Output = Installed capacity × Utilisation % × Yield %

Statement

\[ Q_t = K_t \times u_t \times y_t \]

where \( Q_t \) is good (saleable) output in period \( t \); \( K_t \) the installed (rated) capacity available in the period (annual capacity × days in period ÷ 365, net of commissioning dates); \( u_t \) the utilisation rate (gross production ÷ capacity); and \( y_t \) the yield (good output ÷ gross production, i.e. 1 − scrap/reject/process-loss rate). Sales volume is then \( S_t = \min(D_t, Q_t + I_{t-1} - I^{min}) \), with \( D_t \) demand, \( I_{t-1} \) opening finished-goods inventory and \( I^{min} \) minimum stock.

Explanation

The identity converts physical plant parameters into the volume line of the forecast and makes supply constraints explicit. Each term has a different owner and driver:

  • Capacity (projects/engineering): changes only with new lines, debottlenecking or decommissioning, effective from commercial operation date. Publicly reported by Indian cement, steel, paper and chemicals companies (e.g. MTPA).
  • Utilisation (plant operations and sales): reflects demand, planned maintenance shutdowns, breakdowns, raw-material and power availability. Practical maximum below 100% (often 85–95%).
  • Yield (process/quality): scrap, rework, process losses; in batch chemicals and pharma, yield on the key raw material is also a major cost driver (input = output ÷ yield).

Uses in forecasting:

  • Constrained forecasts: if demand exceeds \( K \times u_{max} \times y \), the forecast must cap volume and flag lost sales or the need for outsourcing (toll manufacturing).
  • Cost linkage: raw material input = gross production × consumption norm; power = gross production × kWh per unit; fixed plant costs spread over output (unit cost falls as utilisation rises — the operating-leverage link to Week 21).
  • Inventory valuation (Ind AS 2): fixed production overheads are allocated on the basis of normal capacity; unallocated overheads due to low production are expensed, not inventoried — so low utilisation affects reported margins directly.

Utilisation and yield multiply, so OEE-style improvements compound: 2 points each on 80% utilisation and 95% yield lift output by roughly 4.7%.

Illustration

Paper mill: 3,00,000 tonnes per annum; Q4 FY26 (January–March, 90 days); planned shutdown 6 days in February; utilisation of available days 91%; yield 95%.

  • Available capacity = 3,00,000 × (90 − 6) ÷ 365 = 69,041 tonnes.
  • Gross production = 69,041 × 0.91 = 62,827 tonnes.
  • Good output = 62,827 × 0.95 = 59,686 tonnes.
  • Q4 demand 64,000 tonnes; opening FG stock 2,500 tonnes; minimum stock 1,500 tonnes → sales = min(64,000; 59,686 + 1,000) = 60,686 tonnes. Lost sales about 3,300 tonnes.
  • At realisation ₹68,000 per tonne, revenue = ₹412.7 crore.

Practice Questions

Q1. Monthly output [L1]

A 4.5 MTPA cement plant runs at 84% utilisation in November (30 days) with 99.2% yield. Compute good output and revenue at ₹5,450 per tonne.

Q2. New line commissioning [L2]

A chemicals plant has 24,000 tonnes per annum on line A. Line B (12,000 TPA) reaches commercial operation on 1 December 2025 with a utilisation ramp of 40%, 60%, 75%, 85% for December–March; line A runs at 88%. Yield is 93% on both. Compute monthly good output for October–March (use actual days) and the FY26 H2 total.

💡 Hint: Compute each line separately, then add; line B has zero capacity before its commercial operation date.

Q3. Normal capacity and Ind AS 2 [L2]

A plant's normal capacity is 10,000 units per month and fixed production overheads are ₹1.5 crore per month. In August, flood disruption reduced production to 6,000 units. Compute the fixed overhead absorbed per unit, the amount inventoried and the amount expensed, and explain the effect on the August gross margin in the forecast vs a model that spreads overheads over actual output.
