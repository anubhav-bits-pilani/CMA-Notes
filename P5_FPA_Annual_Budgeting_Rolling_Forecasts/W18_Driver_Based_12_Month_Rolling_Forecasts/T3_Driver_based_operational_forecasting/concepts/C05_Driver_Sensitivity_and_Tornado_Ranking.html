Key concept

Driver Sensitivity and Tornado Ranking

Concept: Driver sensitivity: ΔEBITDA = ∂EBITDA/∂driver × Δdriver; rank drivers by impact via tornado chart

Statement

For a driver \( x_i \), the first-order change in EBITDA from a change \( \Delta x_i \), holding other drivers constant, is:

\[ \Delta \text{EBITDA}_i \approx \frac{\partial \text{EBITDA}}{\partial x_i} \times \Delta x_i \]

where \( \partial \text{EBITDA} / \partial x_i \) is the marginal effect of the driver (EBITDA change per unit of driver). In a model, the exact sensitivity is computed by flexing the driver: \( \Delta \text{EBITDA}_i = \text{EBITDA}(x_i + \Delta x_i) - \text{EBITDA}(x_i) \). A tornado chart plots, for each driver, the EBITDA at its low and high values as a horizontal bar around the base case, sorted by bar length (largest at top).

Explanation

The partial derivative tells you how much EBITDA moves per unit of driver; multiplying by a realistic change in the driver (not an arbitrary ±10%) gives the risk-relevant impact. Ranking by realistic impact identifies which drivers deserve forecasting effort, ownership and monitoring (s06), and which belong in scenarios.

Examples of marginal effects in a retail model with revenue \( R = N F c A D \) and contribution margin \( m \): \( \partial \text{EBITDA} / \partial c = N F A D \times m \) — each conversion point is worth a fixed rupee amount of contribution. Because the model is multiplicative, a 1% change in any revenue driver gives the same 1% revenue change; but realistic ranges differ (footfall may swing ±8%, ATV ±3%), which is why realistic Δ matters.

Practical construction:

  1. Define base, low and high values for each driver (e.g. P10/P90 from history, or management ranges).
  2. For each driver, compute EBITDA at low and high with others at base — via a one-variable Data Table, or a small macro-free "flex" column with a driver-selector switch.
  3. Compute swing = |EBITDA(high) − EBITDA(low)|; sort descending.
  4. Plot as a stacked/clustered horizontal bar chart with the base as the axis crossing.

Limitations: one-at-a-time sensitivities ignore interactions and correlations (a price rise lowers conversion); non-linearities (capacity caps, tiered commissions) make low and high asymmetric. Use scenarios (Topic 4) for coherent combined changes.

Base H2 EBITDA ₹180 crore for a retailer. Realistic ranges and resulting EBITDA (₹ crore):

Footfall tops the tornado. The analyst's focus — weekly footfall tracking, marketing response — follows from the chart, not from line size in the P&L.

Excel sketch for one driver using a selector: a cell FlexDriver (1–5) and FlexCase (−1 low, 0 base, +1 high); each driver input is =Base*(1+IF(FlexDriver=k,CHOOSE(FlexCase+2,LowPct,0,HighPct),0)); a two-way Data Table over FlexDriver and FlexCase returns the tornado values.

Practice Questions

Q1. Marginal effect [L1]

A D2C brand has sessions 30 lakh per month, conversion 1.8%, AOV ₹1,200 and contribution margin 32% of revenue. Compute the monthly EBITDA effect of +0.1 point conversion and of +₹50 AOV, and state which has the larger effect.

Q2. Building a tornado table [L2]

Base annual EBITDA is a function of volume 2.0 million units (±7%), price ₹850 (±2%), variable cost ₹520 per unit (±4%) and fixed costs ₹38 crore (±5%). Compute EBITDA at low and high for each driver, the swings, the ranking, and comment on why price ranks where it does despite its small range.

💡 Hint: Compute base EBITDA first; each flex changes only one input.

Q3. Asymmetry and interaction [L3]

In a model with a capacity cap at 105% of base volume, explain why the volume tornado bar is asymmetric. Then explain how you would present the interaction between a 4% price increase and a resulting 2% volume loss, and why a tornado chart alone is insufficient for the Board.
