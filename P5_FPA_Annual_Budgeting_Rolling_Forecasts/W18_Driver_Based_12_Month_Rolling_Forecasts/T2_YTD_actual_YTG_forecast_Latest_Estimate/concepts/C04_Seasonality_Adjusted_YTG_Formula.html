Key concept

Seasonality-Adjusted YTG Formula

Concept: Seasonality-adjusted YTG = (YTD actual ÷ Σ YTD seasonality weights) × Σ YTG seasonality weights

Statement

\[ \text{YTG}_{seas} = \frac{A_{YTD}}{\sum_{t=1}^{m} w_t} \times \sum_{t=m+1}^{12} w_t \]

where \( A_{YTD} \) is the year-to-date actual, \( w_t \) the seasonality weight of fiscal month \( t \) (with \( \sum_{t=1}^{12} w_t = 1 \)), and \( m \) the number of closed months. The implied full-year estimate is \( A_{YTD} \div \sum_{t=1}^{m} w_t \), and the projection for an individual open month \( t \) is \( (A_{YTD} \div \sum_{1}^{m} w) \times w_t \).

Explanation

The first factor "grosses up" the YTD to the full year it implies, assuming the year is following its normal seasonal shape; the second factor takes the share of that year still to come. If the YTD has been tracking 5% above AOP, the seasonal method extends that 5% to the remaining months in proportion to their weights — a scaled, shape-preserving projection.

Assumptions and limits:

  • Stable shape: this year's seasonal pattern matches the weights. Moving festivals (Diwali in October vs November), changed sale-event dates, a delayed monsoon or a GST-driven pre-buying spike violate this.
  • Uniform relative performance: YTD over- or under-performance persists proportionally. If YTD was depressed by a one-off (a strike in May), normalise YTD first.
  • No known structural changes: new stores, price increases or capacity additions in YTG are not captured — overlay them separately, or use driver models.
  • Weights quality: derive from multi-year history (average share or ratio-to-moving-average), excluding abnormal years (FY21 lockdowns).

It is therefore a statistical baseline, not the forecast itself. It is the right comparator for challenging a bottom-up YTG and for the seasonality-adjusted required run-rate (c03). It replaces the naive \( A_{YTD} \times (12 - m) \div m \), which is identical only when all weights are equal (\( w_t = 1/12 \)).

Excel, with weights in C6:N6 and flags in C7:N7:

=YTD_Act/SUMPRODUCT(C6:N6,C7:N7)*SUMPRODUCT(C6:N6,1-C7:N7)

Illustration

Ethnic-wear retailer, 6+6. Weights Apr–Sep: 6%, 6%, 5%, 6%, 7%, 8% (Σ = 38%); Oct–Mar: 15%, 14%, 9%, 8%, 8%, 8% (Σ = 62%). YTD actual ₹304 crore.

  • Implied full year = 304 ÷ 0.38 = ₹800 crore
  • Seasonal YTG = 800 × 0.62 = ₹496 crore; October projection = 800 × 0.15 = ₹120 crore
  • Naive YTG = 304 ÷ 6 × 6 = ₹304 crore — understated by ₹192 crore (39%)

If Diwali shifts from November to October this year, the October/November weights must be adjusted before applying the formula.

Connections

Central to s04 (seasonality-adjusted vs naive), feeds \( RR_{seas} \) in c03 and the credibility tests of s03. It is the corrective for the pitfall "extrapolating YTD run-rate linearly in a seasonal business". Topic 3's statistical support (moving averages, FORECAST.ETS with seasonality) offers more sophisticated baselines, and Topic 5's Forecast Value Added asks whether the submitted forecast beats this seasonal baseline. Weight derivation uses the Week 18 statistics refresher (moving averages).

Practice Questions

Q1. Formula application [L1]

YTD (Apr–Jun) actual ₹180 crore; Q1 weights total 22%. Compute the implied full year, seasonal YTG, and the naive YTG, and comment on the reliability of each at this cutoff.

Q2. Normalising YTD before projecting [L2]

At 6+6, YTD revenue is ₹505 crore, including a one-off bulk government order of ₹35 crore in August that will not recur; H1 weights 44%. Compute the seasonal YTG with and without normalisation and the LE in each case (adding back the one-off to YTD actual where appropriate).

💡 Hint: The one-off belongs in YTD actual but not in the base used to gross up.

Q3. Per-month projection vs submitted forecast [L2]

With weights Oct 12%, Nov 13%, Dec 9%, Jan 8%, Feb 8%, Mar 10% and H1 weights 40%, YTD ₹720 crore, compute the seasonal projection for each month October–March and compare with the submitted forecast of 210, 215, 165, 150, 148, 180. Identify months where the submitted forecast deviates by more than 10%.
