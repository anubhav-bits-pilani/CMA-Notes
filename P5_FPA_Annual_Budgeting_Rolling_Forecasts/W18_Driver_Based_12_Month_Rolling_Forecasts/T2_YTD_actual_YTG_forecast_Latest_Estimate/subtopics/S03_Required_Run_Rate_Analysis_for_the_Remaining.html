Required Run-Rate Analysis for the Remaining Months

Scope: Required run-rate analysis for remaining months

Why it matters

The required run-rate converts an abstract annual gap into a monthly operating target that sales, plant and category heads can relate to: "we need ₹118 crore a month from October to March to hit the AOP; we have been doing ₹92 crore". It is the quickest credibility test of any YTG forecast — if the forecast implies a monthly rate far above anything the business has achieved, someone needs to explain why. It is also a staple of MBR slides, sales reviews and FP&A interviews, where candidates are asked to compute it mentally and comment on achievability.

Core notes

Definition

\[ RR_{req} = \frac{T - A_{YTD}}{n} \]

where \( RR_{req} \) is the required average monthly run-rate, \( T \) the full-year target (AOP, guidance or a stretch target), \( A_{YTD} \) the year-to-date actual, and \( n \) the number of remaining months (12 − closed months).

Compare it with three benchmarks:

Benchmark Formula What it tells you
YTD average run-rate \( A_{YTD} \div m \) What the business has delivered on average (m = closed months)
Recent run-rate Average of last 3 actual months Current momentum, less affected by early-year events
Seasonality-adjusted run-rate Seasonality-adjusted YTG ÷ n (s04) What normal seasonal patterns would deliver
Forecast YTG run-rate YTG forecast ÷ n What the forecast assumes

Key ratio — stretch factor:

\[ \text{Stretch} = \frac{RR_{req}}{RR_{bench}} \]

A stretch above about 1.10 against the seasonality-adjusted benchmark generally needs identifiable actions (price increases, launches, new stores, big orders) to be credible; above about 1.25 the target is usually unreachable without extraordinary events. These thresholds are judgement-based rules of thumb, not standards — calibrate them on the company's own history.

Required run-rate at the monthly level

Average monthly figures can mislead in seasonal businesses. A better test spreads the required YTG across the remaining months using seasonality weights:

\[ \text{Req}_t = (T - A_{YTD}) \times \frac{w_t}{\sum_{s \in YTG} w_s} \]

where \( w_t \) is the seasonality weight of month \( t \). This shows, for example, that November must deliver ₹165 crore, which can be compared with last November's ₹148 crore.

Unit-level translation

Translate ₹ run-rates into operational terms owners can act on:

  • Retail: required sales per store per day = \( RR_{req} \div (\text{Stores} \times \text{Days}) \).
  • B2B: required orders per month = \( RR_{req} \div \text{AOV} \); required leads = orders ÷ conversion (with lag — Topic 3).
  • Manufacturing: required tonnes per day = \( RR_{req} \div (\text{Realisation per tonne} \times \text{Days}) \), compared with installed capacity × achievable utilisation.

If the required tonnes exceed capacity, the target is physically unreachable regardless of demand.

Excel implementation

=LET(T,AOP_FY, A,YTD_Act, m,ClosedMonths, n,12-m,
     req,(T-A)/n, ytdavg,A/m,
     last3,AVERAGE(INDEX(Row,m-2):INDEX(Row,m)),
     HSTACK(req,ytdavg,last3,req/ytdavg,req/last3))

Row is the FY revenue row; guard for m < 3 and for m = 12 (no remaining months: return "n.a.").

Illustration

FY26 revenue AOP ₹1,320 crore; cutoff 30-Sep-2025; YTD actual ₹600 crore (Apr–Sep: 92, 95, 96, 100, 104, 113).

  • \( RR_{req} = (1{,}320 - 600) \div 6 = 120 \) per month.
  • YTD average = 600 ÷ 6 = 100; stretch 1.20.
  • Last-3 average = (100 + 104 + 113) ÷ 3 = 105.7; stretch 1.14.
  • If seasonality weights for Apr–Sep sum to 45% and Oct–Mar 55% (s04), seasonality-adjusted YTG = 600 ÷ 0.45 × 0.55 = 733 → run-rate 122; stretch 0.98.

The naive comparison says the target is a 20% stretch; the seasonal comparison says the AOP is achievable if the normal H2 pattern repeats. Conclusion depends on whether this year's festive season is expected to be normal.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Target already achieved or nearly achieved: \( RR_{req} \le 0 \) means the target is met; report "achieved" rather than a negative run-rate.
  • One-off months in YTD: a ₹30 crore bulk order in June inflates the YTD average; exclude one-offs from benchmarks.
  • Short remaining period: at 11+1, \( n = 1 \) — the required "run-rate" is just the March number; compare with prior March and the pipeline, not averages.
  • Price changes: a 5% price increase from January raises the achievable run-rate for Jan–Mar; benchmark on volumes and apply prices separately.
  • Working days: months with festivals or plant shutdowns have fewer selling/production days; use per-day run-rates where days vary materially.
  • Cost lines: the required run-rate concept also applies to costs ("we can spend at most ₹14 crore a month on opex for the rest of the year to stay within AOP"), useful for spend controls.

Key takeaways

  • Required run-rate = (target − YTD actual) ÷ remaining months.
  • Always compare with YTD average, last-3-month and seasonality-adjusted benchmarks; compute the stretch factor.
  • Spread required YTG by seasonality weights for a month-by-month test in seasonal businesses.
  • Translate ₹ run-rates into operational units (per store per day, orders, tonnes) and test against capacity.
  • Exclude one-offs from benchmarks; handle achieved targets and the final month separately.
  • Stretch thresholds are judgement; calibrate on history.

Practice Questions

Q1. Basic required run-rate [L1]

AOP FY26 EBITDA ₹240 crore. 9+3 LE cutoff with YTD EBITDA ₹168 crore. Compute the required monthly run-rate and the stretch vs the YTD average.

Q2. Retail per-store translation [L2]

A 210-store apparel chain needs ₹780 crore revenue from October to March to hit AOP. Stores open 30 days a month on average (ignore closures), October 31 days, November 30, December 31, January 31, February 28, March 31. Last year's average sales per store per day for the same months was ₹1.72 lakh. Compute the required average sales per store per day and the stretch vs last year, and suggest which operational drivers (footfall, conversion, ATV) could plausibly close the gap.

💡 Hint: Use total store-days across the six months, not 30 × 6.

Q3. Capacity-constrained run-rate [L2]

A cement company targets 14.4 million tonnes for FY26. YTD to December: 10.2 million tonnes. Installed capacity 16 MTPA; maximum sustainable utilisation 92%. Compute the required monthly despatch for January–March and the implied annualised utilisation, and conclude whether the target is physically achievable.

Q4. Interpreting a mixed signal [L3]

Required run-rate is 1.18× the YTD average and 1.04× the last-3-month average; the seasonality-adjusted run-rate is 0.97× of required. The sales head's YTG forecast is 1.12× the required run-rate. Write the MBR commentary (about 120 words) explaining what each comparison means and what questions you would ask the sales head.
