Seasonality-Adjusted YTG vs Naive Run-Rate Extrapolation

Scope: Seasonality-adjusted YTG vs naive run-rate extrapolation

Why it matters

Indian businesses are intensely seasonal: consumer durables and apparel peak in the October–November festive season; jewellery spikes around Akshaya Tritiya, Dhanteras and wedding seasons; agri-inputs follow kharif (June–July sowing) and rabi (October–November); air-conditioners and beverages peak in March–May; B2B capital goods and government suppliers bunch billing in March. A YTG projected by straight-line extrapolation of YTD will be systematically wrong in such businesses — usually understating at 6+6 and potentially overstating at 9+3. The seasonality-adjusted projection is the standard "statistical" benchmark against which the bottom-up YTG forecast is challenged, and its mechanics come up in every LE assessment.

Core notes

Naive run-rate extrapolation

\[ \text{YTG}_{naive} = \frac{A_{YTD}}{m} \times (12 - m) \]

where \( A_{YTD} \) is the year-to-date actual and \( m \) the closed months. It assumes every month is equal. Equivalently, LE = YTD × 12/m ("annualising").

Seasonality-adjusted YTG

\[ \text{YTG}_{seas} = \frac{A_{YTD}}{\sum_{t \in YTD} w_t} \times \sum_{t \in YTG} w_t \]

where \( w_t \) is the seasonality weight of month \( t \) (weights sum to 1 over the year). The first term grosses YTD up to an implied full year; the second applies the share of the year still to come. Since \( \sum_{YTD} w + \sum_{YTG} w = 1 \), the implied LE is \( A_{YTD} \div \sum_{YTD} w_t \).

Deriving seasonality weights

  1. Simple share method: weight of month \( t \) = PY month revenue ÷ PY total. Quick but contaminated by PY one-offs and growth.
  2. Multi-year average share: average the monthly share over 3 years — smoother; exclude abnormal years (FY21 COVID lockdowns) explicitly.
  3. Ratio-to-moving-average (classical decomposition): compute a centred 12-month moving average to remove trend, divide each month by it, average the ratios for each calendar month across years, normalise so the 12 indices average 1 (weights sum to 1 after dividing by 12). This separates trend from seasonality and is the better method in a growing business.
  4. Working-day adjustment: where months differ in selling days, compute per-day seasonality and multiply by forecast days.
  5. Moving festivals: Diwali falls in late October in some years and November in others (e.g. 2024: 31 October–1 November; 2025: 20–21 October). Monthly weights computed by calendar month mix the two; adjust by shifting a festival "block" of days to the month in which it falls this year, or forecast the festive period as a separate event.

Comparison

Feature Naive run-rate Seasonality-adjusted
Assumption Every month equal Each month's share follows historical pattern
Data needed YTD only 2–3 years monthly history
Bias in seasonal business Large, predictable direction Small if pattern stable
Captures trend acceleration No Partly (if YTD reflects it)
Captures known changes (price, launches) No No — overlay needed
Use Sanity check only Statistical baseline for challenge

Neither replaces a driver-based forecast; the seasonal projection is the baseline that the bottom-up YTG must beat or explain (cf. Forecast Value Added, Topic 5).

Excel implementation

With weights in row 6 (C6:N6, Apr–Mar) and flag row 7:

YTD_w    =SUMPRODUCT(C6:N6,C7:N7)
YTG_w    =1-YTD_w
YTG_seas =YTD_Act/YTD_w*YTG_w
Month_t  =IF(C7,0,YTD_Act/YTD_w*C6)

Month_t phases the seasonal YTG to each open month for a month-by-month comparison with the submitted forecast.

Illustration

Consumer-durables company, FY26. Three-year average weights: Apr 6%, May 7%, Jun 7%, Jul 6%, Aug 7%, Sep 8% (H1 = 41%); Oct 13%, Nov 12%, Dec 8%, Jan 7%, Feb 8%, Mar 11% (H2 = 59%). YTD (Apr–Sep) actual ₹820 crore.

  • Naive: 820 ÷ 6 × 6 = ₹820 crore YTG; LE ₹1,640 crore.
  • Seasonal: 820 ÷ 0.41 × 0.59 = ₹1,180 crore YTG; LE ₹2,000 crore.
  • October seasonal projection = 820 ÷ 0.41 × 0.13 = ₹260 crore.

The naive method understates the LE by ₹360 crore (18%). If the sales-team YTG is ₹1,050 crore, it is ₹130 crore below the seasonal baseline — they must explain why (e.g. weaker festive demand, channel destocking) or the forecast is sandbagged.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Structural change in pattern: e-commerce sale events (Big Billion Days, Great Indian Festival) have pulled festive demand into late September–early October; quick commerce flattens monthly patterns; GST rate changes create pre-buying spikes. Old weights may not apply.
  • YTD anomalies: a one-off in YTD distorts the gross-up; normalise YTD before applying weights.
  • Growth within the year: if the business is accelerating (new stores), YTD under-represents the run-rate; combine seasonality with a trend factor or use driver logic.
  • Small YTD share: at 3+9, YTD weights may be 20%; a small error in YTD is multiplied by 5. Seasonal extrapolation is least reliable early in the year.
  • Weights for cost lines: fixed costs are not seasonal; apply seasonality only to variable costs and revenue-linked items.
  • Statistical tools: FORECAST.ETS with seasonality = 12 automates seasonal projection from monthly history (Topic 3, s07), but check that history is long enough (at least two full cycles).

Key takeaways

  • Naive YTG = YTD average × remaining months — biased in any seasonal business.
  • Seasonal YTG = (YTD ÷ Σ YTD weights) × Σ YTG weights; implied LE = YTD ÷ Σ YTD weights.
  • Derive weights from multi-year history, preferably by ratio-to-moving-average, excluding abnormal years.
  • Adjust for moving festivals, working days and structural changes in the pattern.
  • Use the seasonal projection as a statistical baseline to challenge bottom-up YTG.
  • Early in the year, small YTD errors are magnified; rely more on drivers.

Practice Questions

Q1. Naive vs seasonal LE [L1]

YTD actual to December (9 months) is ₹1,140 crore. Seasonality weights for April–December sum to 78%. Compute the naive and seasonality-adjusted YTG and LE.

Q2. Deriving weights with an abnormal year [L2]

Monthly revenue shares for April–March were: FY23 (6,6,7,7,8,8,12,12,9,8,8,9), FY24 (6,7,7,7,8,9,13,11,8,8,7,9), FY25 (2,3,6,8,9,10,13,12,9,9,9,10, where April–May were disrupted by a plant fire). Compute a robust weight set, justify the treatment of FY25, and normalise to 100%.

💡 Hint: Decide whether to drop the whole year or only the disrupted months, and renormalise afterwards.

Q3. Moving Diwali adjustment [L3]

Last year Diwali fell on 1 November; this year on 20 October. Historical weights: October 10%, November 14%. Analysis shows the 10 days ending on Diwali contribute 9% of annual sales. Propose adjusted October and November weights for this year, explain your method, and discuss how you would validate it.

Q4. Challenging a bottom-up YTG [L2]

At 6+6, YTD revenue is ₹540 crore, H1 weights total 44%, and the bottom-up YTG submitted by three regional heads totals ₹610 crore (North 240, South 210, West 160) with regional H1 weights of 45%, 42% and 46% and regional YTD of 210, 180 and 150 respectively. Compute the seasonal baseline by region and in total, identify which region's forecast deviates most, and frame the challenge question for that region.
