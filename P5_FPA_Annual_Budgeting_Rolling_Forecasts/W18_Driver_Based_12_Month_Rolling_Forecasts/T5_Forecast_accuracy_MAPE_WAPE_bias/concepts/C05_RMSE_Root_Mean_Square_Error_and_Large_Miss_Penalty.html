Key concept

RMSE: Root Mean Square Error and Large-Miss Penalty

Concept: RMSE = √(Σ (A − F)² ÷ n); penalises large misses more heavily

Statement

\[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} (A_t - F_t)^2} \]

  • \(A_t, F_t\) — actual and forecast for observation \(t\)
  • \(n\) — number of observations
  • RMSE is in the same units as the data (₹ crore, tonnes)
  • Always \(\text{RMSE} \ge \text{MAD}\), with equality only when every absolute error is the same size

Explanation

Why square? Squaring makes each error's contribution grow with its size: one error of 10 contributes 100, while ten errors of 1 contribute only 10 in total. RMSE therefore penalises large misses much more heavily than MAD or WAPE do. This is desirable when the cost of a miss rises disproportionately with its size — for example:

  • Cash and liquidity forecasting close to a covenant or overdraft limit, where a big miss means a breach or emergency borrowing.
  • Capacity and inventory, where small misses are absorbed by safety stock but large ones cause stock-outs or write-offs.
  • Guidance, where a large miss forces a profit warning (SEBI LODR Regulation 30 disclosure considerations) while small misses pass unnoticed.

Statistical role. Least-squares regression and many exponential-smoothing implementations (including the error statistics returned by FORECAST.ETS.STAT) are based on squared error. RMSE is therefore the natural metric for comparing and tuning statistical models (Topic 3, s07). Under normally distributed, unbiased errors, RMSE estimates the standard deviation of the forecast error, which can be used to set confidence intervals around the forecast (e.g., ± 1.96 × RMSE for an approximate 95% band).

RMSE vs MAD ratio. For normally distributed errors, RMSE ≈ 1.25 × MAD. A ratio much higher than 1.25 indicates lumpy errors — occasional big misses — which deserve root-cause analysis (large contracts slipping, one-off events).

Limitations. RMSE is scale-dependent (not comparable across BUs of different size unless normalised, e.g., RMSE ÷ mean actual, sometimes called CV(RMSE) or NRMSE), sensitive to outliers (one exceptional event can dominate), less intuitive for non-specialists, and directionless (pair with bias, c03). It is rarely the headline FP&A metric; WAPE usually is.

Illustration

Two BUs, six months, errors \(A - F\) in ₹ crore:

M1 M2 M3 M4 M5 M6
BU P 4 −4 4 −4 4 −4
BU Q 1 −1 1 −1 1 −19

MAD: P = 24 ÷ 6 = 4.0; Q = 24 ÷ 6 = 4.0 — identical.

\[ \text{RMSE}_P = \sqrt{\frac{6 \times 16}{6}} = 4.0, \qquad \text{RMSE}_Q = \sqrt{\frac{5 \times 1 + 361}{6}} = \sqrt{61.0} = 7.81 \]

Both BUs have the same average absolute miss, but Q's single ₹19 crore miss makes its RMSE nearly double. RMSE ÷ MAD = 1.0 for P (uniform errors) and 1.95 for Q (lumpy). For a cash forecast, Q's profile is far more dangerous. Note also the net error: for Q, Σ(A − F) = −18 (a net over-forecast), while P nets to zero.

Excel: =SQRT(SUMXMY2(Act,Fcst)/COUNT(Act)); DAX: RMSE := SQRT(AVERAGEX(Fact, (Fact[Actual] - Fact[Forecast])^2)).

Connections

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute RMSE and MAD [L1]

Weekly cash-collection forecast errors (A − F, ₹ crore) over eight weeks: 3, −2, 4, −1, −12, 2, −3, 1. Compute MAD, RMSE, the RMSE ÷ MAD ratio and bias in ₹. Interpret the ratio for a treasury team managing a ₹25 crore overdraft limit.

💡 Hint: Square each error before averaging; compare the ratio with about 1.25.

Q2. Choosing the metric [L2]

For each use, state whether WAPE, RMSE or MAD should be the primary error metric and justify briefly: (a) BU revenue accuracy league table across 8 BUs of very different sizes; (b) tuning the smoothing parameters of an ETS model for monthly volumes; (c) 13-week cash forecast near a covenant; (d) explaining accuracy to a non-finance BU head.

Q3. Forecast interval [L2]

A statistical model's monthly revenue RMSE on hold-out data is ₹6.5 crore and the errors appear unbiased and roughly normal. The October 2025 point forecast is ₹142 crore. Construct an approximate 80% and 95% interval, and explain two reasons the true interval may be wider in a festive month.
