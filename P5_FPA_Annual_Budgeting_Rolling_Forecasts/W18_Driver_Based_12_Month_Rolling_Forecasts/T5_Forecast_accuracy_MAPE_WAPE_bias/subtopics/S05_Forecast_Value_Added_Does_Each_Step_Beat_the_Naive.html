Subtopic note

Forecast Value Added: Does Each Step Beat the Naive Baseline?

Scope: Forecast Value Added (FVA): does each adjustment step improve on a naive/statistical baseline

Why it matters

Most forecasting processes have several layers of human touch: a statistical or system baseline, a sales or BU adjustment, a FP&A review, a management override before the Board. Each layer costs time — often days of the monthly cycle — and each is assumed to improve the number. Forecast Value Added (FVA), a method popularised in demand planning by Michael Gilliland (SAS), tests that assumption empirically. It frequently finds that some steps make the forecast worse — typically because of bias (sandbagging, optimism) or anchoring. For FP&A, FVA is the evidence base for streamlining the cycle (Topic 1's effort-vs-accuracy trade-off), for focusing human effort on lines where judgement genuinely helps, and for depersonalising conversations with BU heads about adjustments.

Core notes

Definition

For a forecasting process with steps \(0, 1, \dots, K\) (step 0 = naive baseline), FVA of step \(k\) relative to step \(j\) is:

\[ \text{FVA}_{k \text{ vs } j} = \text{Error}_j - \text{Error}_k \]

where Error is a chosen metric (WAPE, MAPE or MAD — c06 uses "error of naive minus error of submitted forecast"). Positive FVA means step \(k\) reduced error — it added value; negative FVA means it destroyed value. FVA is usually reported in percentage points of WAPE.

Both step-to-step FVA (did the BU adjustment improve on the statistical forecast?) and cumulative FVA (did the final submitted forecast beat naive?) are reported.

The process steps

A typical FP&A revenue forecasting chain:

Step Description Who
0. Naive Last-period or seasonal naive (same month last year × YTD growth) Automatic
1. Statistical Exponential smoothing / FORECAST.ETS, regression on drivers (Topic 3) FP&A analyst / system
2. Driver-based / BU BU adjusts for pipeline, pricing, launches BU head, sales
3. FP&A review Challenge, consistency, bias correction FP&A
4. Management override CFO/CEO adjustments before Board Leadership

Each step's forecast must be stored separately (as a sub-version or "Stage" column in the long-format table — Topic 4, s02) so that its error can be measured against actuals at the same lag.

Naive benchmarks

  • Naive (random walk): \(F_{t+h} = A_t\). Hard to beat for stable series with no seasonality.
  • Seasonal naive: \(F_{t+h} = A_{t+h-12}\), optionally scaled: \(F_{t+h} = A_{t+h-12} \times (1 + g_{\text{YTD}})\), where \(g_{\text{YTD}}\) is YTD growth versus prior-year YTD. Essential for Indian businesses with festive peaks (Oct–Nov), monsoon effects (Jun–Sep) and March year-end loading.
  • Moving average: \(F = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} A_{t-i}\).

The naive forecast is the bar every step must clear. Statistical methods should be selected only if they beat seasonal naive on hold-out data.

Worked illustration

Lag-3 revenue forecasts for a BU, Q3 FY26 (₹ crore):

Month Actual Seasonal naive Statistical (ETS) BU submission Final (after mgmt)
Oct 260 240 252 238 250
Nov 300 285 294 270 285
Dec 220 230 224 205 215
Σ|A − F| 45 18 67 30
WAPE (÷ 780) 5.8% 2.3% 8.6% 3.8%
Bias % −3.2% −1.3% −8.6% −3.8%

FVA (percentage points of WAPE, computed on unrounded WAPE):

  • Statistical vs naive: 5.77 − 2.31 = +3.5 pp — the model adds value.
  • BU vs statistical: 2.31 − 8.59 = −6.3 pp — the BU adjustment destroys value; its bias of −8.6% (all three months under) suggests sandbagging.
  • Management vs BU: 8.59 − 3.85 = +4.7 pp — leadership partially corrects the BU cushion.
  • Final vs naive (cumulative): 5.77 − 3.85 = +1.9 pp — overall positive, but the final forecast is still worse than the statistical forecast alone (3.8% vs 2.3%).

Conclusion: the process could have published the statistical forecast with less effort and better accuracy; the BU step needs redesign (explain deviations from statistical, accuracy-linked incentives), not more review layers.

Implementation

Long-format accuracy table: BU, Account, Target month, Lag, Stage (Naive, Stat, BU, FPA, Final), Forecast, Actual. Compute WAPE by Stage with SUMIFS on a precomputed absolute-error column, then FVA as the difference between adjacent stages:

WAPE_stage = SUMIFS(Acc[AbsErr],Acc[Stage],H$4,Acc[BU],$B6,Acc[Lag],SelLag)
            / SUMIFS(Acc[Actual],Acc[Stage],H$4,Acc[BU],$B6,Acc[Lag],SelLag)
FVA        = G6 - H6        // previous stage WAPE minus this stage WAPE

Present an FVA "stairstep" chart: one bar per stage showing WAPE, with step-to-step FVA labelled. In Power BI, Stage is a dimension and FVA a measure using CALCULATE with the prior stage.

Using FVA results

  • Remove or simplify steps with consistently negative FVA (often the case for low-value review layers).
  • Target judgement to lines where FVA is positive — typically new launches, large contracts, price changes — and let statistical forecasts run elsewhere ("forecast by exception").
  • Feed back results to BUs quarterly; pair with bias analysis (s04).
  • Shorten the cycle: if two of five steps add nothing, the monthly reforecast can close days earlier.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Sample size. FVA on one quarter is noisy; evaluate over at least 6–12 months and across many lines before removing a step.
  • Same lag for all stages. All stages must be measured at the same lag and against the same actuals; otherwise later stages look better simply because they were made later.
  • Information vs skill. A BU adjustment may add value because it incorporates information the statistical model lacks (a lost customer); FVA rewards that correctly. It penalises adjustments driven by bias.
  • Choice of error metric. WAPE-based FVA weights large lines; MAPE-based FVA can be dominated by small lines. Use WAPE for finance.
  • Gaming the baseline. If the naive or statistical forecast is poorly specified (wrong seasonality), FVA of human steps is overstated. Validate the baseline (e.g., FORECAST.ETS with seasonality = 12 for monthly data).
  • Bias vs accuracy. A step can reduce WAPE while increasing bias (or vice versa); report both.

Key takeaways

  • FVA = error of the earlier (or naive) step minus error of the later step; positive = value added.
  • Store every process stage's forecast separately and measure all at the same lag.
  • Seasonal naive is the minimum benchmark for seasonal Indian businesses; statistical forecasts must beat it.
  • Negative FVA at a judgement step usually signals bias (sandbagging, optimism) or anchoring.
  • Use FVA to remove low-value steps, focus judgement by exception and shorten the forecast cycle.

Practice Questions

Q1. Compute stage FVA [L2]

For a BU's Q1 FY26 revenue at lag-2 (₹ crore): Actual Apr 80, May 85, Jun 90. Naive (last period) 78, 80, 85. Statistical 82, 84, 88. BU 76, 79, 83. FP&A-adjusted 80, 84, 88. Compute WAPE and bias % for each stage and FVA step by step and cumulatively versus naive. Recommend which stages to keep.

💡 Hint: Use total actuals of 255 as the WAPE denominator for every stage.

Q2. Seasonal naive construction [L2]

October 2024 revenue was ₹180 crore. April–September FY26 YTD revenue is ₹960 crore versus ₹900 crore in April–September FY25. (a) Construct the scaled seasonal-naive forecast for October 2025. (b) Write the Excel formula using XLOOKUP on a long table Act (Month, Amt) and EDATE. (c) Explain why plain last-period naive would be a poor benchmark for October in a festive-driven business.

Q3. FVA programme design [L3]

The CFO of an NSE-listed FMCG company wants to cut the monthly reforecast from 9 to 5 working days. The current process has five stages for 12 BUs and 8 accounts. Design an FVA study: data to capture, lag, metric, duration, decision rules for removing or keeping stages, and how results will be communicated to BU heads.

Q4. Interpreting mixed results [L2]

A BU's adjustment step improves WAPE by 1.5 pp but moves bias from −0.5% to +4.2%. Explain how this can happen, whether the step adds value, and what further analysis you would perform.
