Subtopic note

Detecting Sandbagging and Over-Optimism by Business Unit

Scope: Detecting sandbagging (persistent under-forecast of revenue) and over-optimism by BU

Why it matters

A forecast error is either noise or bias. Noise is unavoidable and averages out; bias is systematic and, in finance forecasts, frequently behavioural. Sandbagging — persistently under-forecasting revenue (or over-forecasting costs) so that results "beat" the forecast — protects the forecaster's incentive and reputation but starves the organisation of accurate information: plants are under-loaded, working capital is mis-planned, and the company under-guides the market and then repeatedly "surprises" analysts, which itself damages credibility. Over-optimism — persistently over-forecasting revenue or under-forecasting costs — leads to over-production, excess inventory, unachieved guidance and late-year cost cuts. Detecting both by BU, with evidence robust enough to discuss with a BU head, is a core FP&A capability and a frequent interview case.

Core notes

Definitions and direction

Using Bias % \(= \sum (F - A) \div \sum A\) (c03):

Line Bias sign Interpretation
Revenue Negative, persistent Sandbagging (under-forecast)
Revenue Positive, persistent Over-optimism
Cost Positive, persistent Padding / cost sandbagging (over-forecast spend)
Cost Negative, persistent Under-estimation (optimism about cost control)

The key word is persistent. One quarter of negative bias is not sandbagging; six consecutive quarters is a pattern.

Statistical detection tools

  1. Bias % by BU over rolling windows — e.g., rolling 6 or 12 months at lag-3. Flag BUs whose bias exceeds a tolerance (say ±3%) for three or more consecutive windows.
  2. Tracking signal (c04) — cumulative \((A - F)\) ÷ MAD. For revenue, \(\text{TS} > +4\) (actuals persistently above forecast) indicates sandbagging; \(\text{TS} < -4\) indicates over-optimism.
  3. Sign test / run count — count the months in which actual exceeded forecast. Under no bias, the probability of a beat is about 50% each month, so ten or more beats out of twelve has a binomial probability of about 1.9% (formula below) — strong evidence of bias.
  4. Forecast evolution pattern — a sandbagging BU's forecasts for a given month typically rise as the month approaches (it releases the cushion late); an over-optimistic BU's forecasts fall late. Visible in the forecast triangle (s02).
  5. Beat size relative to noise — beats consistently 5–10% above forecast, with low dispersion, suggest a deliberate cushion rather than forecasting difficulty.
  6. Comparison with statistical baseline — if a seasonal-naive or exponential-smoothing forecast (Topic 3, s07) is unbiased while the BU's submission is biased, the bias is introduced by judgement (FVA analysis, s05).

The sign-test probability in item 3, with \(X\) the number of beats in 12 months:

\[ P(X \ge 10) = \sum_{k=10}^{12} \binom{12}{k} 0.5^{12} = \frac{66 + 12 + 1}{4{,}096} \approx 1.9\% \]

In Excel: =1-BINOM.DIST(9,12,0.5,TRUE).

Worked illustration

Quarterly lag-3 revenue for two BUs, FY25 Q1 to FY26 Q2 (₹ crore):

Quarter North F North A South F South A
FY25 Q1 200 214 160 151
FY25 Q2 210 228 168 155
FY25 Q3 250 268 190 178
FY25 Q4 230 245 175 169
FY26 Q1 215 233 170 158
FY26 Q2 225 240 178 166
Total 1,330 1,428 1,041 977
  • North: bias % = (1,330 − 1,428) ÷ 1,428 = −6.9%; beat in 6 of 6 quarters; average beat ₹16.3 crore with little variation → consistent with sandbagging.
  • South: bias % = (1,041 − 977) ÷ 977 = +6.6%; missed in 6 of 6 quarters → consistent with over-optimism.
  • Company: forecast 2,371 vs actual 2,405 → bias −1.4%. The two BUs largely offset at company level (s03), which is why BU-level analysis is essential.

Behavioural and organisational root causes

  • Incentive design: bonuses paid on beating a self-set forecast or on budget attainment where the BU influences the budget. Indian sales-incentive schemes often pay slabs at 90%/100%/110% of target, which rewards a cushion.
  • Asymmetric penalties: a miss is punished more than a beat is rewarded, so forecasters build a buffer.
  • Resource protection: over-optimism in revenue (or under-forecast cost) can protect headcount, A&P or capex allocations ("if we show growth, we keep the budget").
  • Anchoring to budget: forecasts that stay close to the AOP long after conditions changed; bias then flips sign with the economic cycle.
  • Hockey-stick culture: H2-loaded forecasts each quarter ("the recovery is next quarter") — a classic over-optimism pattern.

Remedies

  1. Publish bias by BU in the forecast-accuracy dashboard (s06); transparency alone reduces sandbagging.
  2. Decouple incentives from forecasts: base bonuses on externally set targets (AOP approved top-down, relative performance, prior-year growth), and add a forecast-accuracy component (e.g., 10% of variable pay for WAPE and bias within tolerance).
  3. Statistical baselines: require BUs to explain deviations from a statistical forecast; judgement must add value (FVA).
  4. Central overlay: FP&A applies a documented bias correction (e.g., North +5%) to the consolidated forecast used for guidance, while still recording the BU's own submission for accountability.
  5. Pre-mortems and reference-class forecasting for optimism-prone areas such as new launches and projects.

Accounting and governance link

Sandbagged LEs can understate the year-end bonus accrual under Ind AS 19 short-term employee benefits (where bonuses depend on attainment) until late in the year, causing Q4 catch-ups; over-optimistic forecasts can support impairment tests (Ind AS 36) or deferred-tax asset recognition (Ind AS 12) that later prove unfounded — auditors examine historical forecast accuracy for exactly this reason ("management's track record").

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Genuine structural change. A BU in a rapidly growing market may under-forecast persistently because growth keeps surprising; check whether external indicators (market data, competitor growth) also surprised. Bias still needs correcting, but the conversation differs.
  • Lumpy B2B revenue. A BU with few large contracts will show large errors with random sign; require more periods before concluding bias, and analyse at contract-pipeline level.
  • Cost sandbagging is often invisible. Under-spends are celebrated as savings. Track cost bias as rigorously as revenue bias.
  • Prudence vs sandbagging. A deliberately conservative external guidance is a legitimate policy decision made centrally and disclosed as such; the internal forecast should still be unbiased.
  • Small samples. Use sign tests and rolling windows; three quarters are rarely enough for a confident conclusion.

Key takeaways

  • Sandbagging = persistent negative revenue bias (or positive cost bias); over-optimism = persistent positive revenue bias.
  • Detect with rolling bias %, tracking signal, sign/run tests, forecast-evolution patterns and comparison with statistical baselines.
  • Measure at BU level: offsetting biases disappear at company level.
  • Root causes are usually incentive design, asymmetric penalties, resource protection and anchoring.
  • Remedies: publish bias, decouple incentives from self-set forecasts, add an accuracy component, require explanation versus statistical baselines.

Practice Questions

Q1. Diagnose two BUs [L2]

Monthly lag-1 revenue for Apr–Sep FY26 (₹ crore). BU A: F 42, 44, 45, 47, 50, 52; A 45, 47, 48, 51, 53, 56. BU B: F 30, 32, 35, 38, 40, 44; A 29, 30, 31, 33, 34, 36. (a) Compute bias %, MAD and tracking signal for each. (b) Compute the probability of BU A's beat record under the no-bias assumption. (c) Classify each BU and propose one remedy for each.

💡 Hint: For the sign test, count the months where actual exceeded forecast and use the binomial distribution with p = 0.5.

Q2. Forecast-evolution evidence [L2]

The North BU's successive forecasts for December 2025 revenue were: Q1F ₹180 crore; LE Sep ₹182 crore; LE Oct ₹186 crore; LE Nov ₹195 crore. Actual was ₹201 crore. The South BU's successive forecasts for the same month were ₹150, ₹148, ₹141 and ₹136 crore against actual ₹131 crore. Interpret the patterns and explain what each suggests about the BU's forecasting behaviour.

Q3. Incentive scheme redesign [L3]

An NSE-listed paints company pays regional managers 0% bonus below 95% of their own quarterly forecast, 100% at 100%, and 150% at 110% or above. Four of six regions show persistent negative revenue bias of 5–9%. Redesign the scheme to reduce sandbagging while retaining motivation, specify an accuracy component, and explain the implications for the Ind AS 19 bonus provision estimation at each quarter-end.

Q4. Interview: sandbagging conversation [L3]

You must present evidence of sandbagging to a senior BU head who has a strong track record of "beating forecast". Outline the evidence pack you would prepare, how you would frame the discussion, and the specific process changes you would propose.
