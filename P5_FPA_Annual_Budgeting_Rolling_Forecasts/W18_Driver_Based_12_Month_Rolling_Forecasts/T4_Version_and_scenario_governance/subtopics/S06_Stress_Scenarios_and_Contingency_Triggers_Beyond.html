Stress Scenarios and Contingency Triggers Beyond Sensitivities

Scope: Scenario planning beyond sensitivities: stress cases (commodity spike, demand shock, FX), contingency triggers

Why it matters

Sensitivities tell management how much EBITDA moves for each ₹1/kg or 1% change in a single driver. They do not tell management what happens when the world turns against the business in several correlated ways at once — which is how real shocks arrive. The 2021–22 commodity super-cycle (steel, palm oil, crude derivatives), the 2020 demand collapse and the rupee's slide past ₹85/USD each hit Indian companies through multiple channels simultaneously: input costs, volumes, price pass-through lags, working capital and borrowing costs. Boards and lenders increasingly expect a stress case with pre-agreed responses. For listed companies the Audit Committee's oversight of risk management (SEBI LODR Regulation 21, which mandates a Risk Management Committee for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation, with its role set out in Schedule II), going-concern assessment under Ind AS 1, and covenant headroom analysis for lenders all rely on credible stress scenarios.

Core notes

From sensitivity to scenario to stress

Level What changes Purpose Example
Sensitivity One driver, others held constant Rank drivers by impact; tornado chart Palm oil +10%
Scenario A coherent set of linked drivers Plausible alternative futures (Upside/Downside) Weak monsoon: rural volume −6%, trade discount +1 pp, debtor days +5
Stress case Severe but plausible combination, often 1-in-10 or 1-in-20 Test resilience: liquidity, covenants, going concern Commodity spike + demand shock + INR −8%
Reverse stress test Solve for the conditions that break the business Identify the breaking point What volume decline breaches the 3.0× Net debt/EBITDA covenant?

The defining feature of a scenario (c05) is internal consistency: assumptions move together according to a narrative and the causal links between them.

Designing stress cases

1. Commodity spike. Identify the commodities that make up most of COGS (Pareto). Specify the price path (not just a level), the pass-through rate to customers and the lag. Example for a packaged-foods company: palm oil +25% from November, 60% passed through with a two-month lag, competitors following; volume −3% due to price increases (elasticity); inventory gains for one month if stock was bought earlier (FIFO/weighted average cost under Ind AS 2).

2. Demand shock. Specify which channels and geographies fall, by how much and for how long; the knock-on to trade spends (higher discounts to clear stock), inventory build-up (working capital), fixed-cost absorption at plants (under-recovery charged to P&L under Ind AS 2 if production is abnormally low — fixed overheads are allocated on normal capacity), and receivables quality (higher expected credit loss under Ind AS 109).

3. FX shock. Separate (a) transaction exposure — imported inputs, USD-priced exports, foreign-currency borrowings (ECBs) and their interest — from (b) translation exposure on foreign subsidiaries (Ind AS 21). Include hedge cover: if 60% of next six months' imports are hedged with forwards at ₹86.0, only the unhedged 40% bears the spot shock in that window. For an exporter, INR depreciation is favourable; for an importer, adverse; for a company with USD debt, it raises the rupee value of debt and interest.

4. Combined stress. Combine the above with explicit correlation logic — e.g., a global commodity spike often coincides with INR weakness (oil importer economy), which compounds the cost shock for an importer.

Quantifying the stress case

Run the stress through the full driver model (and ideally the three-statement model) to show:

  • EBITDA, PAT and EPS by quarter.
  • Cash flow, closing cash and undrawn facilities (liquidity runway).
  • Covenant ratios: Net debt/EBITDA, DSCR, interest cover, against thresholds.
  • Key working-capital metrics (debtor, inventory and creditor days).

Illustration

Deccan Packaging Ltd, H2 FY26 (₹ crore):

Item Base Stress Stress logic
Revenue 1,200 1,098 Volume −10%; price +1.7% partial pass-through
Polymer cost 600 648 Volume −10%, polymer price +20%
Other variable costs 180 162 Move with volume
Fixed costs 240 240 Unchanged in H2 (no action yet)
EBITDA 180 48
H2 interest 45 49 Higher utilisation of working-capital lines

Revenue check: \(1{,}200 \times 0.90 \times 1.017 \approx 1{,}098\). Polymer: \(600 \times 0.90 \times 1.20 = 648\). EBITDA falls by 73% — far more than any single sensitivity would suggest, because volume loss, cost spike and incomplete pass-through compound. If the covenant is Net debt/EBITDA ≤ 3.0× on a trailing-12-month basis and net debt is ₹1,050 crore with H1 EBITDA of ₹190 crore, TTM EBITDA falls from ₹370 crore (2.8×) to ₹238 crore (4.4×) — a breach.

Contingency triggers

A stress case is only actionable if management agrees in advance what it will do and when. A contingency trigger has three parts: a measurable leading indicator, a threshold, and a pre-approved action with an owner and quantified impact.

Trigger indicator Threshold Pre-agreed action Owner EBITDA/cash impact
Polymer price (₹/kg, 4-week avg) > ₹120/kg for 4 weeks Invoke price-escalation clauses; 3% list price increase CCO +₹25 cr H2
Secondary sales volume vs Q2F < 92% for 2 consecutive months Freeze discretionary A&P and hiring; defer non-critical capex CFO +₹30 cr EBITDA; ₹60 cr capex deferred
USD/INR spot > ₹90 Raise hedge cover on next 6 months' imports to 80% Treasurer Caps FX loss
TTM Net debt/EBITDA (forecast) > 2.75× Initiate covenant waiver discussion; draw contingency line CFO Liquidity protection

Triggers convert scenario planning from a Board-pack appendix into an operating mechanism. Monitor trigger indicators monthly in the MBR (Week 20) dashboard.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Plausibility vs severity. A stress case that is implausibly extreme is ignored; one that is too mild gives false comfort. Anchor severity to history (e.g., the worst 12-month commodity move in the last ten years) and state the return period.
  • Management actions in the stress case. Show stress both before and after mitigating actions; Boards want to see the unmitigated hit and the credibility of the response separately.
  • Accounting consequences. Severe stress may trigger impairment indicators (Ind AS 36), onerous-contract provisions (Ind AS 37), inventory write-downs to NRV (Ind AS 2) and going-concern disclosures (Ind AS 1). Coordinate with financial reporting.
  • Non-linear effects. Covenant breaches, credit-rating downgrades and loss of a key customer are step changes; model them as discrete events, not smooth flexes.
  • Storage. Stress cases are scenarios within a version (e.g., Q2F / Stress) in the long fact table (s02); never a separate workbook that drifts from the base model.
  • Reverse stress testing. Use Goal Seek or a data table to find the volume decline or commodity price at which a covenant breaks or cash runs out; this is often more informative to the Board than any single stress case.

Key takeaways

  • Sensitivity flexes one variable; scenarios and stress cases flex a coherent, linked set of assumptions.
  • Design stress cases for commodity spikes (price path, pass-through, lag), demand shocks (volume, discounts, absorption, working capital) and FX (transaction, translation, hedge cover).
  • Combined stresses compound: EBITDA impact is usually far larger than the sum of individual sensitivities suggests.
  • Always test liquidity and covenants, not only EBITDA.
  • Contingency triggers = leading indicator + threshold + pre-approved action with owner and quantified impact.
  • Stress cases are scenarios stored in the same fact table and engine as the base forecast.

Practice Questions

Q1. Build a combined stress case [L3]

A Gujarat-based specialty-chemicals company has H2 FY26 Base revenue ₹900 crore (45% exports in USD at ₹87/USD), raw-material cost ₹495 crore (70% imported, priced in USD), other variable costs ₹90 crore and fixed costs ₹150 crore. Design a combined stress case with: export volume −12%, domestic volume −5%, crude-linked raw-material USD price +18%, USD/INR at ₹91, and 50% of imports hedged at ₹87. (a) Compute stress revenue, raw-material cost and EBITDA, assuming no price pass-through and that variable costs move with total volume. (b) Explain why the FX shock partly offsets. (c) Identify which assumption you would challenge first for internal consistency.

💡 Hint: Treat export revenue and imported raw material separately for the FX effect, and apply the hedge only to the imported portion.

Q2. Contingency trigger design [L2]

For a quick-service restaurant chain with 420 outlets, design four contingency triggers covering same-store sales, dairy and edible-oil prices, delivery-aggregator commission rates and rental renegotiations. For each, specify the indicator, threshold, action, owner and a quantified impact estimate with stated assumptions.

Q3. Covenant headroom under stress [L2]

Net debt is ₹840 crore. Base TTM EBITDA for March 2026 is ₹320 crore. The covenant is Net debt/EBITDA ≤ 3.25×. (a) Compute the maximum EBITDA decline before breach. (b) If the stress case reduces EBITDA by ₹70 crore and increases net debt by ₹60 crore (working-capital build), compute the stressed ratio. (c) List two pre-agreed actions that could restore headroom and quantify what each must deliver.

Q4. Sensitivity mislabelled as stress [L1]

A Board pack's "Stress case" applies −15% to revenue with all costs unchanged. List four ways this is inconsistent with a genuine demand-shock scenario and state the direction of the error in EBITDA and in cash flow.
