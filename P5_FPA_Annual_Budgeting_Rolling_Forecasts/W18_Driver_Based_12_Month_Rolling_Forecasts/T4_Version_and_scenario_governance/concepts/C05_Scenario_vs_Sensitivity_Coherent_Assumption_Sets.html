Key concept

Scenario vs Sensitivity: Coherent Assumption Sets vs One-Variable Flex

Concept: Scenario vs sensitivity: sensitivity changes one variable; scenario changes a coherent set of linked assumptions

Statement

Sensitivity — the change in an output \(Y\) when one input \(x_k\) changes by \(\Delta x_k\), all other inputs held at base:

\[ \Delta Y^{\text{sens}}_k = Y(x_1, \dots, x_k + \Delta x_k, \dots, x_n) - Y(x_1, \dots, x_n) \]

Scenario — the change in \(Y\) when a vector of inputs changes together according to a narrative \(s\), with the changes linked by causal relationships:

\[ \Delta Y^{\text{scen}}_s = Y(\mathbf{x} + \Delta \mathbf{x}_s) - Y(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \Delta \mathbf{x}_s = (\Delta x_{1,s}, \dots, \Delta x_{n,s}) \]

where \(\mathbf{x}\) is the base input vector and \(\Delta \mathbf{x}_s\) is the internally consistent set of changes in scenario \(s\). In general \(\Delta Y^{\text{scen}}_s \neq \sum_k \Delta Y^{\text{sens}}_k\) because of interaction effects.

Explanation

Sensitivity analysis answers "which drivers matter most?" It is used for tornado charts (Topic 3, c05), data tables (WACC × terminal growth in DCF), and break-even questions. Its limitation is that real drivers do not move alone: a fall in volume is usually accompanied by higher discounts, lower variable costs, inventory build-up and longer debtor days.

Scenario analysis answers "what does the business look like if this story happens?" Its defining property is internal consistency — every assumption is compatible with the narrative and with the others:

  • Demand shock: volume ↓, trade discount ↑, variable freight ↓ (fewer tonnes), fixed-cost absorption ↓, debtor days ↑, inventory days ↑.
  • Commodity spike: input price ↑, selling price ↑ with lag and partial pass-through, volume ↓ via elasticity, working-capital value ↑.
  • INR depreciation for an importer: import cost ↑, export realisation ↑, foreign-currency debt and interest ↑, hedge gains if hedged.

Tests of a genuine scenario:

  1. More than one assumption changes.
  2. Each change can be explained by the narrative.
  3. Linked items move in the right direction (variable costs with volume; working capital with activity).
  4. Accounting consequences are captured (Ind AS 2 NRV write-down, Ind AS 109 ECL, Ind AS 36 impairment indicators).
  5. Balance sheet and cash flow are affected, not just the P&L.

A ±10% revenue flex labelled "Downside" fails tests 1–3; it is a sensitivity. Both tools are needed: sensitivities identify which drivers to build scenarios around; scenarios describe coherent futures for decisions and Board discussion.

Illustration

Base H2 for Malabar Spices: volume 20,000 t, price ₹500/kg, variable cost ₹320/kg, fixed costs ₹180 crore.

\[ \text{EBITDA}_{\text{base}} = 20{,}000 \times 1{,}000 \times (500 - 320) - 180 \text{ crore} = 360 - 180 = \text{₹}180 \text{ crore} \]

"Downside" as a sensitivity (revenue −10%, costs unchanged): revenue falls ₹100 crore; EBITDA = ₹80 crore. This wrongly keeps variable cost constant while volume falls (if volume drives the fall) and ignores working capital.

Downside as a scenario (export demand shock): volume −8% to 18,400 t; price −2% to ₹490/kg (discounting); variable cost ₹320/kg unchanged per kg; fixed costs ₹180 crore; debtor days 45 → 55.

\[ \text{EBITDA}_{\text{scen}} = 18{,}400 \times 1{,}000 \times (490 - 320) - 180 \text{ crore} = 312.8 - 180 = \text{₹}132.8 \text{ crore} \]

The scenario shows a smaller EBITDA hit than the crude sensitivity (variable costs fall with volume) but adds a working-capital outflow of roughly \(\frac{10}{365} \times \text{annualised revenue}\) — information the sensitivity never surfaces.

Practice Questions

Q1. Classify and repair [L1]

Classify each as a sensitivity or a scenario and, for each sensitivity, list two linked assumptions needed to turn it into a scenario: (a) WACC ±1% data table; (b) "Monsoon failure": rural volume −7%, trade schemes +1.5 pp, receivables +6 days; (c) steel price +15%; (d) "Competitor entry": price −4%, A&P +₹20 crore, volume −3%.

Q2. Interaction effects [L2]

Using the Malabar Spices base data, compute the EBITDA effect of (a) volume −8% alone, (b) price −2% alone, (c) both together. Show that (c) is not the sum of (a) and (b) and quantify the interaction term.

💡 Hint: The interaction term equals the change in volume multiplied by the change in unit margin.

Q3. Board challenge [L3]

A director says: "Your Downside is just a 10% haircut on revenue. What would actually happen to cash if export demand fell?" Draft the revised scenario specification (at least six linked assumptions across P&L, working capital and capex) and explain how you would present it alongside the existing sensitivity table.
