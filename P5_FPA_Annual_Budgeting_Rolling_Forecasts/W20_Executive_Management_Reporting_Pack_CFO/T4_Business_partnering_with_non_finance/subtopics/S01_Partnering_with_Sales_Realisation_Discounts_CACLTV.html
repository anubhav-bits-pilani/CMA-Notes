Subtopic note

Partnering with Sales: Realisation, Discounts, CAC/LTV, Collections

Scope: Sales: price realisation, discount discipline, customer profitability, CAC/LTV, collections

Why it matters

Sales is usually the biggest single driver of the P&L and of receivables, and it is also the function that listens least to finance. A sales head reads the business through volume, order book, wins and targets. Revenue on the income statement is a net number: list price less trade discounts, schemes, rebates, cash discounts and returns. Between list price and cash in the bank, most of the margin leakage sits in places that sales controls: extra discounts, scheme overruns, unprofitable key accounts, acquisition spend that never pays back, and credit given to customers who pay late. When the FP&A partner can show the sales head where those leakages are, in sales language, the MBR stops being a variance-explanation exercise and becomes a margin-recovery conversation.

Under Ind AS 115 most of these items reduce revenue anyway. Volume rebates, price protection, cash discounts offered as incentives and consideration payable to a customer (listing fees, slotting fees, co-op advertising without a distinct service) are variable consideration or deductions from the transaction price (Ind AS 115.50–59, 115.70–72). So "discount discipline" is a reported-revenue issue, not only a commercial one.

Core notes

Price realisation

Price realisation is the net price actually received per unit compared with the list or budgeted price. Define it carefully once and keep it fixed in the KPI definition sheet:

\[ \text{Realisation \%} = \frac{\text{Net revenue per unit}}{\text{List price per unit}} \times 100 \]

where net revenue per unit is after on-invoice discounts, off-invoice schemes, rebates, returns provisions and cash discounts, and before GST (GST is not revenue; it is collected on behalf of government). A price waterfall shows the path step by step:

Step ₹ per unit % of list
List price 1,000 100.0
Less: on-invoice trade discount (80) 8.0
Invoice price 920 92.0
Less: quarterly volume rebate accrual (30) 3.0
Less: scheme / promotional support (25) 2.5
Less: cash discount for early payment (15) 1.5
Pocket price (net realisation) 850 85.0

The waterfall links directly to the Week 19 PVM engine: the price variance is computed on pocket price, not invoice price, otherwise scheme leakage hides inside "other expenses" or "trade spend". For a sales partner the message becomes: "every 1 point of realisation on ₹400 crore gross sales is ₹4 crore of EBITDA, the same as selling 6% more volume at our current contribution margin of about 17%".

Discount discipline

Discount discipline means discounts are granted within an approved matrix and are measurable after the event. The practical tools are:

  • Discount authority matrix — sales executive up to 3%, regional manager up to 6%, national head up to 10%, anything beyond to the pricing committee (see s06).
  • Discount leakage report — invoices priced below the floor, by salesperson and customer, monthly.
  • Scheme ROI — incremental volume and contribution from a scheme versus its cost; compare to a baseline, not to last month.
  • Price-volume scatter — net price versus annual volume by customer; customers far below the trend line receive more discount than their volume justifies.

The partner's framing is not "you are discounting too much" but "these 14 customers are 9 points below the price curve for their size; bringing half of them to the curve is worth ₹1.8 crore a year".

Customer profitability

Gross margin by customer is misleading because the costs that differ between customers are below gross margin: freight, special packaging, merchandisers, returns handling, credit cost and key-account management time. Customer profitability (concept c05) is:

\[ \text{Customer profit} = \text{Net revenue} - \text{COGS} - \text{Cost-to-serve} \]

Cost-to-serve is allocated using activity drivers — number of orders, drops, returns, sales visits, days of credit — rather than revenue share. The output is a "whale curve": cumulative profit plotted against customers ranked by profitability. Typically the top 20–30% of customers generate 150–300% of profit and the tail destroys part of it. The decision options for the tail are reprice, change service model (distributor, minimum order quantity, digital ordering), or exit.

CAC and LTV

For subscription, D2C, SaaS, telecom, NBFC and insurance businesses, acquisition economics matter more than monthly margin. Customer acquisition cost (c01) is sales and marketing spend divided by new customers acquired; lifetime value (c02) is ARPU × gross margin % ÷ churn rate. LTV/CAC of at least 3 and CAC payback under 12–18 months are typical benchmarks. The partner's job is to compute these by channel (performance marketing, inside sales, field sales, partners) because the blended number hides channels that are destroying value.

Collections

Sales owns the customer relationship, so sales owns collections in practice even when credit control sits in finance. Partnering points:

  • DSO and overdue ageing by salesperson and region, published in the sales review, not only in the finance pack.
  • Linking a part of sales incentive to collected revenue rather than billed revenue (common in Indian B2B and channel businesses).
  • Quantifying the cost of credit: overdue balance × cost of debt, plus the expected credit loss provision under Ind AS 109 simplified approach (provision matrix on trade receivables).
  • MSME angle in reverse: if your customers are large companies and you are an MSME, Sec 15 of the MSMED Act and Sec 43B(h) of the Income-tax Act (from AY 2024-25) push them to pay within 45 days — a negotiation lever.

Illustration: annual credit sales ₹365 crore, DSO 75 days. Each day of DSO is ₹1 crore of receivables; reducing DSO to 60 days releases ₹15 crore (concept c03). At 9% cost of debt that is ₹1.35 crore of annual interest saved — a number a sales head can compare with a 0.5% cash discount offer.

Packaging the sales review page

A sales partner page typically has four blocks: net realisation trend versus budget and PY; top 10 customers by profit (not revenue) with cost-to-serve; discount and scheme spend versus approved; DSO and overdue ageing by region with named owners. Each block ends with one proposed action.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mix versus price: realisation can fall because the mix shifts to a lower-priced pack, not because of discounting. Separate mix from price in the PVM before accusing sales of leakage.
  • Accrual timing of rebates: annual volume rebates must be accrued using the expected-value or most-likely amount with the constraint on variable consideration (Ind AS 115.56); under-accrual in early quarters flatters realisation and reverses in Q4.
  • Strategic customers: a loss-making anchor customer may carry fixed-cost absorption or be strategic for a new category; show the contribution after variable cost-to-serve as well as fully loaded profit.
  • CAC definition: whether to include brand spend, onboarding cost and sales salaries changes CAC by 2–3x; agree it once and disclose.
  • Collections-linked incentives can push sales to reduce credit sales and lose good customers; pair DSO targets with revenue growth targets.

Key takeaways

  • Measure realisation at pocket price after all schemes and rebates; this is the basis for the price variance.
  • Discount discipline needs an authority matrix, a leakage report and scheme ROI, not exhortation.
  • Customer profitability is a cost-to-serve question; gross margin by customer is not enough.
  • CAC and LTV must be calculated by channel, with definitions locked.
  • Convert DSO into ₹ of cash and ₹ of interest so sales can trade it against discounts.
  • Every sales page should end in a quantified action with an owner.

Practice Questions

Q1. Price waterfall build [L1]

A consumer durables company has list price ₹24,000 per unit, on-invoice discount 6%, quarterly target rebate ₹600 per unit, festive scheme ₹900 per unit and cash discount 1.5% of invoice price. Annual volume is 1,20,000 units. Build the price waterfall in ₹ and % of list, and compute the annual EBITDA impact of reducing the festive scheme by one-third.

Q2. Discount leakage review [L2]

Your ERP extract shows 2,400 invoices in Q2. 310 invoices were priced below the floor price set by the pricing committee, with an average shortfall of ₹1,150 per invoice and an average invoice of 40 units. Twelve salespersons account for 70% of the below-floor invoices. Draft the analysis you would present to the national sales head, the action you would propose and the control you would add to the order-to-cash process.

💡 Hint: Quantify the annualised leakage before naming individuals, and think about a system block at order entry.

Q3. Collections versus cash discount [L2]

A B2B chemicals company has annual credit sales of ₹730 crore and DSO of 82 days. Sales proposes a 1% cash discount for payment within 15 days and expects 40% of customers by value to take it, bringing their DSO to 15 days; the rest stay at 82. Cost of debt is 9.5%. Evaluate whether the proposal creates value and how you would present it to the sales head.

💡 Hint: Compare discount cost on the accepting share of revenue with interest saved on the released receivables.

Q4. Whale curve conversation [L3]

Customer profitability analysis shows your top 25 of 400 dealers generate 180% of operating profit and the bottom 120 dealers lose ₹6.5 crore a year after cost-to-serve. The regional sales heads insist all dealers are needed for market coverage. Prepare the talking points for a meeting with them, including at least three options other than exit and how you would test the coverage argument with data.

Q5. Interview: realisation drop [L2]

In an interview you are told net realisation fell 2.3 points against budget while list prices were unchanged. List the analyses you would run, in order, to explain the drop and decide whether sales discount discipline is the cause.
