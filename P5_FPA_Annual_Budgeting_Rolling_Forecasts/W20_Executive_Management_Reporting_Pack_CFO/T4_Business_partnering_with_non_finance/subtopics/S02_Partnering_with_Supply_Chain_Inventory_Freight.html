Subtopic note

Partnering with Supply Chain: Inventory, Freight, OTIF, Cost-to-Serve

Scope: Supply chain: inventory days, obsolescence, freight cost per unit, OTIF, cost-to-serve

Why it matters

Supply chain decisions drive three of the largest numbers in the MBR: inventory (often 30–50% of net working capital in manufacturing and FMCG), logistics cost (3–8% of revenue in Indian FMCG, building materials and chemicals) and service levels that decide whether the revenue budget is actually delivered. Supply chain heads think in plans, lead times, safety stock, truck utilisation and service; finance thinks in days, provisions, cost per unit and cash. The partner has to translate both ways, and above all must stop the classic conflict in which finance demands lower inventory while sales demands higher OTIF and supply chain is blamed for both.

Core notes

Inventory days

\[ \text{DIO} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365 \]

using closing or average inventory, applied consistently (state which in the KPI sheet). Split inventory days by stage, because each has a different owner and lever:

Stage Owner Typical lever
Raw and packing material Procurement / planning MOQ, vendor-managed inventory, lead-time reduction
Work-in-progress Manufacturing Batch size, cycle time, line balancing
Finished goods at plant / depots Planning / logistics Forecast accuracy, depot rationalisation
Goods in transit Logistics Mode choice, clearance times (imports)

Days should also be computed by SKU class (ABC by value, XYZ by demand variability) because a single DIO hides a few fast movers with 10 days cover and a long tail with 200 days cover. Inventory value under Ind AS 2 is at lower of cost and NRV, so "inventory days" in the MBR should be stated net of provision, with gross days shown alongside to reveal ageing.

Cash impact (concept c03): reducing DIO from 72 to 60 days on COGS of ₹1,095 crore releases \(12 \times 1{,}095 / 365 = 36\) crore.

Obsolescence

Obsolescence arises from SKU discontinuation, shelf-life expiry (pharma, food, cosmetics), technology changes and overproduction. Accounting and partnering points:

  • Ind AS 2.28–33 requires write-down to net realisable value; most companies use an ageing-based provision matrix (for example 0–180 days nil, 181–365 days 25%, >365 days 50–100%) plus specific provisions for discontinued SKUs. Reversal is allowed when NRV recovers (Ind AS 2.33).
  • Near-expiry stock in FMCG and pharma: track "days to expiry" against channel norms (distributors often reject stock with less than 60–70% residual shelf life).
  • Tax: a write-down reflecting a genuine fall in NRV of identified items is effectively allowed because ICDS II values inventory at lower of cost and NRV; a general or ad hoc obsolescence provision is usually disallowed until the stock is written off or sold — a deferred tax item to flag.
  • Partnering output: an "at-risk inventory" list with value, ageing, liquidation options (discount channel, rework, export, donation, scrap) and an owner, reviewed monthly in S&OP.

Freight cost per unit

\[ \text{Freight cost per unit} = \frac{\text{Primary + secondary freight cost}}{\text{Units (or tonnes, or cases) shipped}} \]

Report primary (plant to depot) and secondary (depot to customer) separately, and decompose the variance into rate (diesel-linked rate revisions, spot hiring), utilisation (truck fill percentage, part-truckloads), mix of lanes (distance) and mode (road, rail, coastal). A useful decomposition:

\[ \text{Freight per tonne} = \text{Rate per tonne-km} \times \text{Average lead distance} \]

Example: freight per tonne rose from ₹1,800 to ₹2,050. Rate per tonne-km rose from ₹4.50 to ₹4.70 (+4.4%), average distance from 400 km to 436 km (+9%) because a southern plant shutdown forced supply from the north. Most of the increase is a sourcing (network) issue, not a transporter rate issue — which changes who owns the action.

OTIF

OTIF (concept from t2) measures orders delivered on time and in full ÷ total orders. Measure at order-line level where possible and agree the "on time" window (customer requested date ± 1 day) and the "in full" tolerance. Finance partners link OTIF to revenue: lost sales from short supply, penalty clauses with modern trade and e-commerce (fill-rate penalties of 1–3% of invoice value are common), and expedited freight to recover misses.

Cost-to-serve

Cost-to-serve is the sum of costs from order receipt to cash collection that vary by customer or channel: order handling, warehousing and picking, freight, returns, merchandising, trade terms and credit cost. It uses activity-based costing — cost pools allocated by drivers such as number of orders, lines, drops, pallets, returns and credit days. A typical result: modern trade orders cost 2–3x more to serve per ₹ of revenue than distributor orders because of appointment deliveries, small drops and chargebacks.

The service–inventory–cost triangle

The partner must present inventory, OTIF and freight together because they trade off:

  • Lower inventory, same forecast accuracy → lower OTIF or more expedited freight.
  • Higher OTIF with no extra inventory → needs better forecasting or shorter lead times.
  • Fewer depots → lower inventory and fixed cost, higher secondary freight and lead time.

A scenario table per decision (for example closing two depots) showing ΔInventory, ΔFreight, ΔOTIF and ΔEBITDA plus Δcash is the most useful single page for a supply chain head.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Seasonal build-up: pre-season inventory (fans before summer, paints before Diwali) raises DIO deliberately; compare with the planned build curve, not with a flat target.
  • Commodity hedging stock: procurement may buy ahead of a price rise; evaluate with carrying cost versus expected saving, and show it as a separate decision.
  • Consignment and VMI stock may sit physically in your warehouse but not on your balance sheet (control test under Ind AS 2/115); exclude it from DIO.
  • Freight in COGS versus selling expenses: classification differs between companies (inward freight is part of inventory cost under Ind AS 2.11; outward freight is usually a selling/distribution cost), so benchmarking freight % across companies needs restatement.
  • OTIF gaming: changing requested dates after the event, or splitting orders, inflates OTIF; audit a sample.

Key takeaways

  • Break DIO down by stage and SKU class so each part has an owner and a lever.
  • Obsolescence needs an ageing provision matrix, a liquidation plan and a monthly at-risk list.
  • Decompose freight per unit into rate, distance, utilisation and mode before assigning responsibility.
  • Express OTIF in revenue and penalty terms, not only as a percentage.
  • Cost-to-serve via activity drivers reveals which channels and customers erode margin.
  • Present inventory, service and cost as one trade-off, never one metric alone.

Practice Questions

Q1. Inventory days by stage [L1]

A paints company has annual COGS of ₹2,190 crore. Closing inventory: raw material ₹180 crore, WIP ₹30 crore, finished goods ₹240 crore, goods in transit ₹30 crore. Compute DIO in total and by stage, and the cash release if finished goods days fall by 8 days.

Q2. Obsolescence provision and decision [L2]

A pharma distributor holds ₹14 crore of stock with ageing: under 6 months ₹9 crore, 6–12 months ₹3 crore, over 12 months ₹2 crore. Policy provides 0%, 30% and 75% respectively. A liquidator offers to buy all stock over 6 months for 45% of cost. Compute the current provision, the P&L effect of accepting the offer, and draft the recommendation for the supply chain head, including the tax point on the provision.

💡 Hint: Compare carrying value after provision with the offer price, and remember the cash and holding cost angle.

Q3. Freight variance decomposition [L2]

Budget: 50,000 tonnes shipped, average distance 380 km, rate ₹4.40 per tonne-km. Actual: 52,000 tonnes, 410 km, ₹4.55 per tonne-km. Decompose the total freight variance into volume, distance and rate effects and state which function owns each.

Q4. Depot closure trade-off [L3]

Supply chain proposes closing 3 of 11 depots. Estimated effects: fixed depot cost saving ₹4.2 crore a year, finished goods inventory down ₹11 crore, secondary freight up ₹2.6 crore a year, OTIF expected to fall from 94% to 91% for 6 months. Cost of debt is 10%; each OTIF point lost costs about ₹0.4 crore of contribution a year. Build the one-page decision table and state the conditions under which you would support the proposal.

Q5. Interview: inventory versus service [L2]

The CFO wants inventory days cut by 15 days; the sales head says OTIF is already below target. How would you structure the analysis and the meeting so that both get a decision rather than a dispute?
