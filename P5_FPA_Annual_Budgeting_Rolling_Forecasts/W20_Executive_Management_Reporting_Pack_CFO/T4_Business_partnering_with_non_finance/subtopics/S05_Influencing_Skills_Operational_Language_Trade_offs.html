Influencing Skills: Operational Language, Trade-offs, Decisions

Scope: Influencing skills: speaking in operational language, trade-offs and decisions rather than accounting entries

Why it matters

A finance partner has authority over very little: sales heads own the customers, plant heads own the assets, HR owns the people. Finance influences outcomes only by changing how others decide. Most CMA-trained professionals are fluent in accounting language — accruals, provisions, reclassifications, Ind AS adjustments — which is correct but useless to a sales or operations head who has to act on Monday. FP&A hiring managers in India consistently test for this in interviews: "explain EBITDA margin decline to the sales head in two minutes" or "how would you get the plant head to agree to a lower inventory norm?" The skill is translating financial outcomes into the levers the partner controls, framing the choice as a trade-off, and asking for a decision.

Core notes

Speaking in operational language

Translate each finance metric into the partner's unit of work. A translation table is a useful personal tool:

Finance says Sales hears better Supply chain hears better HR hears better
Gross margin fell 120 bps We gave away ₹180 per case in schemes — —
DSO up 9 days ₹22 crore stuck with 40 named dealers — —
DIO up 11 days — 11 more days of cover; 3,400 pallets extra —
Freight cost +₹2.1 crore — 14% of trucks left under 70% full —
Employee cost over budget ₹3 crore — — 46 hires joined two months early; increment 1.2 points above plan
Provision for expected credit loss up 12 accounts over 180 days — —

Rules of thumb: replace percentages of revenue with units the partner counts (cases, trucks, heads, dealers, days); name the specific customers, SKUs, lanes or roles; give the size of the prize in ₹ and in their own targets ("this is 2% of your annual volume target").

Avoiding accounting entries

Accounting mechanics matter only if they change a decision. "The ₹1.4 crore is a reversal of a prior-year scheme accrual" should become "₹1.4 crore of last year's schemes turned out not to be payable, so this month's margin looks 70 bps better than the real run-rate; underlying margin is flat". If the mechanics do matter (for example, an Ind AS 115 principal-versus-agent conclusion changes reported revenue), explain the business consequence first, the rule second.

Framing trade-offs

Operating leaders face real trade-offs; finance should make them explicit rather than pretend one metric wins:

  • Price vs volume: a 3% price increase with an assumed elasticity of −1.2 loses 3.6% volume; net contribution effect depends on contribution margin.
  • Inventory vs service: cutting finished-goods cover by 5 days releases ₹15 crore but may reduce OTIF by 2 points.
  • Hiring speed vs cost: agency hiring costs more per hire but shortens vacancy.

Present each option with the same structure — ₹ EBITDA, ₹ cash, operational KPI impact, risk, owner — and a recommendation. Decision-makers trust finance more when it shows the downside of its own recommendation.

Asking for decisions

Every partnering interaction should end with one of: a decision made, an action with owner and date, or an explicit choice deferred with a date to revisit. Techniques:

  • Pre-wire: share the analysis one-on-one with the key decision-maker before the forum, so there are no surprises and objections surface early.
  • Options, not ultimatums: give two or three options and a recommended one.
  • Use their goals: link the recommendation to the partner's KRA, incentive or stated priority.
  • Data the partner trusts: build from their operational data (sales system, WMS, HRMS), reconciled to the ledger, so the debate is about the decision, not about whose number is right.
  • Credibility through accuracy: one wrong number in front of a business head costs months of credibility.

Influencing frameworks

  • Stakeholder map (power/interest grid): who decides, who influences, who must be informed.
  • Cialdini principles applied: reciprocity (help them with their problem first), consistency (refer to their earlier commitments), social proof (peer plant or region benchmark), authority (CFO or MD sponsorship used sparingly).
  • Pyramid / SCR for structure (see t3): answer first, then reasons, then data.
  • "Yes, and" rather than "no": "yes, we can extend 15 more credit days to this distributor, and here is the ₹ cost and what volume would justify it".

Example: one conversation, two versions

Accounting version: "Your region's gross margin is 31.2% vs budget 33.0% due to higher trade spend accruals and COGS variance."

Operational version: "You sold the volume — 104% of target — but we gave ₹2.6 crore more in schemes than planned, mostly the 'buy 10 get 1' scheme in Pune and Nashik, which drove only 2% extra volume. If we stop it in those two towns from next month, we recover about ₹1.5 crore by March without hurting your volume target. Can we agree to end it on the 1st?"

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • When the accounting matters: covenant definitions, SEBI disclosures or audit adjustments sometimes are the decision; then explain the accounting clearly and briefly.
  • Over-simplification can mislead: rounding away a material one-off or presenting an elasticity as certain. Keep the appendix rigorous.
  • Conflict between partners (sales vs supply chain): finance facilitates with neutral data and escalates to the forum (S&OP) rather than taking sides informally.
  • Independence: business partnering must not erode the control role — finance still challenges forecasts and refuses to bless unrealistic numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Translate every metric into the units and names the partner manages.
  • Explain business consequence first; accounting mechanics only when they change a decision.
  • Present options as trade-offs with EBITDA, cash, KPI and risk effects plus a recommendation.
  • End every interaction with a decision, an owned action, or a dated deferral.
  • Pre-wire decisions and build on data the partner trusts, reconciled to the ledger.

Practice Questions

Q1. Translation exercise [L1]

Rewrite each statement for the named audience: (a) "Inventory provision increased by ₹2.2 crore due to ageing" — for the supply chain head; (b) "Revenue is ₹3.5 crore below budget after Ind AS 115 adjustment for returns" — for the sales head; (c) "Employee benefit expense includes ₹1.1 crore actuarial gratuity cost" — for the HR head.

Q2. Trade-off page [L2]

A sales head wants to raise credit days for 60 distributors from 30 to 45 days to win shelf share. These distributors buy ₹146 crore a year at 24% contribution margin. Sales expects 6% volume uplift. Cost of debt is 10%, and bad-debt risk rises by an estimated 0.3% of sales. Build the option comparison (status quo, full extension, conditional extension) and state your recommendation.

💡 Hint: Quantify incremental receivables, interest and ECL cost against incremental contribution for each option.

Q3. Pre-wiring plan [L2]

You will present a recommendation to discontinue 140 slow-moving SKUs at next week's S&OP. The marketing head launched 40 of them. Write a pre-wiring plan: whom you meet, in what order, what you share, and what objection you expect from each.

Q4. Role-play: margin conversation [L3]

The regional sales head in Bengaluru has delivered 108% of volume but region gross margin is 260 bps below budget. Write a five-minute conversation script in operational language that ends with a specific decision request, and list the data you need to have ready.

Q5. Interview: independence vs partnership [L2]

"How do you stay a trusted partner to the business while still challenging their forecasts?" Prepare a structured answer with one example from audit or finance experience.
