Partnering with Capex Owners: ROI, Payback, Post-Investment Review

Scope: Capex owners: project ROI, payback, post-investment review

Why it matters

Capex is the decision with the longest consequences in the plan: a new line, a warehouse, an ERP or a retail roll-out commits cash for years and fixes the cost base. Project owners — plant heads, IT heads, expansion teams — are enthusiastic sponsors; they think in capacity, technology and timelines. The FP&A partner provides the financial discipline at three points: before approval (is the return adequate and the case realistic?), during execution (is it on budget and on time?) and after commissioning (did it deliver what was promised?). For listed Indian companies, capex guidance given to analysts and capex funded from IPO/QIP proceeds (monitored under SEBI ICDR and LODR Reg 32 statements of deviation) add external accountability, so the post-investment review is not optional hygiene.

Core notes

The appraisal toolkit

Four measures are used together; each answers a different question.

Measure Formula Answers Weakness
Accounting ROI Incremental EBIT ÷ Investment Does it earn above our ROCE hurdle in a typical year? Ignores time value and timing
Payback Years until cumulative cash inflow = investment How long is cash at risk? Ignores cash after payback and time value
NPV PV of incremental FCF − Investment, at WACC Does it create value? Sensitive to discount rate and terminal assumptions
IRR Rate at which NPV = 0 What return does it earn? Multiple IRRs, scale-blind, reinvestment assumption

Concept c04 covers ROI and NPV/IRR in detail; Week 21 covers capital budgeting mechanics. In partnering, the key is choosing which measure to emphasise for which audience: plant heads understand payback; boards want NPV and IRR against WACC; the MBR tracks ROI because it ties to ROCE on the scorecard.

Building the business case with the owner

Work with the owner on incremental cash flows only:

  • Investment: equipment, civil works, installation, pre-operative costs eligible for capitalisation under Ind AS 16.16–17, plus working capital build-up and capitalised borrowing costs (Ind AS 23) for qualifying assets.
  • Benefits: incremental contribution from volume, cost savings (power, labour, yield, freight), avoided costs (repairs on old asset, rentals), and residual value.
  • Taxes: depreciation under the Income-tax Act block method (WDV rates; additional depreciation under Sec 32(1)(iia) is not available under the Sec 115BAA regime), and the effective rate ~25.17% if 115BAA is opted.
  • Exclusions: sunk costs (feasibility studies already spent), allocated corporate overheads that do not change, financing costs (captured in WACC).

Illustration: a ₹40 crore automated packing line saves ₹9 crore a year in labour and wastage, has a 10-year life, no salvage, and needs ₹2 crore less working capital. Pre-tax payback ≈ \(38 / 9 \approx 4.2\) years (net of the WC release). Straight-line book depreciation is ₹4 crore a year, so incremental EBIT is ₹5 crore and accounting ROI is \(5 / 40 = 12.5\%\) — below a 15% ROCE hurdle even though the payback looks acceptable. That tension is exactly the conversation to have with the owner before the capex committee.

Approval gates and stage tracking

For larger projects, approval is staged: concept (in-principle), detailed project report with ±10% cost estimate, final approval, and supplementary approval for overruns beyond a tolerance (for example 10% or ₹5 crore). The MBR capex tracker (t1/s04) then tracks approved vs committed (POs) vs spent vs capitalised.

Post-investment review (PIR)

A PIR compares realised outcomes with the approved case, typically 12–24 months after commissioning and again at a later milestone for long-gestation projects.

Steps:

  1. Freeze the approved case (cost, timeline, volume, price, savings, ROI, NPV, IRR) at approval — store it in the capex register so it cannot be rewritten later.
  2. Collect actuals: final capitalised cost (including overruns and capitalised interest), commissioning date, ramp-up curve, realised savings or contribution.
  3. Re-estimate the remaining life cash flows with today's best view.
  4. Compute realised and re-forecast NPV/IRR and payback; decompose the gap into cost overrun, delay, volume, price/margin and savings shortfall.
  5. Record lessons: estimating bias (sponsors often overstate volumes and understate ramp-up time), procurement, project management.
  6. Feed back into the hurdle rates, contingency norms and approval policy.
PIR gap analysis (₹ crore NPV) Value
Approved NPV 18.0
Cost overrun (₹6 crore over budget) (5.4)
Six-month commissioning delay (3.1)
Volume ramp-up slower than case (4.2)
Margin per unit better than case 1.5
Re-forecast NPV 6.8

The bridge makes accountability factual: the project team owns cost and delay; sales owns volume; procurement owns margin inputs.

Partnering behaviours

Be involved early (before the owner has emotionally committed to a vendor), help build the case honestly rather than auditing it at the end, provide sensitivity and break-even analysis (what volume makes NPV zero?), and make PIR a learning process rather than a blame exercise — otherwise owners will game the next case.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Strategic or compliance capex (pollution control under CPCB norms, safety, statutory) may have negative NPV; approve on cost-effectiveness among options, not on ROI.
  • Maintenance capex vs growth capex: separate them; maintenance capex is needed to keep existing EBITDA and is part of the FCF baseline.
  • Interdependent projects (a new line that needs a new warehouse) must be appraised as a package.
  • Lease versus buy: under Ind AS 116 a lease creates a right-of-use asset and liability, so "no capex" leases still consume debt capacity; compare on PV of cash costs.
  • Capitalisation judgement: under the 2022 amendment to Ind AS 16, sale proceeds of items produced while bringing the asset to its intended condition, and the cost of producing those items, go to P&L instead of reducing asset cost (costs of testing whether the asset works properly are still capitalised); this changes capitalised cost compared with older cases.

Key takeaways

  • Use ROI, payback, NPV and IRR together; each answers a different question for a different audience.
  • Build the case on incremental, after-tax cash flows, including working capital and excluding sunk costs.
  • Freeze the approved case so the post-investment review measures against what was actually promised.
  • Bridge the PIR gap into cost, delay, volume and margin effects with named owners.
  • Use PIR findings to adjust contingency norms, hurdle rates and estimating bias in future cases.

Practice Questions

Q1. ROI and payback [L1]

A ₹25 crore solar rooftop project saves ₹5.2 crore of power cost a year for 20 years with no salvage value. Book depreciation is straight line. Compute accounting ROI (on initial investment) and simple payback, and comment against a 14% ROCE hurdle.

Q2. Incremental cash flow cleanup [L2]

A plant head's capex proposal for a ₹60 crore line includes: ₹1.5 crore of a consultant's feasibility study already paid; ₹3 crore of allocated corporate overhead per year; interest on the term loan of ₹4.8 crore per year deducted from cash flows; and no working capital. The line will add sales of ₹90 crore a year at 22% contribution margin, with receivables of 45 days and inventory of 30 days of sales. List each correction you would make and explain why.

💡 Hint: Separate sunk, non-incremental and financing items, then add the working capital build.

Q3. PIR bridge [L2]

Approved case: cost ₹120 crore, commissioning April 2024, NPV ₹32 crore. Actual: cost ₹138 crore, commissioning October 2024; volume in year 1 at 70% of case; unit contribution 5% above case. Using the data, explain how you would build the PIR NPV bridge and what information you would request from each function.

Q4. Negative-NPV capex [L3]

An effluent treatment upgrade costing ₹18 crore is required by the state pollution control board within 18 months. Two vendor options are available with different capex and operating costs. Explain how you would appraise and present the decision to the capex committee, given that both options have negative NPV.

Q5. Interview: optimism bias [L2]

Your company's last eight capex PIRs show average volume realisation at 78% of the approved case. How would you change the appraisal process to address this, without discouraging good investments?
