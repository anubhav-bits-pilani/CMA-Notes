Roadmap topic: Business Partnering with Non-Finance Heads: Framing margin, CAC/LTV, working capital, and Capex ROI insights for Sales, Supply Chain, and HR leaders

Overview

Business partnering is the part of FP&A that turns analysis into changed decisions. The MBR pack (t1), the KPI scorecard (t2) and the commentary (t3) describe performance; partnering is how finance works with the people who actually move the numbers — sales heads who set prices and credit terms, supply chain heads who hold inventory and run logistics, HR heads who shape headcount and attrition, and project owners who spend capex. In Indian companies the role often carries titles such as "Commercial Finance Manager", "BU Finance Controller" or "FP&A Business Partner", and interviews test it with scenario questions far more than with technical accounting.

The technical content is a small set of metrics that link operational levers to P&L and cash: price realisation and discount leakage, customer profitability after cost-to-serve, CAC, LTV and payback for acquisition-led businesses, inventory days, obsolescence, freight per unit and OTIF for supply chain, cost per hire, attrition cost and productivity for HR, and ROI, payback, NPV and IRR for capex. Each metric must be computed with a definition the partner accepts, built from operational data they trust, reconciled to the ledger, and expressed in ₹ of EBITDA and ₹ of cash.

The behavioural content matters as much: speaking the partner's operational language, framing choices as trade-offs, ending every interaction with a decision, and institutionalising decisions in joint forums — S&OP, pricing committee and capex committee — so that they flow into the rolling forecast. A recommended sequence is: sales partnering (s01) with CAC/LTV (c01, c02) and customer profitability (c05); supply chain partnering (s02) with working capital release (c03); HR partnering (s03); capex owners (s04) with ROI/NPV/IRR (c04); then the influencing skills (s05) and forums (s06) that make the analysis land.

For Project 6, this topic supplies the "so what" layer: every variance page in the MBR should carry one partnering action — a price, discount, inventory, hiring or capex decision with an owner — and the margin-recovery actions in the Saturday assessment should be framed exactly this way.

Related concepts to study

Week 21 capital budgeting

→ Week 21: capital budgeting (NPV, IRR, payback)

This topic uses NPV, IRR and payback as partnering tools; Week 21 builds the full mechanics: incremental cash flow construction, tax shields on depreciation under the Income-tax Act block method, working capital recovery, terminal values, discount rate selection (WACC vs project-specific risk), capital rationing with profitability index, sensitivity, scenario and break-even analysis, and MIRR. The capex owner conversations in s04 and the capex committee in s06 depend on a standard appraisal template from Week 21, so that every project is evaluated with the same WACC, tax and inflation conventions and the post-investment review can compare like with like.

Phase 2 working capital and profitability ratios

→ Phase 2: working capital and profitability ratios

Phase 2 covered the ratio toolkit: DSO, DIO, DPO, CCC, gross, EBITDA and net margins, ROCE, ROE and DuPont decomposition. Partnering takes those ratios from the analyst's spreadsheet to the operational owner. The key upgrade is decomposition: DSO by region and salesperson, DIO by stage and SKU class, margin by customer and channel, and ROCE split into margin × capital turnover so a plant head sees how inventory and capex affect returns. Reuse the Phase 2 definitions in the KPI sheet so partner numbers match the MBR and the published financial statements.

Activity-based costing and customer profitability

Activity-based costing and customer/channel profitability

ABC assigns overheads to cost objects through activities and their drivers rather than through volume or revenue. For partnering, the cost objects are customers, channels, SKUs and routes. The steps — identify activities, pool costs, select drivers, compute rates, charge by consumption — produce cost-to-serve and the whale curve (c05). Time-driven ABC (time per transaction × cost per minute of capacity) is simpler to maintain and exposes unused capacity. In Power BI, ABC can be implemented as a driver-rate table joined to transaction-level sales and logistics data, giving a refreshable customer P&L instead of a one-off study.

Stakeholder management and influencing frameworks

Stakeholder management and influencing frameworks

Useful frameworks include the power/interest grid (who to manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed or monitor), RACI or RAPID for decision rights in forums, Cialdini's influence principles (reciprocity, consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity), and the trusted-advisor equation (credibility + reliability + intimacy, divided by self-orientation). In practice they translate into pre-wiring decisions, mapping each stakeholder's KRAs and incentives, choosing the right messenger, and protecting credibility by never presenting unreconciled numbers. They are the soft-skill backbone of s05 and s06.

Common pitfalls

Finance metrics without operational levers

Presenting finance metrics without linking to the partner's operational levers

The mistake: a sales head is shown "gross margin 31.4% vs 33.0% budget" or a plant head "DIO 74 days vs 65". The partner cannot act on a ratio; they act on discounts, schemes, SKUs, batch sizes, dealers and trucks. People make the mistake because the MBR is built from the GL, which is organised by account, not by lever. The correct treatment is to decompose each metric down to the levers the partner controls (scheme by town, SKU by ageing bucket, lane by truck fill) and express the size of the prize in ₹ and in the partner's own targets. In review, check that every partner page names specific customers, SKUs, roles or projects and ends in an action.

Optimising one metric at the expense of another

Optimising one metric (e.g., inventory) at the expense of another (OTIF/service)

The mistake: setting an inventory reduction target that is met by cutting safety stock, after which OTIF falls, expedited freight rises and lost sales exceed the interest saved. It happens because each function is measured on its own KPI and finance pushes the cash metric most visible to the CFO. The correct treatment is to present inventory, service level and logistics cost together, model each proposal's effect on all three plus EBITDA and cash, and set paired targets (for example DIO and OTIF together). In review, look for KPI pages where one metric improved sharply while a linked metric deteriorated in the same period.

Cash impact of working capital not quantified

Not quantifying the cash impact of working capital improvements

The mistake: reporting "DSO improved by 6 days" without saying it released ₹18 crore and saved ₹1.6 crore of interest. Days are abstract, so they lose against margin initiatives in prioritisation. The cause is habit — ratios are taught as days — and the fact that the cash effect is a balance-sheet number rather than a P&L line. The correct treatment is to apply c03 (ΔDays × base ÷ 365) consistently with the days definition, show one-time release and recurring interest saving, and separate days effects from growth effects. In review, check that every working-capital initiative has a ₹ cash figure that ties to the cash flow forecast and net debt.

Scorekeeper rather than decision partner

Being a "scorekeeper" rather than a decision support partner

The mistake: finance produces accurate reports and explains past variances but does not shape decisions — it arrives at the S&OP to take notes, reviews capex cases only at the end, and comments on schemes after they have run. It persists because close and reporting deadlines consume time, and because challenging a business head is uncomfortable. The correct treatment is to automate reporting to free time, get involved before decisions are made (scheme design, deal desk, capex concept stage), bring options with recommendations, and track how many decisions finance influenced each month. In review, ask whether each pack page contains a forward-looking recommendation and whether forum decision logs show finance-originated actions.

Practice Questions

Q1. Margin recovery for a consumer durables BU [L3]

A consumer durables BU has FY revenue budget of ₹1,200 crore and EBITDA budget of ₹132 crore (11%). After nine months, LE shows revenue ₹1,180 crore and EBITDA ₹112 crore. Analysis shows: (i) realisation 1.6 points below budget due to festive schemes extended in two regions; (ii) freight per unit up 9% because a plant shutdown forced long-distance supply; (iii) sales staff attrition at 52% against a 35% plan, with estimated replacement cost per leaver of ₹1.4 lakh across 900 average staff; (iv) DSO up from 48 to 57 days on credit sales of ₹1,095 crore. Cost of debt is 9%. (a) Quantify each issue in ₹ EBITDA and/or ₹ cash for the full year. (b) Write one partnering action for each of the sales head, supply chain head and HR head, with owner and date. (c) Identify which actions can realistically close the EBITDA gap in Q4 and which only affect next year.

Q2. Subscription business unit economics review [L2]

A Bengaluru SaaS company reports for FY25: S&M spend ₹48 crore, new customers 3,200, ARPU ₹14,000 per month, gross margin 72%, monthly logo churn 1.8%. By channel: inside sales CAC ₹1.1 lakh, field sales CAC ₹3.4 lakh, partners CAC ₹0.9 lakh (partners receive 20% revenue share, reducing gross margin for those customers to 52%). (a) Compute blended CAC, CAC payback, LTV and LTV/CAC. (b) Compute payback and LTV/CAC by channel. (c) Prepare the recommendation to the CEO and the head of sales on budget reallocation, including the risks.

💡 Hint: Remember that the partner channel has a different gross margin when computing its payback and LTV.

Q3. S&OP and capex interaction [L3]

A cement company's S&OP shows demand in the south region will exceed capacity by 0.8 million tonnes a year from FY27. Options: (A) a ₹650 crore grinding unit, after-tax FCF ₹105 crore a year for 15 years, WACC 11.5%; (B) continue rail-transferring clinker and cement from the central plant at an extra freight cost of ₹900 per tonne; (C) buy cement from a third party at ₹350 per tonne above own variable cost. Contribution margin on own cement is ₹1,200 per tonne. (a) Compute the NPV of option A and the annual cost of options B and C for 0.8 million tonnes. (b) Explain how you would present the options at the capex committee and what you would ask the S&OP forum to confirm about demand. (c) Design the post-investment review metrics for option A if approved.

Q4. Customer profitability and pricing committee [L2]

A building-materials company's top modern-trade customer contributes ₹85 crore revenue at 34% gross margin. Cost-to-serve analysis shows freight ₹6.8 crore, listing and display fees ₹3.4 crore (already netted in revenue), merchandising ₹2.1 crore, returns ₹1.5 crore, and credit cost on 75 days at 10%. The customer asks for a further 2% discount. (a) Compute customer profit before and after the requested discount, avoiding double counting. (b) Propose a counter-offer for the pricing committee that trades discount for lower cost-to-serve. (c) Write the three-line note you would send to the national key accounts head before the committee.
