Key concept

Capex ROI and NPV/IRR for Investment Decisions

Concept: Capex ROI = Incremental EBIT ÷ Investment; NPV/IRR for decisions

Statement

\[ \text{Capex ROI} = \frac{\Delta \text{EBIT}}{I} \]

\[ \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1+k)^t} - I_0, \qquad \text{IRR}: \ \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1+\text{IRR})^t} - I_0 = 0 \]

where \(\Delta \text{EBIT}\) is incremental operating profit after depreciation in a representative year, \(I\) the total investment (capex plus working capital), \(\text{FCF}_t\) the incremental after-tax free cash flow in year \(t\), \(k\) the cost of capital (WACC adjusted for project risk), \(I_0\) the initial outlay and \(n\) the project life (including terminal or salvage value in the final year).

Explanation

ROI is an accounting measure that ties directly to ROCE on the KPI scorecard, so business heads and the MBR use it to ask "will this project raise or dilute our return on capital?". It is easy to understand but ignores timing, cash and the time value of money, and varies with the depreciation method and the year chosen. It can also be computed on average investment (\(I/2\) for straight-line depreciation with no salvage), which roughly doubles the ratio — always state the basis.

NPV and IRR are the decision rules. Accept projects with NPV > 0 at the risk-adjusted rate; IRR > hurdle gives the same answer for a single conventional project. When projects are mutually exclusive or of different scale, NPV is preferred: IRR can rank a small high-return project above a large one that creates more value, can produce multiple values when cash flows change sign more than once, and implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR. Modified IRR (MIRR) corrects the reinvestment assumption.

In Excel use =NPV(k, C2:L2) + B2 (NPV assumes the first cash flow is one period away, so the year-0 outlay is added separately) and =IRR(B2:L2); for dated cash flows use =XNPV(k, values, dates) and =XIRR(values, dates).

Illustration

Investment ₹50 crore in a new extrusion line, life 5 years, no salvage. Incremental EBITDA ₹16 crore a year; book depreciation ₹10 crore a year; tax 25.17%; ignore working capital and assume tax depreciation equals book.

  • Incremental EBIT \(= 16 - 10 = 6\) crore; ROI \(= 6/50 = 12\%\) on initial investment.
  • After-tax FCF \(= 16 - 0.2517 \times 6 \approx 14.49\) crore per year.
  • At WACC 12%, annuity factor for 5 years ≈ 3.605, so NPV \(\approx 14.49 \times 3.605 - 50 \approx 2.2\) crore.
  • IRR is a little above 13.5%.

The ROI (12%) looks marginal against a 15% ROCE target, yet NPV is positive at 12% WACC. The capex committee should decide on NPV, while noting the ROCE dilution in early years.

Practice Questions

Q1. ROI basis [L1]

A ₹30 crore project earns incremental EBIT of ₹4.5 crore a year for 6 years with straight-line depreciation and no salvage. Compute ROI on initial and on average investment and explain which you would report in the capex tracker.

Q2. NPV and IRR build [L2]

Initial outlay ₹80 crore; after-tax FCF ₹18 crore in years 1–3 and ₹26 crore in years 4–6; salvage ₹8 crore after tax in year 6; WACC 11%. Lay out the Excel model, the formulas you would use and the checks you would add.

💡 Hint: Watch how Excel's NPV function treats the year-0 cash flow.

Q3. Conflicting rankings [L3]

Project A: outlay ₹10 crore, IRR 28%, NPV ₹6 crore. Project B: outlay ₹60 crore, IRR 17%, NPV ₹19 crore. They are mutually exclusive and WACC is 12%. Explain which you recommend and how you would explain the choice to a plant head who prefers the "28% project".
