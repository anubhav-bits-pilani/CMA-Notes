Subtopic note

Tone and Language of Financial Commentary

Scope: Tone and language: concise, factual, forward-looking, no jargon

Why it matters

Commentary is read by people with limited time and varied financial literacy — a CEO from sales, a plant head, an independent director. Its credibility depends as much on how it is written as on the analysis. Commentary that is long, vague, defensive or full of accounting jargon is skimmed and distrusted; commentary that is concise, factual and forward-looking is acted on. Tone is also political: finance must state uncomfortable facts about operational performance without alienating the business partners whose cooperation it needs.

Core notes

1. Concise

  • Lead with the message. First sentence = conclusion with number. Supporting sentences follow (Pyramid).
  • One idea per sentence; average 15–20 words; maximum three to five bullets per commentary box; a word cap per box (e.g. 120 words).
  • Cut restatements of the table. If the reader can see "revenue ₹660 crore vs ₹690 crore", do not write it out; write what it means.
  • Remove filler: "It may be noted that", "During the month under review", "As can be seen from the above table".
  • Prefer numbers to adjectives: "₹3.0 crore (5%)" not "significant"; "−40 bps" not "some pressure".

Before (71 words): "During the month under review it may be noted that freight and other operating expenses have shown an increasing trend as compared to the budgeted figures, primarily on account of various factors including an increase in fuel prices and other logistics-related costs. The management is closely monitoring the situation and appropriate steps are being taken to contain the costs going forward in the coming months of the financial year."

After (38 words): "Freight ₹3.0 crore (5%) over budget YTD: part-load e-commerce trips (+₹3.4 crore) and North lane rates (+₹2.1 crore), partly offset by lower volume. FY impact ₹5.2 crore. Head of Logistics re-tendering North lanes by 15 December."

2. Factual

  • Every claim is evidenced: numbers tie to the pack; causes are validated (s06).
  • Distinguish fact from estimate and opinion: "Copper LME averaged 9% above budget (fact). We expect prices to ease 3–4% by Q4 based on the forward curve (estimate)."
  • Avoid unverifiable causes: "market conditions", "challenging environment", "various factors". Name the market factor and its size.
  • Balanced: report favourable and adverse items with equal candour; do not bury bad news or overstate good news.
  • Neutral, not defensive: "Purifier volume is ₹11 crore below budget because two SKUs were delisted" — not "despite the team's best efforts, volumes were impacted by unforeseen circumstances".

3. Forward-looking

  • Every material variance gets an outlook: continues / reverses by [month] / one-off / worsening.
  • Link to LE: "included in LE", "not in LE — logged as risk R-04".
  • Emphasise actions and decisions over history: the reader can do nothing about October but can change November to March.
  • Use future-oriented verbs: "will", "expect", "plan", "require decision by".

4. No jargon

Translate accounting and analytical terms into business language for non-finance readers:

Jargon Plain language
"Adverse mix variance in the Fans segment" "We sold more entry-level fans and fewer premium fans than planned"
"Ind AS 115 variable consideration true-up" "We revised our estimate of year-end retailer rebates"
"Under-absorption of fixed overheads" "Plant fixed costs were spread over fewer units because output was lower"
"ECL provision increase per simplified approach" "We set aside more for receivables that may not be collected"
"Negative operating leverage" "Revenue fell faster than costs, so margins dropped"
"Timing difference" "Spend moved from October to November; it does not change the full year"

Keep precise terms where the audience expects them (Audit Committee: "ECL", "Ind AS 116"), but define acronyms once and keep them in the appendix glossary.

5. Consistent terminology and style

  • Same term for the same thing throughout (not "LE" on one page, "outlook" on another, "forecast" on a third).
  • Same sign words: "over/under budget" for costs, "above/below budget" for revenue and profit; or F/A labels.
  • Active voice and named owners: "The Head of Logistics will re-tender…" not "Re-tendering is being considered".
  • Numbers formatted per the pack standard (₹ crore, one decimal).

Tone for difficult messages

When commentary criticises an operational outcome, keep it about the business, not the person: describe the gap, its cause and the agreed action; share the draft with the owner before publication ("no surprises" rule); and escalate disagreements to the CFO rather than softening facts. Credibility is built when finance is consistently accurate, early with bad news and fair in attributing causes.

External vs internal tone

Investor-facing commentary (earnings call, results press release) follows the same clarity rules but is constrained by SEBI LODR disclosure rules and by legal review; it avoids naming individuals and internal targets. Internal MBR commentary should be more direct: named owners, internal targets, explicit gaps.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Uncertainty: express ranges ("FY impact ₹4–6 crore") rather than false precision, and say what will narrow the range.
  • Bad news early: if the LE is likely to miss guidance, say so clearly; hedged language destroys trust when the miss materialises.
  • Cultural norms: hierarchical organisations may prefer softer phrasing for senior owners; preserve the facts and actions, adapt only courtesy.
  • Translation for regional teams: if commentary is cascaded to regional sales teams, keep sentences simple for non-native English readers.
  • Humour, emphasis and capitals: avoid; use bold sparingly for the key number.

Key takeaways

  • Concise: message first, one idea per sentence, word caps, numbers instead of adjectives, no restating the table.
  • Factual: evidenced, balanced, neutral; distinguish fact from estimate.
  • Forward-looking: outlook, LE linkage, actions and decisions for every material item.
  • No jargon: translate accounting terms for non-finance readers; define necessary acronyms.
  • Consistent terminology and active voice with named owners; share drafts with owners before publication.

Practice Questions

Q1. Edit for concision [L1]

Rewrite in under 40 words: "It is pertinent to mention that the employee benefit expenses for the period under review have been higher as compared to the budgeted levels, which is mainly on account of the annual increments which were effective from the month of July, as well as certain other factors."

Q2. Remove jargon [L2]

Rewrite for a Sales head audience: "GM compression of 160 bps is attributable to adverse RM price variance on copper and aluminium, adverse product mix in Fans and under-absorption at Hosur, partly offset by favourable price realisation."

💡 Hint: Replace each technical term with what physically happened in the business, and keep the numbers.

Q3. Defensive to neutral [L2]

The Water Purifiers BU draft reads: "Despite tremendous efforts by the team, sales were impacted by unprecedented and unforeseen challenges in the online marketplace beyond our control." Using the facts in s02 (BIS documents expired, delisting for six weeks), rewrite neutrally with outlook and action.

Q4. Same message, two audiences [L3]

Write the same message about the FY27 EBITDA gap (LE ₹127.1 crore vs guidance ₹150 crore) twice: (a) for the internal MBR executive summary (maximum 60 words); (b) as a draft line for the Q3 earnings call script (maximum 60 words), noting what changes and why.
