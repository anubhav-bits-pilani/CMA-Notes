Roadmap topic: Monthly Business Review (MBR) Pack Structure: Executive Summary, P&L Actual vs Budget vs PY, Segment Performance, Working Capital/Cash Tracker, and Risk/Action Log

Overview

The Monthly Business Review (MBR) pack is the standard document through which FP&A tells the leadership team how the business performed in the month and year to date, what the full year now looks like, and which decisions are needed. It differs from statutory financial statements in purpose (decision support, not compliance), audience (CEO, CXOs, BU heads, and in condensed form the Board and Audit Committee), basis (management definitions such as adjusted EBITDA, constant-currency growth, segment contribution) and timing (working day 5–8 after month-end, not 45–60 days). In Indian listed companies the MBR also feeds the quarterly board pack that approves SEBI LODR Regulation 33 results, so its numbers must reconcile to the Ind AS ledger.

For an FP&A role, the MBR is the most visible output you produce. Interviewers at Indian corporates, GCCs and consulting firms regularly ask candidates to critique a pack or to walk through one they built. What distinguishes a strong pack is not the volume of tables but the structure: an answer-first executive summary, a P&L that shows Actual against Budget, Prior Year (PY) and Latest Estimate (LE) at month, YTD and full-year levels, segment pages aligned with Ind AS 108, a working capital and cash tracker that prevents a profit-only view, a capex tracker, a quantified risks and opportunities log and an action log that carries accountability from month to month, and an appendix holding definitions and methodology so the front pages stay lean.

The learning sequence follows the page order of the pack. Start with the executive summary (s01) and the governing principle behind it, the Minto Pyramid (c04). Move to the P&L and segment pages (s02). Then cash: the working capital tracker (s03) with the cash conversion cycle (c01), free cash flow (c02) and net debt and leverage (c03). Next the capex tracker (s04) and the R&O and action logs (s05). Then step back to governance: how the Board pack differs from the MBR and what SEBI LODR and the Audit Committee require (s06). Finish with the appendix (s07) and the discipline of a standard layout every month (c05).

Throughout this topic the running case is Shakti Home Appliances Ltd (NSE-listed, three segments, FY27 AOP revenue ₹1,200 crore, EBITDA guidance ₹150 crore), reporting October 2026 (Month 7).

Related concepts to study

Power BI MBR dashboards and paginated reports

→ Week 11/12: Power BI MBR dashboards and paginated reports

The MBR pack you design this week on paper is the specification for the Power BI build from Weeks 11–12. Interactive report pages suit exploration (drill from segment to SKU, slicers for month and BU), while paginated reports (Power BI Report Builder, .rdl) suit the fixed-format, print-ready pack that must look identical every month and export cleanly to PDF for the Board portal. A mature setup uses one semantic model with a measure library (Actual, Budget, PY, LE, variances, RAG status) feeding both. Design choices made here — page order, column order, sign convention, units — become report-level standards there, so changes in the pack template should be versioned and reflected in the dataset, not patched visually.

Ratio analysis and working capital metrics

→ Phase 2: ratio analysis and working capital metrics

The cash tracker is applied ratio analysis. DSO, DIO, DPO, CCC, current ratio, interest coverage, ROCE and Net debt/EBITDA were covered in Phase 2 as analytical tools on published financial statements; here they become monthly management KPIs with targets and RAG status. The difference is operational granularity: DSO by channel and customer group, DIO by category and ageing bucket, DPO by vendor class (MSME vendors need watching because of the 45-day limit under Sec 15 of the MSMED Act and the Sec 43B(h) disallowance under the Income-tax Act). Recall the denominator choices (revenue vs COGS, average vs closing balances) because consistency between months matters more than the textbook choice.

Ind AS 108 operating segments as basis for segment pages

Ind AS 108 operating segments as basis for segment pages (→ Phase 1)

Ind AS 108 defines operating segments by the "management approach": components whose results are regularly reviewed by the Chief Operating Decision Maker (CODM) for resource allocation and performance assessment. The MBR is often the very document the CODM reviews, so the segment pages effectively evidence what the operating segments are. If the MBR shows four BUs but the financial statements report two segments, auditors and SEBI reviewers will ask why; aggregation is permitted only when segments have similar economic characteristics and meet the aggregation criteria. Segment pages should therefore use the same segment boundaries, the same segment profit measure and a reconciliation of segment totals to the entity P&L, mirroring the Ind AS 108 reconciliation disclosures.

IBCS reporting standards for consistent charts and tables

IBCS reporting standards for consistent charts and tables

The International Business Communication Standards (IBCS) codify a notation for management reports summarised as SUCCESS: Say (convey a message), Unify (consistent notation), Condense (high information density), Check (visual integrity), Express (right chart type), Simplify (remove clutter), Structure (logical organisation). Practical rules relevant to an MBR: actual shown solid dark, plan outlined, PY light grey, forecast hatched; time on the horizontal axis, structure (segments, accounts) on the vertical axis; variances in green/red by favourability; same scale for comparable charts; titles that state the message. Adopting IBCS-like notation consistently is what makes a 15-page pack readable in 15 minutes.

Common pitfalls

Data-dump packs with 40 pages of tables and no executive summary

The mistake is a pack that reproduces trial-balance-level schedules, cost-centre listings and every segment table without a page that says what matters. It happens because finance teams equate completeness with rigour, because adding pages is easier than deciding what is important, and because every stakeholder asks for "their" table to be included. The correct treatment is a strict hierarchy: one-page executive summary, 8–12 core pages each with a message title and commentary box, and an appendix for everything else. Detect it in review by asking a non-finance reader to state the three key messages after five minutes; if they cannot, or if the first page is a table, the pack fails.

Comparing to budget only, without PY and LE context

A budget-only variance view hides two things: whether the business is actually growing (PY) and whether the year will land (LE). It happens because the AOP is the formal accountability baseline and incentive plans are tied to it. But if the budget was set badly, a "favourable variance" may still mean a decline year on year, and by Month 7 the YTD variance matters less than the full-year outlook. Correct treatment: show Actual, Budget, PY and LE side by side at month, YTD and full-year levels, with variances to both Budget and PY and a separate LE vs AOP vs guidance line. Detect it by checking whether the executive summary states growth vs PY and the full-year landing.

Omitting cash and working capital, focusing only on P&L

Packs that end at EBITDA miss the most common cause of corporate distress: profit that is not converting into cash. It happens because P&L data closes first, balance sheet reconciliations lag and many FP&A teams sit in a P&L-centric planning tool. The correct treatment is a standing cash and working capital page: CCC with DSO/DIO/DPO trend, receivables ageing with overdues, inventory days and slow-moving stock, FCF, net debt and covenant headroom. Detect it by checking whether the pack can answer "how much cash did we generate YTD and why is it different from EBITDA?" If not, the pack is incomplete.

Inconsistent definitions between months or between pages

The classic symptom is EBITDA of ₹61.2 crore on the summary and ₹60.4 crore on the segment page, or DSO computed on closing receivables one month and average the next. It happens when pages are built by different people, extracted at different times, or patched by hand after the close. The correct treatment is a single KPI definitions appendix, a single mapping table and measure library, and an automated tie-out check between every page total and the master P&L. Any definition change is disclosed with a restated comparative. Detect it with a reconciliation tab that compares each page's headline numbers to the master and flags non-zero differences.

Practice Questions

Q1. Build the October MBR skeleton for Shakti [L3]

Shakti Home Appliances Ltd (FY27 AOP revenue ₹1,200 crore, EBITDA ₹150 crore) closes October 2026 with YTD revenue ₹660 crore (budget ₹690 crore, PY ₹629 crore), YTD EBITDA ₹61 crore (budget ₹84 crore, PY ₹70 crore), LE EBITDA ₹127 crore, YTD FCF −₹21 crore (budget +₹41 crore), net debt (incl. leases ₹42 crore) ₹191 crore, CCC 91 days (budget 80). (a) Propose the page list and order for a 14-page MBR pack. (b) Draft the four lines of the headline table on the executive summary. (c) Draft three Pyramid-style key messages. (d) List two decisions you would ask for.

Q2. Reconcile segment pages and Ind AS 108 [L2]

Shakti's MBR shows four BUs: Kitchen, Fans, Lighting and Water Purifiers. The annual report discloses three reportable segments (Fans and Lighting combined). YTD segment contribution in the MBR: Kitchen ₹48 crore, Fans ₹22 crore, Lighting ₹9 crore, Water Purifiers ₹6 crore; unallocated corporate costs ₹24 crore. (a) Prepare the reconciliation of segment contribution to entity EBITDA. (b) Explain the conditions under which combining Fans and Lighting is permitted under Ind AS 108. (c) What would an auditor ask if the CODM reviews four BUs monthly?

💡 Hint: Think about who the CODM is and what they actually review, then test the aggregation criteria.

Q3. Cash vs profit diagnostic [L2]

YTD October: EBITDA ₹61 crore, tax paid ₹9 crore, increase in receivables ₹31 crore, increase in inventory ₹14 crore, increase in payables ₹10 crore, capex spent ₹38 crore, lease principal paid ₹6 crore. Opening net debt (excl. leases) ₹120 crore. (a) Compute FCF on both the "EBITDA-based" and "post-lease" definitions. (b) Compute closing net debt excluding leases assuming no dividends or equity flows. (c) Write the two-sentence cash message for the executive summary.

Q4. Pack critique [L3]

A divisional controller submits a 38-page pack: pages 1–30 are cost-centre tables; the only chart is on page 31; there is no LE; variances are shown as Actual − Budget for all lines (so cost overruns appear positive in green); EBITDA on page 3 is pre-Ind AS 116 but on page 18 post-Ind AS 116; there is no cash page. Write a structured review note (maximum 300 words) listing every defect, its consequence and the fix, in order of severity.

Q5. Governance flow from MBR to Board [L3]

Shakti's Q2 (July–September) MBR was reviewed on 8 October. Q2 results must be approved by the Board and filed under SEBI LODR Regulation 33. (a) Set out the timeline and the documents that flow from the September MBR to the Audit Committee and Board meetings, including the latest permissible filing date. (b) Identify three items that appear in the board pack but not in the MBR, and two that appear in the MBR but should not go to the Board in detail. (c) Explain how you would reconcile adjusted EBITDA in the MBR to the published results.
