Key concept

IBCS Colour Rule: Green and Red Mean Good and Bad

Concept: IBCS notation: green/red for good/bad, not for positive/negative numbers

Statement

Under the International Business Communication Standards (IBCS), variance colouring follows a semantic rule:

\[ \text{Colour} = \begin{cases} \text{green} & \text{if } d \times (A - B) > 0 \text{ (good for the business)} \\ \text{red} & \text{if } d \times (A - B) < 0 \text{ (bad for the business)} \end{cases} \]

where \(A\) is actual, \(B\) is the comparison base (plan, PY, forecast) and \(d\) is the line's direction factor (+1 if higher is better, −1 if lower is better). Colour encodes the evaluation of a variance, never the arithmetic sign of a number. Amount bars and amount values are not coloured red/green at all; they use scenario notation (actual solid dark grey, prior year light grey, plan outlined, forecast hatched).

Explanation

The IBCS "Unify" rule asks that the same meaning always looks the same. Colour is the strongest visual signal on a page, so it is reserved for one meaning: good vs bad. A cost increase is arithmetically positive but bad — red. A loss reduction is arithmetically positive and good — green. A revenue decline is negative and bad — red. If the variance column is already computed as Fav/(Adv) (c01, c02), "green if positive" happens to coincide with "green if good" — but only for that column. Applying positive-green formatting to an Actual column, a cost-change column or a "Increase/(Decrease)" column breaks the rule.

Related IBCS conventions that FP&A packs adopt:

  • Scenario notation: AC (actual) solid dark, PY light grey solid, PL (plan/budget) outlined, FC (forecast) hatched — so a reader knows which scenario a bar represents without reading the legend.
  • Variance charts: absolute variances as bars from a zero line in green/red; relative variances (%) as "pins" (thin lines with a marker).
  • Neutral items: variances that are neither good nor bad (e.g., capex timing, some balance-sheet movements) are shown in grey or the standard dark colour.
  • Accessibility: IBCS colours are usually supplemented by position (above/below the axis) and labels; packs for colour-blind readers can substitute blue/orange while keeping the good/bad semantic.

The rule is implemented in Excel via conditional formatting on the Fav/(Adv) variance (or a formula using Dir), and in Power BI via conditional formatting rules on the Fav/(Adv) measure or a colour measure returning a hex code based on Dir × (Actual − Budget).

Illustration

Line Dir Budget Actual Actual − Budget Fav/(Adv) IBCS colour
Revenue +1 4,200 4,350 +150 150 Green
COGS −1 2,940 3,120 +180 (180) Red
Employee cost −1 410 395 −15 15 Green
D2C EBITDA (loss) +1 −120 −90 +30 30 Green

A "positive = green" rule on the Actual − Budget column would colour COGS and DSO green — the exact error IBCS prohibits.

Practice Questions

Q1. Colour the variances [L1]

Assign IBCS colours (green, red or neutral) to these variances and justify each: Export revenue down ₹30 lakh vs budget; Power cost up ₹12 lakh vs budget; Capex ₹4 crore below budget due to project delay; Attrition 2 percentage points above target; Segment loss ₹50 lakh smaller than budget.

💡 Hint: Decide the direction factor for each line, and consider whether any of them should be neutral rather than good or bad.

Q2. Fix a dashboard [L2]

A Power BI page colours every variance column with "green if value > 0". The page shows columns Actual − Budget for Revenue, COGS, Opex and EBITDA, and a KPI table with DSO and OTIF. Describe exactly which cells are coloured wrongly, and specify the colour measure (in words or DAX) that would implement the IBCS rule.
