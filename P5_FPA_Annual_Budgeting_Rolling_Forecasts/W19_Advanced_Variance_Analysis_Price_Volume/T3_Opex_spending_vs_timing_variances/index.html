Roadmap topic: Opex Spending & Timing Variances: Separating permanent cost overruns from timing shifts (e.g., delayed marketing campaign or deferred hiring)

Overview

Operating expenses — marketing, employee costs, freight, IT, professional fees, overheads — are where most monthly variance commentary is written, and where most of it is wrong. A YTD underspend is reported as a "saving", the CFO raises the EBITDA outlook, and three months later the campaign runs, the vacancies fill and the saving disappears. Or a large overspend triggers a cost-control drive, when in fact an invoice was booked to the wrong cost centre. This topic gives you the discipline to separate permanent variances (real savings or overruns that change the full-year result) from timing variances (spend shifting between months), and to strip out volume-driven variances, one-offs, and pure accounting artefacts before anything is explained.

For a CMA with audit and GL-finalisation experience, much of the toolkit is familiar: flexible budgets (volume vs spending/expenditure variance), accruals and cut-off, GR/IR, prepaid expenses, cost centre accounting. What FP&A adds is the forward-looking test: the full-year LE (Week 18) determines how much of a YTD variance will reverse. The key identities are short — permanent = FY budget − FY LE, timing = YTD variance − permanent — but they are only as good as the phasing of the budget and the quality of the LE, and they must be applied to the right variance (after flexing variable costs for volume, after fixing accounting errors).

The learning sequence: start with budget phasing (s01), because phasing creates or prevents most timing variances. Then learn the classification framework (s02) and the sign convention for cost variances (c01). Work through the typical real-world cases — delayed campaigns, deferred hiring, advance payments, accrual cut-off errors (s03). Apply the LE test (s04, c02), supported by committed-spend evidence (s05). Separate volume-driven and spending variances for variable lines (c03, c04). Clean out reclassification and mapping errors (s06). Finally, apply materiality thresholds to decide what gets commented on (c05). The outputs feed the opex steps of the EBITDA bridge (Topic 4) and the week's checklist item on timing vs permanent classification.

Related concepts to study

Phase 1 accruals, prepaids, cut-off and close quality

→ Phase 1 (W1–4): accruals, prepaids, cut-off and month-end close quality

Phase 1 covered accrual accounting under the Conceptual Framework and Ind AS 1, prepaid expenses, provisions under Ind AS 37, GR/IR and month-end close checklists. Every one of those topics reappears here as a source of false opex variances: a missed accrual is an "underspend", an annual licence expensed on payment is an "overspend", a provision released is a "saving". The FP&A analyst should apply close-quality checks before writing commentary: accrual completeness (GR/IR, open POs past due, recurring vendors), prepaid amortisation schedules, reversal of prior-month accruals, and cut-off of invoices received after close. A fast, clean close (often working day 3–5 in Indian listed companies) is the foundation of credible variance analysis.

Cost centre hierarchies and responsibility accounting

Cost centre hierarchies and responsibility accounting

Responsibility accounting assigns each cost to the manager who controls it, organised through a cost centre hierarchy (company → function → department → cost centre) such as the SAP CO standard hierarchy. Variance analysis follows the hierarchy: cost centre owners explain spending variances they control; volume-driven variances are explained by the volume owner; allocations from shared services are explained by the service centre. Good hierarchy design makes timing/permanent classification meaningful because there is a clear owner for each LE line. Poor design — costs pooled in "corporate", frequent reorganisations without budget restatement, allocations without agreed keys — produces mapping variances (s06) and diluted accountability. The materiality thresholds (c05) are set per level of the hierarchy.

Purchase-to-pay commitments in ERP

Purchase-to-pay commitments reporting in ERP (SAP CO/MM, Oracle)

The purchase-to-pay (P2P) cycle — requisition, purchase order, goods receipt/service entry, invoice verification, payment — creates data FP&A can use before costs reach the GL. In SAP, POs with cost-centre or internal-order account assignment generate CO commitments that can be reported against budget (commitment line items, budget availability control), and GR/IR shows received-not-invoiced items. Oracle's encumbrance accounting reserves budget at requisition and PO stages and supports funds checking. Commitment reports give the evidence for timing classifications (is the H2 catch-up already ordered?) and for accrual completeness. Weak P2P discipline — retrospective POs, services without service entry sheets, blanket POs — degrades both, so FP&A has a stake in procurement controls.

Week 17 ZBB packages as baseline

Week 17 ZBB packages as baseline for discretionary spend tracking

Week 17 built zero-based budgeting decision packages for discretionary spend: each package describes an activity, its cost, its benefit and its priority ranking, and the approved packages make up the discretionary budget. Packages provide a natural baseline for variance tracking: each approved package has a planned timing (which drives phasing, s01), an owner, and a deliverable. A package that has not started is a timing variance if it is still planned, permanent if it has been dropped; a package that has been replaced by an unbudgeted activity is a reallocation. Tracking actuals and commitments at package level (via internal orders or WBS elements) makes timing/permanent classification evidence-based and lets released savings be re-allocated to the next-ranked unfunded package.

Common pitfalls

Celebrating timing underspend

Celebrating YTD underspend that is purely timing and will reverse

The mistake: presenting a YTD opex underspend as savings, raising the EBITDA outlook or releasing funds on the strength of it. It happens because the YTD number is visible and favourable, owners are happy to take credit, and the LE is not consulted. The underspend reverses when the campaign runs or the hires join, and the business misses the revised outlook. Correct treatment: always show FY budget, FY LE, permanent and timing columns; only permanent variances change the full-year view (c02, s04). Detection: compare YTD underspend with FY budget − FY LE; if the LE equals budget, the underspend is all timing; check the rest-of-year run rate against commitments.

Flat-phased budgets

Flat-phased budgets creating artificial monthly variances

The mistake: phasing every line at 1/12 of the annual budget, so seasonal or event-driven costs (festive marketing, annual increments, contract renewals, audit fees) produce large monthly variances that are purely calendar artefacts. It happens because flat phasing is quick and budget owners are not asked for timing. The pack fills with meaningless variances, real issues are hidden (in s01's example, flat phasing showed a September underspend while plan-based phasing revealed an overspend), and reviewers stop trusting monthly numbers. Correct treatment: driver- or event-based phasing for material seasonal lines; accrual-basis phasing; freeze AOP phasing and show changes in the LE. Detection: lines with identical monthly budgets and highly seasonal actuals; large monthly variances that alternate in sign.

Missing accruals as false savings

Missing accruals so underspend is an accounting artefact, not real savings

The mistake: explaining an underspend that exists only because services received were not accrued — events completed, consultants' work done, freight delivered — with invoices arriving after close. It happens under close-time pressure, with weak service entry discipline and when FP&A is separated from accounts. The underspend reverses next month as an apparent overspend, and both months' commentary is wrong. Correct treatment: accrue all services received by period-end (Dr expense, Cr accrued expenses / GR/IR), reverse next month, and test completeness with GR/IR, open POs past delivery date, recurring vendor analysis and post-close invoices (s03, s05). Detection: month-to-month sign reversals on the same line; vendors missing from the month's postings.

Timing not reflected in the LE

Not reflecting timing shifts in the full-year forecast

The mistake: explaining a variance as "timing — will be spent in Q4" in the commentary while the LE still shows rest-of-year spend at the original budget, or vice versa. It happens when commentary and forecast are prepared by different people or at different times, or when owners copy the budget into the LE. Result: the classification and the forecast disagree, FY EBITDA is mis-stated, and the timing never visibly reverses. Correct treatment: the LE must contain the catch-up spend for any variance labelled timing — timing = rest-of-year forecast − rest-of-year budget by identity (s04); reconcile commentary to the LE line by line. Detection: compute timing from the LE and compare with the commentary; flag LEs equal to budget.

Practice Questions

Q1. Opex variance pack for a consumer company [L3]

Sundaram Home Care's opex at 31 December (9+3 LE), ₹ crore:

Line YTD budget YTD actual FY budget FY LE Facts
A&P 9.00 7.60 12.00 11.80 Festive campaign of ₹1.2 crore moved to February; rest of underspend from cheaper digital rates
Employee costs 7.50 7.05 10.00 9.60 Two sales managers (₹2.5 lakh per month each) budgeted from 1 July now joining 1 February
Secondary freight 3.80 4.47 5.10 5.95 Budget ₹4.00 per unit on 95 lakh units YTD; actual 104 lakh units at ₹4.30
Other overheads 5.50 5.95 7.40 7.90 Includes a ₹0.30 crore one-off legal fee; ₹0.15 crore Q3 audit fee accrual missed

(a) For each line compute YTD variance, permanent and timing. (b) Split freight into volume-driven and spending variance and redo its timing/permanent split on the spending component. (c) Correct the missed accrual with a journal entry and restate the overheads analysis. (d) Using a materiality policy of absolute variance > ₹0.25 crore AND > 5% of YTD budget, decide which lines need commentary and draft it.

Q2. Phasing and LE consistency review [L2]

A shared-services function has a flat-phased FY budget of ₹48 crore. At 30 September, YTD actual is ₹20.5 crore, the 6+6 LE is ₹48 crore, and the commentary says "₹3.5 crore underspend is a permanent saving from automation". Open commitments for October–March total ₹14 crore; the H2 run rate based on the last three months is ₹3.6 crore per month. (a) Compute the YTD variance and the permanent and timing components implied by the LE. (b) Identify the inconsistency between the commentary and the LE. (c) Propose a revised LE with justification, and recompute the classification.

💡 Hint: Compare the rest-of-year forecast implied by the LE with commitments and run rate.

Q3. Mapping error and genuine variance [L2]

In the November pack: Marketing shows a ₹65 lakh YTD underspend; IT a ₹58 lakh YTD overspend; total opex is ₹4 lakh adverse. Investigation finds ₹48 lakh of marketing automation software licences (annual, paid in August, fully expensed) booked to IT instead of Marketing; marketing also has ₹20 lakh of event services received in November not yet accrued. (a) Pass the correcting entries, including the prepaid treatment. (b) Restate the YTD variances for both cost centres. (c) Explain how much of the original commentary ("Marketing savings, IT overrun") survives.

Q4. Hiring plan analysis [L3]

A company budgeted 40 new hires in FY: 20 joining 1 May and 20 joining 1 October, at an average monthly CTC of ₹1.5 lakh; increments of 9% on the existing payroll of ₹3.0 crore per month were budgeted from 1 April but implemented from 1 July with arrears for April–June paid in July. By 31 December, 12 hires joined on 1 June, 10 on 1 September and 8 on 1 December, at an average of ₹1.6 lakh; the remaining 10 are expected on 1 March. (a) Compute budget and actual YTD employee cost for April–December. (b) Decompose the YTD variance into vacancy, rate and increment-timing components. (c) Build the FY LE and split the YTD variance into permanent and timing. (d) Write the CFO commentary.
