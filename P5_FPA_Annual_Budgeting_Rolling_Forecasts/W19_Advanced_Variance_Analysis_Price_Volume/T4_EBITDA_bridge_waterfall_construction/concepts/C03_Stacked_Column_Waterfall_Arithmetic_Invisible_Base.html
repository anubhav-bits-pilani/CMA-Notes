Key concept

Stacked-Column Waterfall Arithmetic: Invisible Base and Visible Bar

Concept: Stacked-column method: invisible base = MIN(cumulative before step, cumulative after step); visible bar = ABS(step)

Statement

For bridge step \(k\) with signed value \(\Delta_k\), let the cumulative position before the step be \(S_{k-1}\) and after the step \(S_k = S_{k-1} + \Delta_k\), with \(S_0\) = base EBITDA. Then, provided \(S_{k-1}\) and \(S_k\) are both on the same side of zero:

\[ \text{Base}_k = \min\left(S_{k-1},\, S_k\right), \qquad \text{Visible}_k = \left\lvert \Delta_k \right\rvert \]

The visible bar is assigned to an "Increase" series if \(\Delta_k > 0\) and a "Decrease" series if \(\Delta_k < 0\). Start and end totals are plotted with Base = 0 and Visible = the total. If the step crosses zero (\(S_{k-1} > 0 > S_k\) or the reverse), the rule fails and the bar must be split.

Explanation

A stacked column chart draws the invisible base from the axis to \(\min(S_{k-1}, S_k)\), then stacks the visible bar on top, so the visible segment spans exactly from the lower to the higher of the two cumulative positions — which is what a waterfall bar should do. In Excel:

Base:     =MIN(C5,D5)
Increase: =MAX(B5,0)
Decrease: =MAX(-B5,0)

with step in B5, cumulative before in C5 and cumulative after in D5. Note that MAX(step, 0) and MAX(−step, 0) together equal ABS(step), split by sign for colouring.

Why zero-crossing breaks it. Excel stacks positive values upward from zero and negative values downward from zero, separately. If both positions are negative, Base and Visible should both be negative numbers (Base = MAX of the two positions, i.e. closer to zero, and Visible = −ABS(step)) so the bar hangs below the axis. If the step crosses zero, no single base works. The fix is to split the visible bar into two series of the same colour:

\[ \text{Above}_k = \max\left(S_{k-1}, S_k, 0\right) - \max\left(\min(S_{k-1}, S_k), 0\right) \]

\[ \text{Below}_k = \min\left(\max(S_{k-1}, S_k), 0\right) - \min\left(S_{k-1}, S_k, 0\right) \]

plotted as a positive segment from zero up and a negative segment from zero down (Below entered as a negative value), with the base set to zero for that bar. Excel's native waterfall chart (2016+) performs this automatically, which is a strong reason to prefer it where the business can be loss-making.

Illustration

Budget EBITDA ₹10 crore; steps: Price +4, Volume −18, Cost +6 (₹ crore).

Step Before After Base (naive) Visible Correct rendering
Budget — 10 0 10 (Total) 0 to 10
Price 10 14 10 4 (Increase) 10 to 14
Volume 14 −4 −4 18 (Decrease) 14 down to −4
Cost −4 2 −4 6 (Increase) −4 up to 2
Actual — 2 0 2 (Total) 0 to 2

With the naive rule, the Volume row has Base −4 (drawn from 0 down to −4) and Visible 18 (drawn from 0 up to 18) — the bar appears to span −4 to 18 in two pieces in different places, not 14 to −4. Correct split: Above = 14 − 0 = 14, Below = 0 − (−4) = 4 (plotted as −4), base 0. The Cost row also crosses zero (−4 to 2): Above = 2, Below = 4 (plotted as −4), base 0.

Connections

This is the mechanical core of the stacked-column fallback in s03 and the root of the "negative-crossing bars" pitfall in this topic. Sign conventions (topic 5) must already have been applied: the method takes signed EBITDA effects as inputs. In Power BI and Excel's native waterfall the crossing is handled internally, but the same cumulative logic drives custom visuals and think-cell style templates.
