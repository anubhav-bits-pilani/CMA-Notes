Subtopic note

Standard Step Sequence for a Budget-to-Actual EBITDA Bridge

Scope: Bridge sequencing: Budget EBITDA → Price → Volume → Mix → COGS inflation/efficiency → Opex → FX → Others → Actual EBITDA

Why it matters

Every monthly business review in an Indian FMCG, pharma, auto-component or chemicals company opens with "why is EBITDA different from budget?". The bridge answers this in one picture, but only if the steps follow a sequence that management recognises and that is computed the same way every month. A stable sequence lets the CFO compare April's bridge with May's, lets the sales head own the price/volume/mix bars, the supply-chain head own COGS, and functional heads own opex. Changing the sequence, or the definition behind a bar, from month to month destroys that accountability.

Core notes

The logic of the sequence

The standard order follows the P&L from top to bottom and from commercial levers to cost levers:

Step What it isolates Valuation basis Owner
Budget EBITDA Starting total AOP as approved by the board FP&A
Price Change in net realisation per unit (Actual price − Budget price) × Actual qty, per SKU Sales / revenue management
Volume Change in total units sold (Actual total qty − Budget total qty) × Budget weighted-average unit margin Sales
Mix Shift in proportion of SKUs/channels Σ (Actual mix % − Budget mix %) × Actual total qty × Budget unit margin Sales / marketing
COGS inflation / efficiency Input cost changes and conversion productivity (Budget unit cost − Actual unit cost) × Actual qty, split into inflation and efficiency Procurement / manufacturing
Opex Spending changes in fixed and semi-variable overheads Budget opex − Actual opex (or flexed budget − actual for variable opex) Cost-centre owners
FX Currency effect on export revenue and imported inputs Actual FC amount × rate difference Treasury
Others Small items: other operating income, provisions, one-offs Budget − Actual or Actual − Budget by nature FP&A
Actual EBITDA Ending total Reported MIS EBITDA, tied to GL FP&A

Three principles drive the order.

  1. Price first, at actual volume. Price is a per-unit change applied to what was actually sold. Valuing it at actual quantity puts the whole price × quantity interaction into the price bar, so the volume and mix bars can then be valued purely at budget unit economics. This is what makes the bridge reconcile with no joint residual (concept c02).
  2. Volume and mix at budget unit margin, not budget price. In an EBITDA bridge, selling one extra unit adds its margin, not its revenue. Using revenue volume (budget price) in an EBITDA bridge overstates the volume bar by the budget unit cost and forces a balancing item. Whether you use unit gross margin or unit contribution (after variable selling costs such as freight and trade commission) is a policy choice; once chosen, variable selling costs must be flexed out of the opex step to avoid double counting.
  3. Costs after revenue, at actual volume. COGS inflation and efficiency are computed on actual units so they measure the cost of what was actually produced and sold. Opex comes after COGS because it is largely fixed and is the area where timing vs permanent analysis (topic 3) applies.

COGS inflation vs efficiency

The COGS bar is usually split in two because the conversations are different. Inflation is the external input-price effect — palm oil, crude derivatives, packaging, power and fuel — computed as (Budget input price − Actual input price) × Actual consumption. Efficiency is the internal effect — usage, yield, labour efficiency, conversion cost absorption — computed from the material usage/yield and labour efficiency variances of topic 2. Together they must equal the GM cost variance Σ (Budget unit cost − Actual unit cost) × Actual qty. If a commodity price fell, the inflation bar is positive ("deflation benefit").

Where FX sits

FX is shown separately when export revenue or imported inputs are material. To avoid double counting, the price step must then be computed at constant currency: export price in foreign currency × budget exchange rate. The FX step is Actual FC revenue × (Actual rate − Budget rate) for exports and Actual FC cost × (Budget rate − Actual rate) for imports. Some companies place FX immediately after price (because it is a realisation effect); the location matters less than consistency and the constant-currency definition of the price bar.

What belongs in Others

Others is a genuine, itemised bucket, never a plug. Typical contents: other operating income (scrap sales, export incentives such as RoDTEP), changes in provisions (expected credit loss, slow-moving inventory), inventory revaluation, royalty, and one-offs that are not large enough for their own bar. Items above the materiality threshold for the pack get their own step (see s05).

Illustration

Godavari Foods Ltd, FY25 actual vs budget (₹ crore; revenue: budget ₹3,000 cr, actual ₹3,210 cr):

Step ₹ crore Cumulative
Budget EBITDA 420.0 420.0
Price +45.0 465.0
Volume +60.0 525.0
Mix −28.0 497.0
COGS inflation −95.0 402.0
Manufacturing efficiency +18.0 420.0
Opex +22.0 442.0
FX −9.0 433.0
Others −5.0 428.0
Actual EBITDA 428.0 428.0

The story reads cleanly: commercial levers added ₹77 cr (45 + 60 − 28), input inflation took ₹95 cr, partly offset by ₹18 cr of plant productivity and ₹22 cr of opex savings; the rupee and other small items cost ₹14 cr. EBITDA beat budget by ₹8 cr, but margin fell from 14.0% to 13.3% — a point the margin bridge (concept c04) must explain.

Building it in Excel

Keep a single bridge-engine sheet: rows = SKUs, columns = budget and actual units, price, unit cost, unit margin, then one column per step computed with the formulas above. Totals at the bottom feed a bridge table (Step, Value, SortOrder) that the chart reads. For example, the volume step uses budget weighted-average margin from SUMPRODUCT:

=(SUM(ActQty)-SUM(BudQty))*SUMPRODUCT(BudQty,BudMargin)/SUM(BudQty)

and a check cell =BudEBITDA+SUM(Steps)-ActEBITDA must return zero.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Volume vs opex for variable costs. Freight and trade commission rise with volume. If the volume step uses unit gross margin, the extra freight shows up in opex as an overspend that is not really an overspend. Either value volume at budget unit contribution (net of variable selling cost) or flex those opex lines before computing the opex step — not both.
  • New and discontinued SKUs. A new SKU with no budget price should be shown in volume/mix at its actual (or a proxy budget) margin, or as a separate "New products" bar; a discontinued SKU's lost margin goes to volume/mix. Ignoring them creates a residual.
  • Promotions and trade spend. If trade promotions are netted from revenue (Ind AS 115 variable consideration), they are part of price; if the MIS shows them in A&P, they are opex. The bridge must follow the revenue definition used at both ends.
  • One large one-off. A ₹30 cr VRS charge sitting above EBITDA should not hide in Opex or Others; show it as its own bar or bridge to adjusted EBITDA.
  • Fixed-cost absorption. If inventory is valued at standard absorption cost, production volume differences create under/over-absorption in COGS. Show it inside efficiency or as a separate "absorption" bar, and explain it — it is a timing effect, not productivity.

Key takeaways

  • Fix the sequence (Price → Volume → Mix → COGS → Opex → FX → Others) and the formula behind each step; change neither between months without restating.
  • Price at actual volume; volume and mix at budget unit margin; costs at actual volume — this is what eliminates the joint residual.
  • Split COGS into external inflation and internal efficiency because they have different owners and actions.
  • If FX is a separate step, compute price at constant currency to avoid double counting.
  • "Others" must be itemised and small; one-offs above threshold get their own bar.
  • A check cell (Budget + Σ steps − Actual = 0) is mandatory on the bridge engine.

Practice Questions

Q1. Sequence and owner mapping [L1]

List the standard EBITDA bridge steps from Budget EBITDA to Actual EBITDA, and for each step state the valuation basis and the business owner who would be asked to explain it in a monthly review.

Q2. Volume at margin vs revenue [L2]

A company budgets 50 lakh units at ₹100 each with a unit gross margin of ₹30. Actual sales are 56 lakh units; actual price and cost equal budget. The analyst's EBITDA bridge shows a volume bar of ₹600 lakh and a balancing bar of −₹420 lakh. Explain the error and compute the correct volume bar.

💡 Hint: Ask what one extra unit adds to EBITDA rather than to revenue.

Q3. FX placement without double counting [L2]

An exporter budgets USD 20 million of export sales at ₹83/USD. Actual export sales are USD 21 million realised at ₹85/USD; the USD price per unit was 2% higher than budget. Imported raw material purchases were USD 6 million at an actual rate of ₹85 against a budget rate of ₹83. Explain how you would compute the Price step and the FX step so they do not overlap, and compute the net FX step on EBITDA.

Q4. Freight in volume or opex [L2]

Budget freight is ₹1,200 per tonne on 2,00,000 tonnes. Actual volume is 2,30,000 tonnes and actual freight cost is ₹28.5 crore. The volume step uses unit gross margin. (a) What will the opex bar show for freight if left unflexed? (b) Restate it into a volume-driven element and a spending element, and explain where each belongs in the bridge.

Q5. Defending the sequence [L3]

The sales head argues that Mix should come before Price because "mix is a strategic choice and price is tactical". Write a short memo (150–200 words) to the CFO explaining whether moving Mix ahead of Price changes the bar sizes under your formula set, and what governance you would apply before changing the sequence.
