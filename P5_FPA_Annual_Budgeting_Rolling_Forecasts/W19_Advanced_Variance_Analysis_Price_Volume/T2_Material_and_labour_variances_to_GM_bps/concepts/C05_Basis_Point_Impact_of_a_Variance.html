Key concept

Basis-Point Impact of a Variance

Concept: Basis-point impact = Variance ÷ Actual net revenue × 10,000; 1% margin = 100 bps

Statement

\[ \text{bps impact} = \frac{V}{R_{\text{act}}} \times 10{,}000 \]

where \(V\) is the variance in ₹ (favourable positive, adverse negative) and \(R_{\text{act}}\) is actual net revenue for the same period and scope (segment, channel, SKU group). One basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point:

\[ 1\% = 100 \text{ bps}, \qquad 0.01\% = 1 \text{ bp} \]

Explanation

Why actual revenue. Actual margin % is computed on actual revenue. A cost variance measured at actual quantity changes actual profit but not actual revenue. Its effect on actual margin % is therefore exactly \(V / R_{\text{act}}\). Because every cost line shares one denominator, the bps of separate cost variances add up. Using budget revenue would leave an unexplained residual when actual and budget revenues differ.

Where it is exact and where it is not.

  • Cost variances (material price, usage, labour, overhead spending, opex) are exact, because the denominator does not move.
  • Price and mix variances are not exact, because they change revenue as well as profit. Their bps effect on GM% must be computed by sequential recalculation of GM% (c06, s04). The simple ratio overstates the price effect.
  • Volume at constant mix has a zero effect on GM%, although it has a ₹ effect. At EBITDA level, volume does change margin % through fixed-cost leverage.

Units discipline. Use "bps" for changes in a percentage (margin moved from 34.5% to 30.5% = −400 bps) and "%" for growth rates (revenue +4.7%). Never write "margin down 4%" when you mean 400 bps. A 4% fall in a 34.5% margin is only about 138 bps.

Scope discipline. Variance and revenue must refer to the same scope. A plant's material variance divided by company-wide revenue understates its effect on that segment's margin. State which basis is used.

Illustration

Core two-SKU example: actual net revenue 11,520 (₹ '000), cost variance −270.

\[ \frac{-270}{11{,}520} \times 10{,}000 = -234.4 \text{ bps} \]

By SKU: SKU X −90 gives −78.1 bps and SKU Y −180 gives −156.3 bps. Together they make −234.4 bps, which matches the cost step in the sequential GM% bridge (34.545% → 30.469% overall, with the cost step taking 32.813% to 30.469%).

Materials example: net revenue ₹15 crore, MPV −₹36.6 lakh gives −244 bps, and usage −₹30 lakh gives −200 bps. A CFO can read this as "4.4 points of gross margin lost to palm oil price and plant yield".

Practice Questions

Q1. Conversion and wording [L1]

A company's Q1 net revenue was ₹1,240 crore. It reports packaging price variance −₹14.9 crore, freight-in variance −₹3.7 crore and labour efficiency +₹2.2 crore. Convert each to bps. Write one headline sentence that uses bps and % correctly for a gross margin that moved from 46.2% to 44.8%.

Q2. Denominator mismatch [L2]

A segment's material variance is −₹6.4 crore. Segment net revenue is ₹210 crore and company net revenue is ₹1,050 crore. Compute the bps impact on segment GM and on company GM. Explain which belongs in the segment review and which in the company-level bridge, and why the analyst presentation might show both.

Q3. Price vs cost bps [L2]

Actual net revenue is ₹500 crore, of which a price variance of +₹15 crore is part. Actual GM is ₹180 crore, and a cost variance of −₹10 crore is included. Explain why −₹10 crore ÷ ₹500 crore gives the exact cost bps, while +₹15 crore ÷ ₹500 crore overstates the price contribution to GM%. State what additional data you need to compute the exact price bps.

💡 Hint: Recompute GM% with and without the price change, holding cost constant.
