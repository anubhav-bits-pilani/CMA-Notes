Key concept

Idle Time Variance

Concept: Idle time variance = Idle hours × Standard rate (always adverse)

Statement

\[ \text{Idle time variance} = -\,H_{\text{idle}} \times SR, \qquad H_{\text{idle}} = H_{\text{paid}} - H_{\text{worked}} \]

where \(H_{\text{idle}}\) is the number of hours paid but not worked on production, and \(SR\) is the standard labour rate per hour. Under the favourable-positive convention the variance is always negative (adverse), because idle hours generate cost with no output.

If the standard already includes a normal idle allowance \(H_{\text{normal}}\), only the excess is a variance:

\[ \text{Idle time variance} = -\,(H_{\text{idle}} - H_{\text{normal}}) \times SR \]

Explanation

Idle time is valued at standard rate, because the rate difference on those hours is already in the rate variance (which uses hours paid). It is separated from efficiency because the causes and owners differ. Efficiency is about how fast people work when they are working. Idle time is about whether they could work at all.

Typical causes, with owners:

Cause Owner Controllable by plant?
Machine breakdown Maintenance Partly
Power outage / grid failure Utilities, external Low
Material shortage (late supplier, import delay) Procurement, supply planning No (plant)
Lack of orders / demand shortfall Sales, S&OP No
Strike, lockout, flood Management, external No

Why it can never be favourable: you cannot have fewer than zero idle hours. If the standard includes a normal allowance and actual idle is below it, the "saving" shows up in efficiency (more hours worked for the same pay). Some cost systems report it as a favourable idle variance against the allowance. Under the FP&A presentation, show it within efficiency to avoid the oddity.

Accounting link. Under Ind AS 2, abnormal idle time (from strikes, prolonged shutdowns) and the fixed production overhead not absorbed because of low production are recognised as expense, not inventoried. Normal idle time built into standards is part of conversion cost. CMA cost accounting standards similarly exclude abnormal idle time from product cost.

FP&A use. Idle time is an early indicator: rising idle hours often precede a volume shortfall, because the plant is waiting for material or orders. In the EBITDA bridge it usually falls under "COGS efficiency". Separately, it supports capacity-utilisation commentary and the case for maintenance capex.

Illustration

Hours paid 22,000; hours worked 21,000; standard rate ₹250/hour. A two-day compressor breakdown caused 1,000 idle hours, and the standard has no idle allowance.

\[ \text{Idle time variance} = -1{,}000 \times 250 = -₹2.5 \text{ lakh} \]

On net revenue of ₹4 crore that is −62.5 bps of gross margin, the same size as the efficiency variance for the month. If the standard had included a normal allowance of 400 hours (shift change-overs), the variance would be \(-600 \times 250 = -₹1.5\) lakh, and the commentary would attribute the full ₹1.5 lakh to the breakdown.

Practice Questions

Q1. Idle time with allowance [L1]

A bottling plant pays 58,000 hours in a month at an actual rate of ₹225/hour against a standard of ₹215/hour. Hours booked to production orders total 54,200; the standard includes a normal idle allowance of 4% of hours paid. Compute the idle time variance and the rate variance.

Q2. Accounting and reporting a strike [L2]

A 9-day strike at a tyre plant resulted in 72,000 idle hours paid under a settlement, at a standard rate of ₹310/hour, plus ₹1.4 crore of fixed production overheads that could not be absorbed. Explain the Ind AS 2 treatment of both amounts. Show how you would present them in the management GM bridge and the EBITDA bridge, including whether they should be treated as exceptional items.

💡 Hint: Distinguish what is excluded from inventory cost from what is shown as "exceptional" on the face of the P&L.
