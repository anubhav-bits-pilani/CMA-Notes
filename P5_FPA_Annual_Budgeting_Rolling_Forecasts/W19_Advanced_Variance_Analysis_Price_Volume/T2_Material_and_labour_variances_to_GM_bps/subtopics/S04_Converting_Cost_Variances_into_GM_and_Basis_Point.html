Subtopic note

Converting Cost Variances into GM % and Basis-Point Impact

Scope: Converting cost variances into gross margin % and basis-point impact

Why it matters

A CFO, CEO or Board member reads margins, not rupee variances. "Material adverse ₹2.7 crore" means little without scale. "Gross margin down 234 bps, entirely from input cost, of which palm oil 180 bps" tells them both the size and the lever straight away. Analyst calls of Indian listed FMCG and consumer companies are framed this way ("gross margin contracted 310 bps YoY on account of commodity inflation"). So is the investor presentation, and the internal pack should be consistent with it.

The conversion looks simple: divide by revenue, multiply by 10,000. Two subtleties make it a real skill:

  • The denominator: which revenue, and why actual rather than budget.
  • Decomposing a GM% change, where price and mix also move the denominator.

Core notes

Definitions

  • Net revenue: revenue from operations net of GST, trade discounts, schemes and other variable consideration under Ind AS 115. Excise-type duties included in price are handled per the company's policy. Use the same net revenue that the GM% is computed on.
  • Gross margin (GM): net revenue minus COGS. Schedule III does not define gross margin. The P&L presents cost of materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade and changes in inventories, and many companies also include direct labour, power and other conversion costs in management COGS. State the definition used in the pack and keep it constant across versions.
  • Basis point (bp): 1/100th of a percentage point. 1% = 100 bps.

\[ \text{bps impact of a variance} = \frac{\text{Variance (₹)}}{\text{Actual net revenue (₹)}} \times 10{,}000 \]

Why actual net revenue is the denominator

Actual GM% is computed on actual revenue. A cost variance (standard vs actual cost at actual quantities) changes actual GM but not actual revenue. Dividing it by actual revenue therefore gives exactly the percentage-point effect of that variance on actual GM%. Using budget revenue would leave a residual, because a different base is used for each line.

Core illustration (same data as Topic 1)

Two SKUs, units '000 and ₹ '000:

SKU X Budget SKU X Actual SKU Y Budget SKU Y Actual
Units 100 90 300 360
Price 50 52 20 19
Unit cost 30 31 14 14.5

Budget: revenue 11,000, GM 3,800, GM% 34.545%. Actual: revenue 11,520, GM 3,510, GM% 30.469%. Revenue is up 520 (₹5.2 lakh) and GM is down 290 (₹2.9 lakh). The GM variance splits as price −180, cost −270, volume +475, mix −315.

Cost variance in bps:

\[ \frac{-270}{11{,}520} \times 10{,}000 = -234.4 \text{ bps} \]

By SKU: X \((30 - 31) \times 90 = -90\), which is −78.1 bps. Y \((14 - 14.5) \times 360 = -180\), which is −156.3 bps.

Decomposing the full GM% change (sequential method)

To decompose −407.7 bps into drivers, recompute GM% step by step, changing one driver at a time in a fixed order: volume → mix → price → cost.

Step Revenue GM GM % Δ bps
Budget 11,000 3,800 34.545%
+ Volume (450 units at budget mix, price, cost) 12,375 4,275 34.545% 0.0
+ Mix (actual mix, budget price and cost) 11,700 3,960 33.846% (69.9)
+ Price (actual price, budget cost) 11,520 3,780 32.813% (103.4)
+ Cost (actual cost) = Actual 11,520 3,510 30.469% (234.4)
Total (407.7)

Points to note:

  1. Volume at constant mix has zero margin-% effect. It changes ₹ GM (+475) but not GM%. Executives often expect "volume growth improves margin". That holds only after fixed costs (EBITDA level) or through mix.
  2. The cost step equals the simple bps formula because cost comes last and its denominator is actual revenue.
  3. The price step is not price variance ÷ revenue. −180/11,520 × 10,000 = −156.3 bps, but the true GM% effect is −103.4 bps. A price cut reduces GM and revenue by the same ₹ amount, so the percentage falls less than the naive ratio. In a bps bridge, use the sequential GM% figure for price and mix, and the simple ratio for cost lines placed last.
  4. Order dependence: putting cost before price gives a slightly different split. Fix the order and document it.

Splitting cost bps into material and labour components

When the cost variance itself has components (MPV, usage, labour rate, efficiency), each is a ₹ amount at actual volume and has no denominator effect. Dividing each by actual net revenue gives additive bps that sum to the cost step. Using the soap-noodles plant from s01 with net revenue of ₹15 crore:

Component ₹ lakh bps of net revenue
Material price (36.6) (244)
Material mix 12.0 80
Material yield (42.0) (280)
Total material (66.6) (444)

Excel implementation

bps of revenue        =Variance/ActNetRev*10000
GM% after each step   =StepGM/StepRevenue
Step bps              =(GMpct_this-GMpct_prev)*10000
Check (bps)           =(ActGMpct-BudGMpct)*10000-SUM(StepBps_range)

Use the custom number format #,##0" bps";(#,##0" bps") so that bps cells are clearly distinguishable from ₹ cells. Round only at presentation level so the bps bridge foots exactly.

Presenting to executives

A one-line headline, then a small table:

Gross margin 30.5% vs budget 34.5% (−408 bps). Input cost −234 bps (palm oil and packaging), price −103 bps (SKU Y price cut of ₹1 outweighing SKU X +₹2), mix −70 bps (shift to lower-margin SKU Y). Volume +12.5% units added ₹4.75 lakh gross profit but no margin %.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Variance vs PY: for year-on-year GM% bridges, the denominator is still current-year actual net revenue. The cost step is then "current cost vs PY cost at current volume".
  • Gross vs net revenue denominators: if the business reports "gross sales" internally and net revenue externally, use net revenue for bps, so the bps reconcile to the reported GM% that analysts see.
  • Tiny revenue base: in a launch month or for a small segment, bps can be huge (a ₹1 lakh variance on ₹20 lakh revenue = 500 bps). Pair bps with ₹ and apply a materiality floor.
  • Inventory timing: if COGS includes standard-to-actual variances released from inventory (s05), the bps relate to consumption-period cost, not purchase-period cost. State the basis.
  • Rounding: bps rounded to whole numbers may not add to the total. Show one decimal in the working and round the chart labels with a balancing "rounding" note, never a plug bar.

Key takeaways

  • bps = variance ÷ actual net revenue × 10,000. For cost variances this is exact and additive.
  • Volume at constant mix changes ₹ GM but not GM%. Mix, price and cost move GM%.
  • Price and mix move the denominator, so compute their bps by sequential GM% recalculation in a fixed order.
  • Material and labour sub-components convert to bps independently and sum to the cost step.
  • Always pair ₹ and bps, define GM consistently, and prove the bps bridge foots to Actual GM% − Budget GM%.

Practice Questions

Q1. bps conversion [L1]

For Q3, a personal-care company reports net revenue of ₹642 crore, material price variance −₹9.8 crore, packaging price variance −₹2.1 crore, material usage +₹1.3 crore and labour efficiency −₹0.6 crore. Convert each to bps of net revenue and compute the total cost-driven GM% movement.

Q2. Sequential GM% bridge [L2]

A two-SKU business has the following data (units lakh, ₹ per unit). SKU A budget 20 units at price ₹120, cost ₹70; actual 18 units at ₹125, cost ₹74. SKU B budget 40 units at ₹60, cost ₹45; actual 50 units at ₹58, cost ₹44. Build the sequential GM% bridge (volume → mix → price → cost) in bps and prove that it foots to the total GM% change.

Q3. Why the price bar looks wrong [L2]

A regional sales head points out that the price variance of −₹1.8 crore on net revenue of ₹115.2 crore "should be −156 bps", but your GM% bridge shows −103 bps for price. Explain the difference in plain language and decide which number belongs in a GM% bridge.

💡 Hint: Think about what happens to both the numerator and the denominator of GM% when price changes.

Q4. Executive headline [L3]

Using the data in Q2, draft a three-sentence headline and a five-row bps table for the CFO's monthly pack. Identify which bar(s) the CEO is most likely to challenge and prepare the supporting analysis you would keep in the appendix.

Q5. Denominator choice [L2]

A colleague builds a bps bridge using budget net revenue as the denominator for all cost variances and finds a 22 bps residual. Explain where the residual comes from and how to remove it without introducing a plug.
