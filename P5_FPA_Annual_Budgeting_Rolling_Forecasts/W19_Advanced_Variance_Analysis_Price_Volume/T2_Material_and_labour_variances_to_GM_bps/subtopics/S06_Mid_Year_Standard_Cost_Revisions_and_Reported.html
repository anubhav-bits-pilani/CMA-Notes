Subtopic note

Mid-Year Standard Cost Revisions and Reported Variances

Scope: Standard cost revisions mid-year and their effect on reported variances

Why it matters

Standards are set during AOP preparation, usually in January–February for an April–March year. When commodity markets move sharply (palm oil, crude derivatives and paper prices have all had 30–60% swings within a year in the recent past), the standard becomes stale. Variances then stop telling you anything about operational performance, and inventory at standard stops approximating cost under Ind AS 2.21. Companies respond by revising standards mid-year, often at the start of H2 (October) or quarterly.

A revision changes three things at once:

  1. The split of reported variances (more in "planning/standard revision", less in "price variance vs current standard").
  2. The valuation of inventory on hand, through a one-off revaluation posting.
  3. The comparability of month-on-month and YTD variance trends.

FP&A must keep the bridge to AOP intact, because the Board approved the AOP and incentives are tied to it. At the same time it must give operations a fair "vs current standard" view. This is the CMA planning-vs-operational variance framework applied in an ERP environment.

Core notes

Planning vs operational split

Let \(SP_0\) be the original (AOP) standard price, \(SP_1\) the revised standard and \(AP\) the actual price, with \(AQ\) the actual quantity (consumption basis):

\[ \underbrace{(SP_0 - AP) \times AQ}_{\text{total price variance vs AOP}} = \underbrace{(SP_0 - SP_1) \times AQ}_{\text{planning (standard revision)}} + \underbrace{(SP_1 - AP) \times AQ}_{\text{operational (vs current standard)}} \]

The planning variance is attributed to the change in market conditions, which is outside the control of the plant or procurement team. The operational variance measures performance against the conditions that actually prevailed (as captured by the revised standard).

Worked illustration

Palm oil AOP standard ₹90,000/t. From 1 October the standard is revised to ₹96,000/t. H2 actual price averages ₹97,000/t and H2 consumption is 400 t.

Component Working ₹ lakh
Planning (standard revision) (90,000 − 96,000) × 400 (24.0)
Operational price variance (96,000 − 97,000) × 400 (4.0)
Total price variance vs AOP (90,000 − 97,000) × 400 (28.0)

After the revision, the ERP reports only the −₹4 lakh as PPV/MPV, because it measures against the current standard. The −₹24 lakh no longer appears as a variance. It is now embedded in standard COGS, which has risen from ₹90,000 to ₹96,000 per tonne. A management P&L that compares "standard COGS + variances" with the AOP will therefore show:

  • Standard COGS above the AOP standard COGS by ₹24 lakh, at actual volume.
  • Price variance of only −₹4 lakh.

Unless FP&A adds back a "standard revision" line, the bridge from AOP GM to actual GM will show a new unexplained ₹24 lakh in "COGS at standard". That is exactly the kind of hidden plug the week's cross-cutting rule forbids.

Inventory revaluation on the revision date

When the new standard is released (in SAP via the cost-estimate mark/release cycle), stock on hand is revalued from the old to the new standard. Suppose 200 t is on hand on 1 October:

\[ \text{Revaluation} = 200 \times (96{,}000 - 90{,}000) = ₹12 \text{ lakh (increase in inventory)} \]

Account Dr ₹ lakh Cr ₹ lakh
Inventory – raw materials 12.0
Inventory revaluation / price difference (P&L) 12.0

This is a one-off credit in October's P&L. Its economic meaning depends on what was done before:

  • If the 200 t was bought at around ₹96,000 and the adverse PPV of ₹12 lakh was expensed at receipt in earlier months, the revaluation simply reverses that earlier expense. Over the two periods the total is right, but September is understated and October overstated.
  • If the adverse PPV on that stock was already capitalised into inventory (s05), the revaluation double-counts. The capitalised variance must be reversed against the revaluation, or inventory will exceed actual cost.

In management reporting, show the revaluation as a separate one-off line ("standard revision – stock revaluation") outside the operating price variance, and exclude it from run-rate GM.

Bridge presentation after a revision

For a GM bridge vs AOP in H2:

Bridge step ₹ lakh Comment
AOP GM x
Price / volume / mix (revenue) ...
Commodity – standard revision (planning) (24.0) Market-driven; approved in October standard update
Commodity – operational price variance (4.0) Procurement vs current standard
Usage / yield / labour ...
One-off: stock revaluation 12.0 Non-recurring; offsets PPV expensed earlier
Actual GM y

For the YTD view, H1 variances were measured against the old standard and H2 against the new standard. Present YTD price variance vs AOP as H1 MPV plus H2 (planning + operational) so that the YTD view still reconciles to AOP.

Effects on reported trends and the LE

  • Month-on-month: September shows a large adverse PPV and October a small one. This is not a performance improvement, just a change in the yardstick.
  • Latest estimate: the LE should use the revised standards for future months. It should still show the AOP-to-LE bridge with a "standard revision" step, so the Board sees the commodity impact.
  • Pricing and product profitability: SKU-level margin reports at standard will drop after the revision. Sales teams will see some SKUs falling below hurdle margin, which is precisely the signal needed for the pass-through discussion (s03).

Governance

A good policy specifies: the triggers for revision (e.g., a commodity index more than ±7.5% from standard for two consecutive months), the frequency (quarterly or semi-annual), approval by the CFO, the handling of inventory revaluation, and the communication to plant and procurement scorecards (targets restated or not). Incentive scorecards usually remain vs AOP for commercial teams and move to current standard for operations.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Partial revisions: revising only the purchased-material standard while leaving conversion costs unchanged changes the FG standard cost. Revalue WIP and FG as well, not just raw materials.
  • Retrospective vs prospective: revisions are prospective. Restating earlier months' variances in the ERP is generally not done. Do any restatement analytically in the FP&A model.
  • Revision downwards: a falling commodity market creates a revaluation loss on stock and favourable planning variances. The stock write-down may coincide with an NRV test, so do not double-count.
  • Mid-month revision: split the month's consumption at the cut-over date. Otherwise apply the convention used by the ERP (often from the first day of the period).
  • Budget re-baselining: some companies re-baseline the whole AOP to new standards. Keep the original AOP as a frozen version (Week 18 version structures) so the Board bridge remains possible.

Key takeaways

  • A standard revision splits the AOP price variance into planning (standard change) and operational (vs current standard) components.
  • After revision, the ERP shows only the operational variance. FP&A must add a "standard revision" bridge step to avoid a hidden plug.
  • Stock revaluation on the revision date is a one-off P&L item. Check whether it reverses earlier expensed PPV or double-counts capitalised PPV.
  • YTD reporting must combine H1 (old standard) and H2 (new standard) variances to reconcile to AOP.
  • Policy should define triggers, approvals, inventory treatment and scorecard impact.

Practice Questions

Q1. Planning and operational split [L1]

A packaging laminate had an AOP standard of ₹245/kg. From 1 January the standard is revised to ₹262/kg. Q4 consumption is 3,20,000 kg at an actual price of ₹258/kg. Compute the planning, operational and total price variances vs AOP.

Q2. Revaluation entry and meaning [L2]

Continuing Q1, 85,000 kg of the laminate is on hand on 1 January. It was purchased in December at ₹260/kg against the old standard of ₹245/kg, and the December PPV was expensed at receipt. Compute the revaluation, pass the journal entry, and explain its effect on December and January management GM. Recommend how it should be shown in the January bridge.

Q3. Bridge with hidden plug [L2]

An analyst's H2 GM bridge vs AOP shows: price +₹1.2 crore, volume +₹0.8 crore, mix −₹0.3 crore, material price variance −₹0.4 crore, usage −₹0.2 crore and a "COGS at standard – other" bar of −₹2.1 crore, with actual GM ₹1.0 crore below AOP. Standards were revised on 1 October. Explain what the "other" bar most likely contains and how to rebuild the bridge.

💡 Hint: Compare AOP standard cost per unit with the current standard cost per unit at actual volume.

Q4. Policy design [L3]

Draft a one-page standard-revision policy for a listed FMCG company with 40 key raw and packaging materials. It should cover triggers, frequency, approval, inventory revaluation, interaction with Ind AS 2 variance capitalisation, and how plant, procurement and sales scorecards are measured after a revision.

Q5. Interview question [L3]

"If we revise standards every quarter, aren't we just hiding procurement inefficiency?" Prepare a structured response for a CFO interview that explains when frequent revision helps, when it hurts, and what reporting safeguards keep performance visible.
