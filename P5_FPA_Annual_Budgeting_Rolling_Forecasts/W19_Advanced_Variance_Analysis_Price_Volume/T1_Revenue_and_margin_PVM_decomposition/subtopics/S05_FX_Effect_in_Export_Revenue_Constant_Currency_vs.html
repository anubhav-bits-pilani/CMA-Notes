FX Effect in Export Revenue: Constant-Currency vs Reported

Scope: FX effect separation for export revenue (constant-currency vs reported)

Why it matters

Indian IT services, pharma, specialty chemicals, auto components and textiles earn a large share of revenue in USD, EUR or GBP. Their reported rupee revenue moves with the exchange rate even if not a single extra unit is sold and no price is renegotiated. If FX is left inside the price variance, a 2% rupee depreciation looks like a 2% price increase, and the commercial team gets credit (or blame) for the RBI reference rate. Every listed Indian IT company reports revenue growth in "constant currency" (CC) alongside reported USD and INR growth, precisely to separate operating performance from currency translation. FP&A teams need the same separation in the management bridge, and it is also needed to test whether forward-contract hedges are doing their job.

Core notes

Definitions

  • Reported revenue — actual foreign-currency (FC) revenue translated at the actual rate (for Ind AS 21 purposes, the spot rate on the transaction date or an average rate that approximates it, Ind AS 21.21–22).
  • Constant-currency revenue — actual FC revenue translated at the budget (or comparison-period) rate.
  • FX effect — the difference:

\[ \text{FX effect} = \text{FC revenue}^A \times (r^A - r^B) \]

where \(r\) is ₹ per unit of FC (e.g., ₹ per USD), \(r^A\) the actual average rate and \(r^B\) the budget rate. Positive = rupee depreciated against the budget rate, which increases rupee revenue.

  • Constant-currency growth = (CC revenue − comparison revenue) ÷ comparison revenue, which excludes the FX effect.

Where FX sits in the PVM chain

FX is placed after price and volume/mix, with all commercial effects measured in FC and translated at the budget rate:

\[ \text{Budget INR} \xrightarrow{\text{Vol, Mix, Price at } r^B} \text{CC actual INR} = \text{FC}^A \times r^B \xrightarrow{\text{FX}} \text{Reported INR} = \text{FC}^A \times r^A \]

That is, compute PVM on FC prices and quantities, multiply each component by \(r^B\), then add a single FX bar. Because FX is valued on actual FC revenue, the chain reconciles exactly with no residual.

Worked illustration

An auto-component exporter budgets USD 1.8 million of exports for Q1 at ₹83.00/USD = ₹14.94 crore. Actual exports are USD 2.0 million at an average realised rate of ₹84.50/USD.

Measure Calculation ₹ crore
Budget revenue 1.8 mn × 83.00 14.94
CC actual revenue 2.0 mn × 83.00 16.60
Reported actual revenue 2.0 mn × 84.50 16.90
Commercial variance (PVM at budget rate) 16.60 − 14.94 +1.66
FX effect 2.0 mn × (84.50 − 83.00) +0.30
Total variance +1.96

Reported growth vs budget = 13.1%; CC growth = 11.1%. The 2.0 percentage points difference is currency. The commercial variance of +₹1.66 crore is then split into price, volume and mix in USD, each translated at ₹83.00.

Multi-currency portfolios

With revenue in USD, EUR and GBP, compute FX by currency and sum. Note the subtle currency mix effect: if the share of EUR revenue rises and EUR has a different budget rate trajectory, the "FX effect" computed per currency is still exact, but the CC growth rate depends on which comparison rates are used. IT companies define CC as current-period FC revenue at prior-period (or budget) average rates for each currency; follow the same convention consistently.

Hedging and the FX bar

If the company hedges with forward contracts:

  • Under Ind AS 109 cash-flow hedge accounting, the effective portion of hedge gains/losses is reclassified to revenue (or the hedged line) when the hedged sale occurs, so reported revenue reflects the hedged rate. The FX bar should then be split: spot FX effect (actual spot vs budget rate) and hedge effect (hedged rate vs spot), or presented at the realised (hedged) rate with a note.
  • Without hedge accounting, forward contract gains and losses sit in other income/expense (often "foreign exchange gain/loss" in other income or finance cost), and revenue is at spot. The EBITDA bridge must then decide whether to include the forward gains in an FX bar — many companies include realised hedge gains in "FX" so that the bar shows the economic currency outcome.

Document the treatment; mixing hedge-accounted revenue in actual with spot-rate revenue in budget is a classic source of unexplained residuals.

Costs and natural hedges

FX also hits costs: imported raw materials (crude derivatives, APIs from China, electronic components) priced in USD. An importer-exporter has a natural hedge. The GM-level FX bar should be net — FX on revenue minus FX on imported COGS — computed the same way (actual FC cost × rate difference). Report gross and net so the treasury view (net exposure) and the commercial view (revenue growth) are both visible.

Translation of foreign subsidiaries

For a group with overseas subsidiaries (functional currency not INR), consolidated revenue is translated at average rates under Ind AS 21.39–40. The same CC technique applies to subsidiary P&Ls: re-translate the current period at budget or PY rates. This is translation, not transaction, exposure; the FX bar mechanics are identical.

Excel implementation

CC revenue       = SUMPRODUCT(ActQty,ActPriceFC)*BudRate
Reported revenue = SUMPRODUCT(ActQty,ActPriceFC)*ActRate
FX effect        = SUMPRODUCT(ActQty,ActPriceFC)*(ActRate-BudRate)
Price (₹ at CC)  = SUMPRODUCT(ActPriceFC-BudPriceFC,ActQty)*BudRate

Store FC prices and rates as separate inputs; never store only rupee prices for export SKUs, or FX cannot be separated later.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • INR-invoiced exports (some Middle East and neighbouring-country sales) carry no FX effect in revenue; exclude them from the FX bar.
  • Pricing linked to FX (contracts with exchange-rate escalation clauses) — the price change is an FX pass-through. Show it in FX or label it, but not both.
  • Budget rate choice — budget rate is usually set at the AOP date (e.g., ₹83.00 flat) or as a forward curve by month; monthly budget rates make the FX effect phased.
  • Rate used for actual — monthly average rate vs transaction-date rate; immaterial differences can create small residuals in system extracts. Recompute FX from FC data rather than taking the ERP exchange-difference account.
  • Export incentives (RoDTEP, duty drawback) are other operating revenue under Schedule III, not price; keep them out of the FX and price bars.

Key takeaways

  • FX effect = actual FC revenue × (actual rate − budget rate); it sits after price, volume and mix measured at the budget rate.
  • Constant-currency revenue = actual FC revenue at budget (or PY) rates; CC growth excludes FX.
  • Store FC prices and rates separately; otherwise FX cannot be isolated.
  • Decide and document how hedges are presented (spot vs hedged rate, revenue vs other income).
  • Show FX net of imported-cost exposure at GM level for a complete economic picture.

Practice Questions

Q1. CC vs reported growth [L1]

A pharma exporter's PY revenue was USD 40 million at an average ₹82.20/USD. Current-year revenue is USD 43 million at ₹84.80/USD. Compute reported INR growth, CC growth (at PY rate) and the FX effect in ₹ crore.

Q2. PVM with FX bar [L2]

Budget: 1,000 tonnes of a specialty chemical at USD 3,200/t, budget rate ₹83.50/USD. Actual: 1,150 tonnes at USD 3,050/t, actual average rate ₹85.10/USD. Prepare a bridge from budget ₹ revenue to actual ₹ revenue showing volume, price (both at budget rate) and FX, in ₹ crore, and confirm zero residual.

Q3. Multi-currency [L2]

An IT services company budgets revenue of USD 120 mn at ₹83.00 and EUR 30 mn at ₹90.00. Actual is USD 118 mn at ₹84.20 and EUR 36 mn at ₹92.50. Compute the FX effect by currency, total CC revenue and CC growth vs budget.

💡 Hint: Compute each currency separately at its own budget and actual rates, then sum.

Q4. Hedge accounting presentation [L3]

An exporter hedged 70% of Q2 USD revenue of USD 10 mn at a forward rate of ₹83.80; the budget rate is ₹83.00 and the actual average spot is ₹85.00. Under cash-flow hedge accounting, the effective portion is reclassified into revenue. Set out how you would split the FX bar between spot effect and hedge effect, and explain to the CFO why reported revenue growth is lower than the "spot" FX story suggests.

Q5. Natural hedge [L2]

A company exports USD 25 mn and imports raw materials of USD 15 mn in the year. Budget rate ₹83.00; actual average ₹85.00. Compute the FX effect on revenue, on COGS and on gross margin, and comment on what the net figure means for the treasury hedging policy.
