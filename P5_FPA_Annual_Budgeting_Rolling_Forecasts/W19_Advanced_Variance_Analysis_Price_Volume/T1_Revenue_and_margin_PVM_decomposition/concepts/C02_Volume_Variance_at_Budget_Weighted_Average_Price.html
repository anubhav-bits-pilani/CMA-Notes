Key concept

Volume Variance at Budget Weighted Average Price or Margin

Concept: Volume variance = (Actual total qty − Budget total qty) × Budget weighted average price (or margin) per unit

Statement

\[ \text{Volume variance} = \left(Q^A - Q^B\right) \times \bar{V}^B, \qquad \bar{V}^B = \frac{\sum_i Q_i^B V_i^B}{\sum_i Q_i^B} \]

where \(Q^A = \sum_i Q_i^A\) and \(Q^B = \sum_i Q_i^B\) are total actual and budget quantities in a common unit, and \(V_i^B\) is the budget price \(P_i^B\) (for revenue PVM) or the budget unit margin \(M_i^B = P_i^B - C_i^B\) (for gross margin PVM). \(\bar{V}^B\) is the budget weighted average price or margin per unit.

Explanation

The volume variance isolates the effect of selling more or fewer units in total, holding everything else at budget: budget mix, budget prices, budget costs. Using the weighted average is what holds mix at budget: \(Q^A \times \bar{V}^B\) equals what actual total volume would have earned had it been spread across items in budget proportions.

Why budget, not actual, values?

  • At actual price, volume would include the price change on the extra units — double-counting price, which is already measured on all actual units in c01. This is the first pitfall of the topic.
  • At budget values, each bar measures one thing: volume measures quantity, mix measures composition, price measures realisation.

For gross margin, value at budget average margin per unit, not price — each extra unit at budget mix earns \(\bar{M}^B\), not \(\bar{P}^B\).

Common unit. The total \(Q\) must be additive: tonnes, litres, standard cases. Adding a ₹1 sachet and a 1-litre bottle as "units" makes \(\bar{V}^B\) meaningless.

CMA link. This is the sales quantity variance (also called sales volume variance in some texts when combined with mix): (actual total quantity − budget total quantity) × budgeted average price/margin. In marginal-costing variance reports the margin version is valued at standard contribution per unit.

Absorption costing caution. If \(M^B\) is based on full absorbed cost, a volume shortfall also causes under-absorbed fixed overhead, which appears in the cost bar unless the model is at contribution level (s02).

Illustration

Core example: \(Q^B = 400\), \(Q^A = 450\) ('000 units). Budget revenue 11,000, budget GM 3,800 (₹ '000).

Basis \(\bar{V}^B\) Volume variance
Revenue \(11,000/400 = 27.50\) \(50 \times 27.50 = +1,375\)
Gross margin \(3,800/400 = 9.50\) \(50 \times 9.50 = +475\)
Wrong: at actual avg price \(11,520/450 = 25.60\) \(50 \times 25.60 = +1,280\)

The "wrong" row is not just a different number; once price is also computed at actual quantity, the components no longer sum to +520 and a residual appears.

Excel: =(SUM(ActQty)-SUM(BudQty))*SUMPRODUCT(BudQty,BudPrice)/SUM(BudQty).

Connections

Works with c01 (price at actual quantity) and c03 (mix at budget item values) to produce the zero-residual reconciliation (c04). Revenue (s01) uses budget average price; GM (s02) uses budget average margin. In hierarchical mix (s03) the volume step is unchanged — only mix is split. In opex analysis (Topic 3, c03) the analogous concept is the volume-driven opex variance valued at budget variable rate. In the EBITDA bridge (Topic 4, c02) the volume step uses budget unit contribution. Refreshes the Week 17 flexible budget: the flexed budget at actual volume is \(Q^A \times \bar{V}^B\) when mix is held at budget.

Practice Questions

Q1. Volume at revenue and GM [L1]

Budget: SKU A 8,000 t at ₹1,20,000/t (margin ₹30,000/t), SKU B 12,000 t at ₹70,000/t (margin ₹14,000/t). Actual total volume is 21,500 t. Compute the volume variance on revenue and on GM, in ₹ crore.

Q2. Spotting the double count [L2]

A colleague's bridge shows volume = (Actual qty − Budget qty) × Actual average price, and price = Σ (Actual price − Budget price) × Actual qty. Budget: 50,000 units at ₹400 average; actual 56,000 units at ₹420 average (single SKU). Compute both of the colleague's bars, show the resulting residual against the total revenue variance, and identify the double-counted amount.

💡 Hint: The double-count equals the price change times the change in volume.

Q3. Unit choice [L2]

A beverage company sells 200 ml, 600 ml and 2-litre bottles. Explain, with a numerical illustration of your own design using three SKUs, how measuring volume in bottles instead of litres changes the volume and mix variances while leaving the total unchanged.
