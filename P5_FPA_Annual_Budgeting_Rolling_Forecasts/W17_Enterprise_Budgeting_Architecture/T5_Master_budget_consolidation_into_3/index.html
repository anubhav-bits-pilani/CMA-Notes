Roadmap topic: Master Budget Consolidation: Rolling departmental templates into consolidated Budgeted P&L, Budgeted Balance Sheet, and Budgeted Cash Flow

Overview

The master budget is the point at which dozens of departmental and functional budgets become one coherent financial plan: a budgeted P&L, a budgeted balance sheet and a budgeted cash flow statement that reconcile to each other every month. CMA training covers the functional budget sequence and the cash budget; FP&A practice adds the engineering — standard templates, chart-of-accounts mapping, corkscrews, integration checks, circularity handling and tax provisioning — that lets a ₹1,000 crore company consolidate 30 submissions in days rather than weeks and defend every number to the Board, auditors and lenders.

The topic has two chains and a set of controls. The operating chain (s01) turns sales into production, materials, labour, overheads, COGS and opex using the production and RM purchase identities (c01, c02). The financial chain (s02) adds capex, working capital (s03, c03), debt, tax (s07) and equity to produce the three statements, with cash flowing out of the indirect cash flow bridge (c05) rather than being plugged. The controls are template design and CoA mapping (s04), integration checks (s05, c04), and circularity management (s06, c06), which together make the consolidation repeatable and auditable.

A sensible learning sequence: first master the identities (c01–c03) on a single SKU; then build a one-year three-statement link by hand (s02) and prove the balance check; then move to monthly granularity with days-based working capital and the count-back method; then add tax and the interest loop; finally, design the departmental template that would feed such a model at scale. For FP&A roles, the test of competence is simple: given an opening balance sheet and a set of departmental assumptions, can you produce three statements that balance without a plug, and explain every movement?

Related concepts to study

3-statement linkage, corkscrews and debt schedules (Phase 4)

→ Phase 4 (W13–16): 3-statement linkage, corkscrews and debt schedules

Phase 4 built integrated 3-statement models for valuation and transaction work: revenue and cost drivers, PPE and working capital schedules, debt schedules with revolvers and sweeps, and checks. The master budget is the same architecture applied monthly, with a stronger emphasis on departmental granularity and governance. Reuse Phase 4 disciplines directly: every balance sheet line as a corkscrew, cash derived from the CFS, a single balance check with a master flag, and consistent sign conventions. The differences are horizon (12 months vs 5–10 years), granularity (cost centre × account × month) and ownership (many contributors instead of one modeller).

Power Query appending departmental templates (Week 10)

→ Week 10: Power Query appending departmental templates into a single fact table

When each department submits a standard template with a flat load tab (s04), Power Query can consolidate them automatically: Folder.Files on the submissions folder, filter on file name pattern, extract the Output_Load table from each workbook, append, change types, and merge with the CoA mapping table and cost centre master. The result is a single fact table (Entity, Cost centre, Account, Month, Version, Amount) that feeds the master budget P&L via PivotTables or a Power BI model. Re-submissions simply replace files in the folder and a refresh updates the consolidation — removing copy-paste errors and creating an audit trail.

CoA mapping and management vs statutory P&L

Chart of accounts mapping and management vs statutory P&L reconciliation (→ Phase 1)

Budget lines must map to GL accounts and from there to both the management P&L (how the business is run — e.g. A&P including trade schemes, EBITDA before ESOP cost) and the statutory P&L under Schedule III and Ind AS (trade schemes as revenue deductions under Ind AS 115, lease costs under Ind AS 116 as depreciation and interest). A reconciliation bridge between management EBITDA and statutory operating profit should be part of the budget pack, so the Board-approved numbers can be compared with published results. Phase 1's Ind AS knowledge is what makes this mapping correct.

EPM cube dimensions

EPM cube design: Account, Entity, Period, Scenario, Version, Department dimensions (Oracle EPBCS / Anaplan)

Enterprise performance management tools store budgets in multidimensional cubes. Standard dimensions: Account (CoA hierarchy, including statistical accounts such as headcount), Entity (legal entities and consolidation hierarchy), Period (months, quarters, years), Scenario (Actual, Budget, Forecast), Version (Working, Submitted, Final; or v1/v2 challenge rounds), and Department/Cost centre, often plus Product, Channel, Project. Oracle EPBCS uses Essbase cubes with approval units by entity; Anaplan uses lists and modules. Designing the Excel template with the same dimensions (s04) makes migration to EPM and loading of the "Budget" version straightforward.

FAST-like modelling standards

FAST / FAST-like modelling standards for template consistency

The FAST Standard (Flexible, Appropriate, Structured, Transparent) is a widely used set of modelling rules: separate inputs, calculations and outputs; one consistent timeline across sheets; consistent formulas across rows (no column-specific overrides); short, readable formulas; no hard-coded numbers in calculations; sign conventions stated; circularity avoided where possible; and clear labelling of units. Applying FAST-like rules to departmental templates makes them reviewable by anyone in FP&A, supports Power Query consolidation, and reduces errors when the model is handed over between budget cycles.

Common pitfalls

Cash as the balancing plug

Using cash as a plug to force the balance sheet to balance

The mistake: cash is computed as total equity and liabilities minus non-cash assets, so the balance sheet always balances. It is tempting because it removes the hardest debugging step. But it hides every error — a missing tax payment, a double-counted dividend, a capex creditor omitted — inside the cash number, which is exactly the number treasury and the Board rely on. Correct treatment: compute cash from the cash flow statement (or direct cash budget), let the balance check test the model, and debug any imbalance systematically (s05). Detection: look at the balance sheet cash formula; if it references total liabilities and equity rather than the CFS closing cash, it is a plug. Also, a model whose balance check is "always zero" with no check row is suspect.

Inconsistent mapping and hard-coded totals

Departmental templates with inconsistent account mapping or hard-coded totals

The mistake: departments map the same cost to different GL accounts (e.g. software subscriptions to IT expenses in one, professional fees in another), or type totals rather than summing lines. It happens when templates are not standardised and FP&A accepts whatever arrives. Consequences: budget vs actual comparisons break because actuals post to the "correct" GL, and consolidated totals do not equal the sum of lines. Correct treatment: a central, locked CoA mapping table with data-validated mapping codes; totals calculated, never typed; a check that load-tab totals equal summary totals. Detection: compare budget by GL with prior-year actual by GL — large swings between accounts with flat totals indicate mapping inconsistency; test formula coverage on total rows.

Ignoring the LE opening balance sheet

Ignoring opening balance sheet (from latest estimate of current year) as the budget starting point

The mistake: the budget starts from last year's audited balance sheet, or from no balance sheet at all (P&L-only budgeting). Since the budget is prepared in December–February, the correct opening position is the latest estimate of 31 March of the current year (e.g. 9 months actual + 3 months forecast). Without it, working capital changes, debt repayments, capex creditors and tax payable in April are wrong, and the cash budget starts from the wrong cash. Correct treatment: prepare a full LE balance sheet (balancing), use it as the opening position, and refresh it before final AOP lock. Detection: check whether the opening balance sheet date is the current year-end and whether opening cash agrees with treasury's latest year-end forecast.

Tax, dividends or capex creditors forgotten

Forgetting tax, dividends or capex creditors, leading to cash that does not reconcile

The mistake: the cash budget omits advance tax instalments, the final dividend paid after the AGM, or capex creditor movements (cash capex assumed equal to additions). These are not part of any departmental template, so nobody owns them. Consequences: overstated cash and understated borrowing need, especially in June/September/December/March (advance tax), August–October (dividend) and project completion months (creditors and retention). Correct treatment: central FP&A schedules for tax (s07, Topic 4 c04), dividends (Board calendar, c04) and capex cash (Topic 4 c06), each as a corkscrew linked to the balance sheet. Detection: the cash tie and balance check may still pass if a line is omitted consistently from both P&L and BS — so review the cash flow statement for these lines explicitly and compare with prior-year actual CFS.

Practice Questions

Q1. Quarterly master budget build [L3]

A consumer goods company presents its LE opening balance sheet at 31 March 2026 (₹ crore): cash 12, receivables 48, inventory 60, PPE 210, CWIP 18, trade payables 45, capex creditors 6, term loan 90, tax payable 2, equity share capital 30, other equity 175. FY 2026-27 quarterly assumptions: revenue 220, 240, 320, 260; gross margin 42%; opex 24% of revenue; depreciation 7 per quarter rising to 9 from Q3 when CWIP of 18 plus Q1–Q2 capex of 30 (paid 70% in quarter of spend, 30% next quarter) is capitalised on 1 October; DSO 20 days, DIO 75 days on COGS, DPO 55 days on COGS (use a 91-day quarter); term loan repayment 7.5 per quarter with interest at 9% on opening balance; tax at 25.168% of PBT with advance tax at 15/45/75/100%; final dividend 12 paid in Q2; minimum cash 10 with a revolver at 10% interest on opening balance. Prepare the quarterly budgeted P&L, balance sheet and cash flow statement with a balance check, cash tie and RE roll-forward.

💡 Hint: Build each balance sheet line as a corkscrew first; do not let cash be the balancing figure.

Q2. Consolidating departmental submissions [L2]

Twenty-two departments submit FY 2026-27 budgets. You find: four use an old template version; marketing maps trade schemes to A&P instead of the revenue-deduction mapping; the plant template phases a ₹3 crore June shutdown flat; HR's payroll total differs from the sum of department payroll lines by ₹1.8 crore. Describe your consolidation process (tools, checks, mapping) and the actions for each issue, and explain how the statutory and management P&Ls will differ after correction.

Q3. Model review and imbalance diagnosis [L3]

A monthly master budget has a balance check of +₹0.9 crore in June, +₹0.9 crore in July–August, +₹1.8 crore in September, +₹1.8 crore in October–November, +₹2.7 crore in December and +₹4.1 crore in March. The cash tie holds; interest uses an iterative average-balance calculation with no circuit breaker. List the hypotheses you would test (consider the advance tax calendar and other quarterly flows), the order of testing, and the structural changes you would recommend after fixing the error.

Q4. Tax and dividend capacity [L2]

Budgeted PBT is ₹180 crore with CSR ₹3.2 crore, book depreciation ₹40 crore vs tax depreciation ₹52 crore, and an increase in gratuity provision of ₹2.5 crore with payments of ₹1.0 crore. Under Sec 115BAA, compute current tax, deferred tax, ETR and PAT. The Board's dividend policy is 35% of PAT, subject to net debt/EBITDA after dividend of ≤ 1.5× (budgeted EBITDA ₹260 crore, net debt before dividend ₹350 crore). Determine the maximum dividend and explain the timing of its recognition and cash outflow in the FY 2027-28 budget.
