Subtopic note

The Operating Budget Chain: Sales to COGS to Opex

Scope: Operating budget chain: sales → production → materials purchases → direct labour → overheads → COGS → opex

Why it matters

The master budget is a chain: every downstream schedule consumes an upstream output. Sales volume drives production; production drives materials, labour and overhead; those drive inventory valuation and COGS; and the combination with opex produces the budgeted P&L. If the chain is broken — for example, production is budgeted from plant capacity rather than from sales and inventory policy — the budget will show inventory building or depleting for no reason, COGS will not match volumes, and the balance sheet will not reconcile. The CMA syllabus teaches the chain as a sequence of functional budgets; in practice FP&A builds it as linked schedules in a model with one volume driver flowing end to end, and interviewers test whether a candidate understands the dependencies and the limiting (principal budget) factor.

Core notes

The principal budget factor

The chain starts from the limiting factor — usually sales demand in consumer goods, but it can be plant capacity, a key raw material (e.g. imported palm oil quota), skilled labour or working capital. If capacity is binding, sales are capped at what can be produced and the chain runs partly backwards. Identify the principal budget factor first; it decides where the chain begins.

Step 1: Sales budget

Volume by SKU × territory × channel × month (Topic 2), priced at net realisation after the GTN waterfall. Output: units and net revenue by month. Seasonality must be applied here — every downstream schedule inherits it.

Step 2: Production budget

\[ \text{Production}_t = \text{Sales}_t + \text{Closing FG}_t - \text{Opening FG}_t \]

Closing FG is set by policy, usually as days or weeks of next month's sales (forward cover): \(\text{Closing FG}_t = \text{Sales}_{t+1} \times \text{cover days} \div \text{days}_{t+1}\). Seasonal businesses pre-build stock ahead of peaks; the production budget must also respect capacity (line-hours × OEE × rated speed) and maintenance shutdowns. Where production exceeds capacity in a month, levelling is required: build earlier, use contract manufacturers (loan licensing / third-party manufacturing), or accept lost sales.

Step 3: Materials usage and purchases budget

Usage: \(\text{RM usage}_t = \text{Production}_t \times \text{BOM quantity per unit} \times (1 + \text{wastage \%})\).

Purchases (quantity):

\[ \text{RM purchases}_t = \text{RM usage}_t + \text{Closing RM}_t - \text{Opening RM}_t \]

Value at budgeted purchase price (including landed cost — freight, non-creditable duties; excluding creditable GST). Separate schedules for packing materials, which often have MOQs and longer lead times. Commodity price assumptions (palm oil, crude derivatives for packaging, milk) should be explicit and sensitivity-tested.

Step 4: Direct labour budget

\[ \text{Labour hours}_t = \text{Production}_t \times \text{standard hours per unit} \]

Direct labour cost = hours × rate, or — more realistically in Indian plants with permanent workforce and contract labour — a headcount-based cost (Topic 3 payroll model) for permanent staff plus variable contract labour linked to hours or shifts. Many FMCG plants treat direct labour as largely fixed in the short run; the budget should reflect the actual cost behaviour (see Topic 6).

Step 5: Manufacturing overhead budget

Variable overhead (power, fuel, consumables) on a driver (machine hours, units); fixed overhead (plant salaries, maintenance contracts, insurance, depreciation from the capex schedule). For absorption costing, compute the predetermined overhead absorption rate:

\[ \text{OAR} = \frac{\text{Budgeted production overhead}}{\text{Budgeted activity (normal capacity)}} \]

Under Ind AS 2.13, fixed production overheads are allocated based on normal capacity; unallocated overheads due to low production are expensed in the period, not carried in inventory.

Step 6: Inventory valuation and COGS

\[ \text{COGS}_t = \text{Opening FG value} + \text{Cost of production}_t - \text{Closing FG value} \]

Cost of production = materials consumed + direct labour + absorbed overhead (± WIP change). Use the costing method consistent with statutory accounts (weighted average or FIFO under Ind AS 2; standard costing permitted if it approximates actual). Under-/over-absorbed overhead is a separate P&L line.

Step 7: Opex (selling, distribution and administration)

Freight-out and distributor/C&F agent costs (variable with sales volume or value), marketing (A&P, often ZBB-based — Topic 3), salaries (payroll model), rent, IT, professional fees. Selling and distribution costs are period costs, not inventoriable (Ind AS 2.16).

Excel implementation

Build each step on a separate calculation block with the same monthly timeline. Use named ranges or structured references for policies (FGCoverPct, RMCoverPct, BOM), and a check row: closing FG must not be negative; production ≤ capacity; RM purchases ≥ 0.

=F12 + G12*FGCoverPct - F13

(Row 12 = sales, row 13 = opening FG; production = this month's sales + next month's sales × cover − opening FG.)

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Capacity constraints reverse the chain: the sales budget becomes an output; FP&A must flag lost sales or contract-manufacturing cost.
  • Contract manufacturing / loan licensing: production at third-party sites changes the cost structure (conversion charges instead of labour and overhead) and inventory location.
  • Shelf life: FMCG products with short shelf life limit pre-building; inventory policy must respect expiry.
  • Multi-level BOMs: intermediates (soap noodles, base oil) need their own production budgets before finished goods.
  • Opening inventory must come from the latest estimate of the current year's closing position, not last year's budget.
  • Normal capacity vs actual: budgeting a low-volume year with full overhead absorption into inventory overstates inventory and profit — Ind AS 2.13 requires abnormal unabsorbed overhead to be expensed.
  • Scrap and by-products: budget scrap sale realisations separately and net against cost per the costing policy.

Key takeaways

  • Start from the principal budget factor — usually sales, sometimes capacity or materials.
  • Production = sales + closing FG − opening FG; RM purchases = usage + closing RM − opening RM.
  • Labour and overheads must reflect actual cost behaviour (headcount-based vs hours-based).
  • Ind AS 2 normal-capacity absorption governs inventory valuation; unabsorbed overhead goes to P&L.
  • COGS must reconcile with opening/closing inventory values; selling costs are period costs.
  • Seasonality applied at sales cascades through every schedule and into cash.

Practice Questions

Q1. Production and RM purchase budgets [L1]

Budgeted sales (units): Apr 50,000, May 60,000, Jun 75,000, Jul 70,000. Closing FG = 30% of next month's sales; opening FG in April 14,000. Each unit uses 1.5 kg of RM; closing RM = 25% of next month's production needs; opening RM in April 20,000 kg. Prepare production budgets for April–June and RM purchase budgets for April–May.

Q2. Capacity-constrained chain [L2]

A plant can produce 130,000 cases per month. Sales budget (cases): Aug 110,000, Sep 140,000, Oct 175,000, Nov 120,000. FG policy: 40% of next month's sales; opening FG 1 August 50,000 cases; maximum warehouse capacity for FG 90,000 cases. Prepare a feasible production plan that meets sales if possible, identify any lost sales, and propose two ways to resolve the constraint.

💡 Hint: Work out the cumulative requirement and pre-build within warehouse limits.

Q3. Absorption and Ind AS 2 [L2]

Budgeted fixed production overhead is ₹24 crore for the year at normal capacity of 1.2 crore units. The budget shows production of only 90 lakh units due to a demand slowdown. Compute the OAR, the overhead absorbed into inventory and the amount to be expensed as unabsorbed, and explain the Ind AS 2.13 basis.

Q4. Model-build specification [L3]

Specify the tabs, inputs, calculation blocks and checks for a 12-month operating budget chain for 15 SKUs sharing 3 production lines and 25 raw/packing materials. Explain how you would structure the BOM as a table and use SUMPRODUCT or matrix multiplication to compute material usage.
