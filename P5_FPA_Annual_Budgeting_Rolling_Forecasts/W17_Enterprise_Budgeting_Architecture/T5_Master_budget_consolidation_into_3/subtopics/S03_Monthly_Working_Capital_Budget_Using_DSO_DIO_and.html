Subtopic note

Monthly Working Capital Budget Using DSO, DIO and DPO

Scope: Working capital budget via days assumptions (DIO, DSO, DPO) by month

Why it matters

Working capital is where the P&L budget turns into cash — or fails to. A consumer goods company with ₹1,000 crore revenue and a 30-day cash conversion cycle ties up roughly ₹80 crore; a 10-day deterioration costs another ₹27 crore of funding. Boards set cash conversion targets in the AOP guidance letter (Topic 1), lenders compute drawing power from the same balances, and the budgeted balance sheet cannot be built without them. Days-based assumptions (DSO, DIO, DPO) are the standard way to budget working capital because they scale with activity, are intuitive for business owners and connect directly to the cash conversion cycle KPI. The challenge is to apply them monthly in a seasonal business without creating artificial swings.

Core notes

Definitions and bases

Metric Formula (period of \(n\) days) Correct base
DSO \(\text{Receivables} \div \text{Credit sales (incl. GST)} \times n\) Gross invoiced credit sales — receivables include GST
DIO \(\text{Inventory} \div \text{COGS} \times n\) COGS (or material consumption for RM, production cost for FG)
DPO \(\text{Trade payables} \div \text{Purchases (incl. GST)} \times n\) Credit purchases — not COGS, which includes labour and depreciation
CCC \(\text{DSO} + \text{DIO} - \text{DPO}\) —

Using revenue ex-GST as the DSO base while receivables include GST inflates DSO by the GST rate; using COGS as the DPO base understates DPO when COGS contains non-purchase costs. Either is acceptable only if historical days were computed the same way and the forecast is consistent — but the balance must ultimately match invoiced amounts for drawing power and cash.

Monthly application

Simple monthly approach (single-month base):

\[ \text{AR}_t = \text{Credit sales incl. GST}_t \times \frac{\text{DSO}}{\text{days in month}_t} \]

This works when DSO is under 30 days. When DSO exceeds the month length (e.g. 45 days), the receivable comprises the full current month plus half of the previous month. The count-back method is more accurate:

\[ \text{AR}_t = S_t + S_{t-1} \times \frac{\text{DSO} - d_t}{d_{t-1}} \quad \text{(for } d_t < \text{DSO} \le d_t + d_{t-1}) \]

where \(S\) is gross credit sales and \(d\) days in the month. Count-back avoids the distortion of applying 45 days to a single festive month (which would overstate October receivables if October sales are double September's).

Rolling average base: \(\text{AR}_t = \text{Average monthly sales over last 3 months} \times 12 \div 365 \times \text{DSO}\). Smoother, but lags turning points.

Similarly:

  • Inventory: split RM, packing, WIP and FG. FG is often set as forward cover on next month's sales (production budget, s01); RM as cover on next month's usage. Converting back to DIO is a check, not the driver.
  • Payables: by supplier class (MSME at ≤ 45 days, imports at LC terms, others per contract — Topic 4 c03). Capex creditors and employee-related payables are separate lines, not trade payables.

Excel implementation

With monthly gross sales in row 10, days in month in row 4 and DSO input DSO:

=IF(DSO<=F$4, F10*DSO/F$4, F10 + E10*MIN(1,(DSO-F$4)/E$4))

Count-back for DPO and DIO follows the same pattern. Add a "days check" row back-computing days from the resulting balance to confirm the method.

From balances to cash

\[ \Delta\text{NWC}_t = (\text{AR}_t - \text{AR}_{t-1}) + (\text{Inv}_t - \text{Inv}_{t-1}) - (\text{AP}_t - \text{AP}_{t-1}) \]

An increase in NWC is a use of cash in the indirect CFS. Equivalently, collections and payments are derived from the roll-forwards (Topic 4 c02, c03). Both views must agree.

Seasonality and target-setting

  • Apply days assumptions by month where business reality differs: distributors take longer credit during the festive push (DSO up in October–November); inventory builds ahead of summer for beverages or ahead of winter for skin care.
  • Express AOP targets as year-end and average days; Boards tend to focus on 31 March, which encourages window-dressing (pushing collections, delaying payments around year-end). Budget average days as well.
  • Link days to initiatives: a channel-financing scheme with a bank for distributors can cut DSO from 21 to 7 days for the participating distributors; a VMI arrangement can cut RM days.

Illustration

Gross credit sales (₹ crore): Sep 90, Oct 150, Nov 110. DSO 45 days. Days: Sep 30, Oct 31, Nov 30.

Method AR at 31 Oct AR at 30 Nov
Single-month: \(S_t \times 45 \div d_t\) 150 × 45/31 = 217.7 110 × 45/30 = 165.0
Count-back 150 + 90 × 14/30 = 192.0 110 + 150 × 15/31 = 182.6

The single-month method overstates October receivables by ₹25.7 crore (a false cash squeeze) and understates November by ₹17.6 crore (false release). Count-back follows how invoices actually age.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Channel mix: modern trade (45–60 days), general trade via distributors (7–21 days), e-commerce marketplaces (settlement cycles) and exports (LC/DA terms) have different days — compute DSO by channel, then aggregate.
  • Trade schemes settled by credit note reduce receivables at settlement; an unpaid scheme accrual is a liability or contra-receivable, not a payable to vendors — map consistently.
  • Inventory in transit and at depots/C&F agents is still the company's inventory; include it in DIO and in drawing-power eligibility only per sanction terms.
  • Bill discounting / factoring: with-recourse factoring keeps receivables on the balance sheet (Ind AS 109 derecognition not met); without-recourse may derecognise. Budget DSO effects accordingly and disclose.
  • Supply chain finance extending DPO may be reclassified as debt-like by analysts and rating agencies.
  • Days on inventory: RM and FG should use their own bases; a single DIO on total COGS is a crude top-down check.

Key takeaways

  • DSO on gross credit sales, DIO on COGS/usage, DPO on gross purchases; be consistent with historical computation.
  • For days longer than a month, use count-back or rolling bases, not a single month's activity.
  • Set days by channel/supplier class and by month where seasonality changes terms.
  • Changes in balances must equal the difference between accrual and cash (collections, payments).
  • Budget average as well as year-end days to discourage window-dressing.

Practice Questions

Q1. Count-back DSO [L1]

Gross credit sales (₹ crore): Jan 60, Feb 70, Mar 95. DSO 40 days. Days: Jan 31, Feb 28, Mar 31. Compute receivables at 28 February and 31 March using both the single-month and count-back methods.

Q2. Monthly working capital and cash [L2]

For a beverage company, monthly COGS (₹ crore) Jan–Jun: 40, 45, 70, 95, 110, 85. DIO target: 35 days on next month's COGS (forward cover). Purchases equal 70% of next month's COGS, GST 18%, DPO 50 days count-back. Compute inventory and payables at each month-end from February to May, and the monthly change in net working capital from these two items.

💡 Hint: Decide the day-count basis for "next month's COGS" first and keep it constant.

Q3. Channel-weighted DSO [L2]

Sales mix: general trade 60% (DSO 12 days), modern trade 25% (DSO 52 days), e-commerce 15% (DSO 28 days). The AOP shifts mix to 50/30/20. Compute the blended DSO before and after, and the incremental receivables at ₹1,200 crore annual gross sales.

Q4. Window-dressing debate [L3]

A BU head proposes a year-end DSO target of 25 days (from 32), achieved by offering distributors an extra 1% cash discount for payment by 31 March and delaying supplier payments by 10 days in March. Analyse the P&L, cash, average-days and supplier-risk consequences, and recommend how the AOP should set working capital targets.
