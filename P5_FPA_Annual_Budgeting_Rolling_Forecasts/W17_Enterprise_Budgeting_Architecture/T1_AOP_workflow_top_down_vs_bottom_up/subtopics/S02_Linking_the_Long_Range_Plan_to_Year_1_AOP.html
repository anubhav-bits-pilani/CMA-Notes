Subtopic note

Linking the Long Range Plan to Year-1 AOP Envelopes

Scope: Linking 3–5 year Long Range Plan (LRP) / strategic plan targets to Year-1 AOP envelopes

Why it matters

A Long Range Plan (LRP) or strategic plan sets the company's direction for three to five years: revenue ambition, margin trajectory, capital allocation, portfolio moves and funding. The AOP is supposed to be Year 1 of that journey. Without an explicit link, each year's AOP is negotiated in isolation, the LRP's back-ended "hockey stick" never arrives, and the valuation or investor guidance built on the LRP becomes unachievable. For an FP&A analyst the link appears as envelopes — top-down caps or floors (revenue, EBITDA, capex, headcount, cash) that the bottom-up plan must fit inside or explain against.

Core notes

What the LRP contains

A typical LRP model (built in Week 16) holds, by year and BU: market growth and share, revenue, gross margin, A&P and other key costs, EBITDA, capex (maintenance vs growth), working capital days, free cash flow, funding and dividends, plus a list of strategic initiatives with their expected financial impact and timing. It is annual (not monthly), built at BU or category level (not cost centre), and driven by a few high-level assumptions. Its outputs feed the DCF and the Board's capital allocation decisions.

From LRP Year 1 to AOP envelopes

The translation has four steps.

  1. Refresh the starting point. The LRP was built off last year's actuals; the AOP starts from the current-year LE. If FY 2026-27 LE revenue is ₹2,350 crore against an LRP Year-0 assumption of ₹2,400 crore, applying the LRP growth rate to the lower base gives a lower Year-1 figure than the LRP's absolute number. Management must decide whether the envelope is the LRP absolute (₹2,760 crore — implying catch-up growth of 17.4%) or the LRP growth rate applied to the new base (₹2,350 crore × 1.15 = ₹2,702.5 crore). This decision should be explicit in the target letter.
  2. Split by BU and metric. Allocate the corporate envelope to BUs using each BU's LRP share, adjusted for known changes (a divested brand, a new plant). Metrics usually include revenue (floor), EBITDA or contribution (floor), capex (cap), headcount or employee cost (cap), and working capital days or cash conversion (target).
  3. Embed the initiatives. Year-1 slices of strategic initiatives (new categories, distribution expansion, cost programmes) are listed with their envelope impact so that BUs build them into submissions and FP&A can track them separately in the gap bridge.
  4. Set the "LRP gap" tracker. If the approved AOP lands below LRP Year 1, record the shortfall and its carry-forward effect on Years 2–5. This keeps the Board aware that either the LRP must be revised or later years must catch up.

Option A protects the LRP and investor guidance but may force unrealistic growth; option B accepts the base slippage and resets the trajectory. Many CFOs choose a middle path: the revenue envelope is option B, but EBITDA is held closer to option A by requiring cost initiatives — making the gap explicitly a cost-out challenge.

Keeping LRP and AOP consistent

  • Use the same definitions (EBITDA before/after other income and Ind AS 116 effects; capex including or excluding intangibles and ROU assets).
  • Use the same macro assumptions (inflation, FX, commodity) unless deliberately updated — in which case update the LRP's Year-1 too.
  • Reconcile the AOP back to the LRP in a bridge: LRP Year 1 → base slippage → market changes → initiative timing → AOP. This bridge goes in the Board paper.
  • After approval, the next LRP uses the AOP as Year 1 — the plans "roll" each year.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • LRP no longer credible: if market conditions have changed fundamentally (a new competitor, regulatory change), forcing the AOP into the old LRP envelope produces gaming. Better to revise the LRP explicitly in the September–October refresh.
  • Back-ended LRPs: LRPs where most growth sits in Years 4–5 put little pressure on Year 1. FP&A should test whether Year 1 includes the investments (capex, A&P, hiring) needed to deliver later growth, or the LRP is hollow.
  • Portfolio changes: a planned divestment in Year 1 should be shown in the envelope both with and without the divested BU to avoid comparing unlike perimeters.
  • Value vs Year-1 EBITDA: an initiative that dilutes Year-1 EBITDA but has a positive NPV (Week 21) may be worth protecting even if it breaks the margin envelope; the envelope rules should allow a ring-fenced "strategic investment" line.
  • Listed-company guidance: if management has publicly guided on margin, the AOP envelope will be at least the guidance; the difference between internal stretch and external guidance should be deliberate and understood.

Key takeaways

  • The AOP is Year 1 of the LRP; envelopes are the bridge between them.
  • Decide explicitly whether envelopes follow the LRP absolute or the LRP growth rate applied to the latest estimate.
  • Envelopes cover revenue, EBITDA, capex, headcount and cash conversion — each a floor, cap or target.
  • Track and report the LRP gap when the approved AOP falls below LRP Year 1.
  • Keep definitions and macro assumptions consistent between LRP and AOP, and reconcile them in a bridge.
  • Ring-fence strategic investments so the envelope does not starve long-term value creation.

Practice Questions

Q1. Deriving envelopes when the base has slipped [L2]

The LRP for Deccan Appliances Ltd assumed FY 2026-27 revenue of ₹1,800 crore and 12% CAGR thereafter with EBITDA margin rising by 75 basis points a year from 11.0%. The FY 2026-27 LE is revenue ₹1,710 crore and EBITDA margin 10.4%. Compute the FY 2027-28 revenue and EBITDA envelopes under (a) LRP absolute and (b) LRP growth and margin step applied to the LE base, and the growth and margin improvement each implies.

💡 Hint: Compute the LRP Year-1 absolute first, then measure the implied growth from the LE base.

Q2. Allocating a corporate envelope to BUs [L2]

The corporate revenue envelope is ₹2,700 crore. LRP Year-1 shares are Snacks 54%, Beverages 30%, Exports 16%. Since the LRP was approved, a beverage brand with ₹60 crore annual revenue has been sold (effective 1 April 2027), and Snacks has commissioned a new plant expected to add ₹45 crore. Propose BU envelopes and explain the treatment of each change.

Q3. Board bridge from LRP to AOP [L3]

The approved AOP EBITDA is ₹395 crore against an LRP Year-1 EBITDA of ₹428 crore. Causes: LE base slippage ₹18 crore, palm oil inflation not in LRP ₹22 crore, delay of a rural distribution initiative by six months ₹9 crore, new cost-out programme +₹16 crore. Prepare the bridge for the Board paper and write two sentences on its implication for the LRP Years 2–5.

Q4. Hollow LRP test [L3]

An LRP shows revenue growing 8%, 9%, 14%, 18% and 20% in Years 1–5 while A&P stays at 7% of revenue and capex at 0.9× depreciation throughout. Explain the tests you would run on the Year-1 AOP to assess whether the LRP is achievable, and what envelope you would set for growth investment.
