Key concept

Beyond Budgeting: The 12 Principles and Decoupling

Concept: Beyond Budgeting (Hope & Fraser, BBRT): 12 principles; decouple targets, forecasts and resource allocation

Statement

Beyond Budgeting is a management model, developed by Jeremy Hope and Robin Fraser with the Beyond Budgeting Round Table (BBRT, founded 1998) and set out in Beyond Budgeting (2003), which argues that the traditional annual budget forces one number to serve three incompatible purposes:

\[ \text{Annual budget} = \text{Target (ambition)} = \text{Forecast (best estimate)} = \text{Resource allocation (spending authority)} \]

Its central prescription is to decouple them: set ambitious, often relative targets; prepare frequent, unbiased rolling forecasts; and allocate resources dynamically as needed — supported by twelve principles (six leadership, six management process).

Explanation

When one number serves all three purposes, each corrupts the others: a forecast that will be used as a target is understated (sandbagging); a target that is also a resource cap is padded; a resource allocation fixed a year in advance leads to use-it-or-lose-it. Beyond Budgeting removes the link.

The 12 principles (BBRT current formulation)

Leadership principles:

  1. Purpose — engage and inspire people around bold and noble causes, not short-term financial targets.
  2. Values — govern through shared values and sound judgement, not detailed rules.
  3. Transparency — make information open for self-regulation, innovation, learning and control.
  4. Organisation — cultivate belonging and organise around accountable teams; avoid hierarchical control and bureaucracy.
  5. Autonomy — trust people with freedom to act; do not punish everyone if someone abuses it.
  6. Customers — connect everyone's work with customer needs; avoid conflicts of interest.

Management process principles:

  1. Rhythm — organise management processes dynamically around business rhythms and events, not only the calendar year.
  2. Targets — set directional, ambitious and relative goals; avoid fixed and cascaded targets.
  3. Plans and forecasts — make planning and forecasting lean and unbiased, not rigid and political.
  4. Resource allocation — foster a cost-conscious mindset and make resources available as needed, not through detailed annual budget allocations.
  5. Performance evaluation — evaluate performance holistically, with peer feedback, for learning and development; not on measurement only and not for rewards only.
  6. Rewards — reward shared success against competition, not against fixed performance contracts.

(The original 2003 book grouped them as six "adaptive process" and six "devolved leadership" principles; the BBRT later revised the wording into the list above.)

The best-known implementation is Statoil/Equinor under Bjarte Bogsnes ("Ambition to Action"), along with Handelsbanken (no budgets since the 1970s) and companies such as Borealis. In practice, few Indian companies abandon the AOP entirely, but many adopt elements: rolling forecasts (Week 18), relative KPIs, quarterly capex and discretionary-spend releases, and separating forecast reviews from performance reviews.

Critics note that Beyond Budgeting requires strong culture, transparent information and mature leadership; without them, removing budgets can remove control. Statutory and lender requirements (Board-approved plans, covenant projections) also mean a financial plan still exists — the change is in how it is used.

Connections

Beyond Budgeting is the main critique of the process in s01–s08 and is directly connected to budget gaming (c03) and Jensen (c05). Its rolling forecast principle is the subject of Week 18; its relative-target principle links to peer benchmarking (Weeks 7–8). ZBB (Topic 3) and Beyond Budgeting share the goal of challenging incremental resource allocation but differ — ZBB intensifies annual justification while Beyond Budgeting makes allocation continuous.

Practice Questions

Q1. Mapping principles to an AOP redesign [L2]

For a ₹3,000 crore Indian FMCG company with a traditional AOP, propose one concrete change for each of the six management-process principles (rhythm, targets, plans and forecasts, resource allocation, performance evaluation, rewards), and state one risk of each change.

💡 Hint: Keep the Board-approved financial plan; change how it is used.

Q2. Interview: is the annual budget dead? [L3]

An interviewer at a GCC asks whether companies should abolish the annual budget. Give a balanced answer referring to Beyond Budgeting, Statoil/Equinor, statutory and lender requirements in India, and the elements you would adopt.

Q3. Decoupling in practice [L2]

A BU's AOP revenue is ₹500 crore. In Q2, the unbiased forecast is ₹470 crore. Explain how the target, forecast and resource allocation would each be handled under a traditional approach and under a decoupled approach, and the behaviour each encourages.
