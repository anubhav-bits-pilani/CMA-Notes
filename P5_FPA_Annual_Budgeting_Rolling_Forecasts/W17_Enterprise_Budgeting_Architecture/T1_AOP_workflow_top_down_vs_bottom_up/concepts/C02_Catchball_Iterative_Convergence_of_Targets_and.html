Key concept

Catchball: Iterative Convergence of Targets and Plans

Concept: Catchball: iterative negotiation where targets descend and evidence-backed plans ascend until convergence

Statement

Catchball is a planning protocol in which, in each round \(k\):

  1. Leadership passes down a target \(T_k\) (and the reasoning behind it).
  2. The receiving level returns a plan \(B_k\) supported by drivers, initiatives and risks.
  3. Leadership reviews the evidence and either accepts, adjusts the target to \(T_{k+1}\) or challenges the plan, prompting \(B_{k+1}\).

The process stops when \(\lvert T_k - B_k \rvert \le \varepsilon\) (an agreed tolerance) or when a decision-maker with DoA authority closes the gap by decision. Each round is versioned (V0, V1, V2) and documented.

Explanation

The term comes from hoshin kanri (policy deployment), developed in Japanese manufacturing and popularised in lean management. Its central idea is that strategy deployment is a dialogue, not a decree: the people who receive a target know things about feasibility that leadership does not, and leadership knows things about strategic necessity that BUs do not. Passing the "ball" back and forth surfaces both sets of information.

Conditions for catchball to work:

  • Evidence standards: plans that ascend must be driver-backed (volumes, rates, headcount, initiatives). An ascending plan of "we can only do 8%" without drivers is not a valid return throw.
  • Transparency of the target's rationale: descending targets should explain why (LRP, investor guidance, peer benchmarks), so BUs can propose alternative ways of meeting the underlying need.
  • Bounded rounds: typically two formal rounds in the AOP (BU × FP&A × CFO, then CEO + CFO), with a hard calendar; open-ended catchball becomes an endless negotiation.
  • Multi-level cascade: catchball happens at each level — CEO to BU heads, BU heads to regional managers, regional managers to area managers — so the quota and cost centre budgets converge too.
  • Documented outcomes: the challenge log records each throw, making the final budget explainable.

What catchball is not: a ritual in which BUs negotiate down and leadership negotiates up until they meet in the middle. Splitting the difference rewards sandbagging (c03); evidence-based convergence rewards accurate planning.

FP&A's role is that of the referee and analyst: preparing the challenge analytics, keeping versions and the log, and quantifying the consequences of each proposal on the consolidated plan.

Connections

Catchball is the process engine of s05 (gap bridge) and uses the gap measure in c01. It is undermined by gaming (c03) and by cliff-based incentives (c05, s08), and governed by the DoA (c06), which determines who can make the final throw. Its cascade logic reappears in Topic 2's quota setting and in Week 18 rolling forecasts, where the dialogue is about forecasts rather than targets.

Practice Questions

Q1. Designing a two-round catchball [L2]

A company with four BUs has seven weeks between the V0 cut-off (15 December) and the final submission. Design the two catchball rounds: participants, pre-reads, agenda, evidence standards, outputs and dates, and state the tolerance at which you would stop.

💡 Hint: Allow resubmission time after each round and avoid the Q3 results close week.

Q2. Split-the-difference failure [L3]

In the last two cycles, a BU submitted 20% below target and the CFO settled halfway each time. Explain why this outcome is predictable, its effect on future submissions, and how you would change the catchball rules this year.

Q3. Catchball below BU level [L2]

A BU head has accepted a revenue budget of ₹600 crore. Describe how catchball would work with his five regional managers, including how you would prevent the sum of regional plans from falling short of ₹600 crore.
