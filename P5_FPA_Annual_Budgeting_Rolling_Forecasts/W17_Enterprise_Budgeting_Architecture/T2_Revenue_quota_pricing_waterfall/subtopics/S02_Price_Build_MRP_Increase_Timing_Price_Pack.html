Price Build: MRP, Increase Timing, Price-Pack Architecture, Grammage

Scope: Price build: list price/MRP, price increase timing, price-pack architecture, grammage changes

Why it matters

Price is the most powerful profit lever in consumer goods — a 1% net price increase with no volume loss typically adds more to EBITDA than a 3–4% volume gain. In the budget, price is built from MRP and list prices per SKU, the timing of increases, the price-pack architecture (PPA) that determines which packs sit at which price points, and grammage changes that move effective price per gram without changing the shelf price. FP&A must model these precisely because they drive revenue, GST, GTN, inventory revaluation and the price element of the PVM bridge (Week 19).

Core notes

MRP and list price

In India, packaged commodities must carry a Maximum Retail Price (MRP) inclusive of all taxes under the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011. The company's revenue is not MRP; it is the price at which it invoices its customer (distributor, MT chain, platform). Work back from MRP through the channel margin chain:

Step (per unit) Amount (₹) Basis
MRP (incl. GST) 150.00 Printed on pack
Less GST at 5% (embedded) 7.14 150 × 5/105
MRP ex-GST 142.86
Less retailer margin (16% of MRP ex-GST) 22.86 Trade norm by category
Retailer landed cost ex-GST (= distributor selling price) 120.00
Less distributor margin (6.67% on distributor selling price, i.e., ₹8) 8.00 Distributor ROI-based
Price to distributor ex-GST (company list price) 112.00 Company gross sales price

Margin conventions differ — some companies express retailer margin on MRP incl. GST, or as markup on cost — so state the convention explicitly. Company "gross sales" in the budget is the list price to the trade (₹112), after which the GTN waterfall (s03) applies.

For MT and e-commerce, list prices may be channel-specific (or the same list with different on-invoice terms). Exports are priced in foreign currency.

Price increase timing

A price increase affects revenue only from the date it reaches invoices — and the effective date differs by channel:

  • GT: list price changes on a date; however, new MRP packs must be produced and old MRP stock in the channel sells through. Companies often give distributors notice, triggering pre-increase buying (forward buying) in the weeks before.
  • MT: key accounts require notice (often 30–60 days) and may negotiate delays; the increase may be effective later than GT.
  • E-commerce: listing price changes can be immediate but platform agreements may require notice.

Budget mechanics: apply the increase from the effective month by channel; model forward buying (primary shifted into the month before) and its reversal; apply elasticity to volume (s07, c05); revalue the GTN items that are percentage-based. Price increase effect for the year:

\[ \text{Price effect} = \sum_{m \ge m_0} V_m \times P_0 \times g \]

where \(V_m\) is volume in month \(m\) from the effective month \(m_0\), \(P_0\) the old price and \(g\) the increase %.

Illustration: list price ₹92 detergent, +4% from 1 July; monthly volume 25 lakh units (flat for simplicity). Price effect = 25 lakh × 9 months × ₹92 × 4% = ₹8.28 crore. If the increase slips to 1 October, the effect falls to ₹5.52 crore — timing is worth ₹2.76 crore.

Price-pack architecture (PPA)

PPA is the deliberate design of pack sizes and price points across consumer segments and channels. Indian FMCG is built around price points (₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20 low-unit packs — LUPs) for rural and lower-income consumers, plus larger packs for MT and urban consumers. Principles:

  • Each pack targets a consumer, channel and occasion (sachet for trial/rural; 1 kg for monthly purchase; value packs for MT; combos for e-commerce).
  • Price per unit volume (₹ per 100 g/ml) normally declines with pack size, but the ladder must avoid cannibalising margins (a large pack too cheap per gram drags mix down).
  • Price points are sticky: a ₹10 pack cannot easily become ₹11, so price action at LUPs is taken through grammage.

In the budget, plan by pack with its own price and margin; mix shifts between packs then show up in the PVM bridge.

Grammage changes

When a price point cannot move, companies change grammage (pack content): reduce a ₹10 biscuit pack from 75 g to 68 g (an effective price increase of 75/68 − 1 = 10.3% per gram), or increase grammage to give value. Budget implications:

  • Revenue per pack unchanged; volume in packs may be stable but volume in tonnes falls, affecting RM, production and freight.
  • Per-unit COGS falls with lower content; gross margin per pack improves.
  • Legal Metrology labelling and new packaging material — budget artwork and transition costs and the write-off of old packaging stock.
  • Track both pack volume and tonnage in the volume cube so the RM budget is correct.

Model implementation

Price table by SKU × channel × month, generated from base price, increase %, effective month by channel:

=[@Base_Price]*(1+IF(F$3>=[@Increase_Month],[@Increase_Pct],0))

Keep MRP, GST rate and margin chain inputs for SKUs where the list price is derived from MRP, so a change in GST rate or retailer margin flows through correctly.

GST effects

GST is not revenue but matters for price because MRP is GST-inclusive. When GST rates fall (as for many personal care products in September 2025), companies must either reduce MRP (passing the benefit to consumers — anti-profiteering scrutiny historically applied) or increase grammage. Budget the chosen response and its effect on list price; the receivable and GST payable in the cash budget are on the GST-inclusive invoice value.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Old MRP stock in the channel: after an MRP increase, old-MRP stock sells at the old price; companies sometimes compensate distributors; budget the compensation as a GTN item.
  • MT price-protection clauses may require compensation for retailer stock when prices fall; model it if a price reduction is planned.
  • Differential pricing by state is limited by MRP being national on most packs but freight and schemes vary by state.
  • Price increases vs scheme reductions: reducing schemes raises net price without changing list price — budget it in GTN, not as a list price increase, so the PVM bridge classifies it correctly.
  • Commodity-linked pricing (edible oils, detergents) may require multiple increases; plan a base case with one increase and an RM-linked scenario.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue price is the list price to the trade, derived from MRP through GST and channel margins — not MRP itself.
  • Increase timing by channel, forward buying and elasticity determine the price effect in the year.
  • Price-pack architecture assigns packs to price points; LUP price points are sticky and moved through grammage.
  • Grammage changes alter tonnage, RM, COGS and freight even when pack volume and price are unchanged.
  • Model prices in a SKU × channel × month table driven by base price, increase % and effective month.
  • GST rate changes flow into MRP decisions and cash, not revenue.

Practice Questions

Q1. MRP to list price [L1]

A toothpaste with MRP ₹120 (GST 5%) has a retailer margin of 15% of MRP ex-GST and a distributor margin of ₹6 per unit. Compute the company's list price to distributor ex-GST.

💡 Hint: Strip GST first using the tax-inclusive fraction.

Q2. Value of increase timing [L2]

A biscuit brand sells 40 lakh packs a month at a list price of ₹8.50 ex-GST. A 5% increase is planned from 1 August in GT (70% of volume) and 1 October in MT (30%). Elasticity is −0.6, applied to volume from the effective month. Distributors forward-buy 15% of August GT volume in July. Compute the price effect, the volume effect and the net revenue impact for FY 2027-28, and the monthly primary volume profile July–September for GT.

Q3. Grammage reduction impact [L2]

A ₹10 MRP namkeen pack (GST 5%, list price ₹7.40) reduces content from 40 g to 36 g from 1 June 2027. Annual volume is 12 crore packs (evenly phased); RM and packaging cost is ₹0.11 per gram plus ₹0.60 per pack. Compute the effective price increase per gram, the annual gross margin impact, and the tonnage reduction to pass to the RM purchase budget.

Q4. PPA review [L3]

A hair oil range has packs of 50 ml at ₹35, 100 ml at ₹68, 200 ml at ₹130 and 500 ml at ₹280 (MRPs). The budget assumes the 500 ml pack grows 40% in MT. Evaluate the price ladder per 100 ml, the mix risk to margins, and the questions you would ask the brand team before accepting the budget.
