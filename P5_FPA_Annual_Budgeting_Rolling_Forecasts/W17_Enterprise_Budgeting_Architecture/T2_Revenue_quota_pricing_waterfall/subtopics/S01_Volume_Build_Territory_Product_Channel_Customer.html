Subtopic note

Volume Build: Territory × Product × Channel × Customer Class

Scope: Territory × product × channel × customer-class volume build (primary vs secondary sales in FMCG distribution)

Why it matters

Volume is the base driver for revenue, production, RM purchases, freight, trade spend and sales incentives. In FMCG and consumer durables, volume is planned in a multi-dimensional cube — territory (zone, state, area), product (category, brand, SKU/pack), channel (general trade, modern trade, e-commerce, quick commerce, institutional, CSD/CPC, exports) and customer class (super-stockist, distributor, wholesaler, key account) — because each dimension has different growth, price and margin. Getting the cube's grain right determines whether the budget can later be forecast and bridged (Weeks 18–19). The FMCG distribution chain also forces a clear choice between primary and secondary sales.

Core notes

The Indian FMCG distribution chain

Flow From → to Recorded in Used for
Primary sales Company → super-stockist/distributor (GT), → MT chain DC, → e-commerce/quick commerce Company ERP invoices Revenue (Ind AS 115), GST, receivables
Secondary sales Distributor → retailer/wholesaler Distributor Management System (DMS) Sales force performance, demand, scheme calculation
Tertiary / offtake Retailer → consumer Retail audits (NielsenIQ), MT POS data, e-commerce sell-through Market share, consumer demand

Primary revenue differs from secondary by changes in channel inventory (distributor stock):

\[ \text{Primary}_t = \text{Secondary}_t + \text{Closing distributor stock}_t - \text{Opening distributor stock}_t \]

Distributor stock is usually managed in days of secondary sales (e.g., target 21–30 days in GT). A budget that plans healthy secondary growth while reducing distributor stock will show lower primary growth; one that "loads" the channel shows primary above secondary — sustainable only briefly.

Choosing the grain of the cube

Dimension Typical budget grain Why
Territory Zone/state (budget); area/town (quota) GST state-wise, distribution depth, freight
Product Brand × pack (SKU groups), 50–300 lines Price points and GTN differ by pack
Channel GT, MT, e-commerce, quick commerce, institutional, CSD, exports Margin and payment terms differ
Customer class Super-stockist, distributor, wholesaler, key account Margins, schemes and credit differ
Time Month Seasonality, cash

The test: plan at the lowest level at which drivers differ materially and data exists; aggregate above that. Planning 2,000 SKUs × 600 towns × 12 months is unmanageable in Excel; planning 80 SKU groups × 20 states × 5 channels × 12 months (96,000 cells) is manageable in a structured table or EPM.

Building secondary volume

For GT, the driver-based secondary build is outlet-led:

\[ \text{Secondary volume} = \text{Outlets billed} \times \text{Lines per call} \times \text{Units per line} \times \text{Calls per month} \]

or more simply outlets × rate of sale (c06). For MT and e-commerce, volume is built by account: stores × store throughput, or e-commerce GMV ÷ selling price. Baseline growth is decomposed into:

  • Market growth (category value or volume growth from NielsenIQ).
  • Share change (from distribution gains, launches, activation).
  • Price-led volume effect (elasticity, s07).
  • Channel shift (GT → MT, e-commerce, quick commerce).

Deriving primary volume

  1. Budget secondary volume by month per distributor cluster.
  2. Set target closing distributor stock days per month (e.g., 25 days, with festive build-up to 30 days in September).
  3. Compute closing stock = secondary of the next month × days ÷ days in month (or simpler: next month's secondary × days/30).
  4. Primary = secondary + closing stock − opening stock.

Illustration (detergent, GT, lakh units)

Month Secondary Closing stock days Closing stock (next month × days/30) Opening stock Primary
August 18.0 25 17.5 (21.0 × 25/30) 15.0 20.5
September 21.0 30 25.2 (25.2 × 30/30) 17.5 28.7
October 25.2 22 16.9 (23.0 × 22/30) 25.2 16.9

The festive build-up pulls primary into September; post-festive destocking depresses October primary even though secondary peaks. Budgeting primary at secondary growth would misstate both months.

Excel/model implementation

Use a structured table with one row per territory × SKU group × channel × customer class and columns for each month's secondary volume, stock days and derived primary. Inputs (baseline volume, growth components, stock days) are blue cells; drivers come from the assumptions sheet.

=[@Secondary_Next]*[@Stock_Days]/30
=[@Secondary]+[@Closing_Stock]-[@Opening_Stock]
=SUMIFS(tblVolume[Primary],tblVolume[Channel],"GT",tblVolume[Month],F$3)

Presentation

Show the volume budget as: secondary by channel and region; distributor stock days by month; primary by channel; growth decomposition (market, share, price, channel shift). Reconcile to last year's actuals at the same grain.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Super-stockist layers (common in rural and north-east markets) add another stock point; model stock at each layer or treat super-stockist + distributor as one combined stock.
  • E-commerce and quick commerce: the platform may be a marketplace (company sells to seller entities or directly to consumers) or an inventory model (sells to platform) — channel stock and revenue recognition differ (principal vs agent).
  • CSD/CPC (Canteen Stores Department) and institutional sales have tender-based pricing and lumpy timing; budget them by order, not seasonality.
  • Exports: primary only; revenue recognition on Incoterms, FX at budget rate.
  • Year-end loading: a historical March primary spike (distributors pushed to meet annual targets) must be identified and not built into the seasonality baseline if management intends to stop it.
  • DMS data quality: secondary data from smaller distributors may be incomplete; validate coverage before relying on it.

Key takeaways

  • Plan volume in a cube: territory × product × channel × customer class × month, at the lowest grain where drivers differ and data exists.
  • Primary sales (revenue) = secondary sales + change in distributor stock; plan both and keep them separate.
  • Build secondary from market drivers — outlets, rate of sale, market growth, share and channel shift.
  • Set explicit distributor stock days by month, including festive build-up and destocking.
  • Use structured tables and SUMIFS so the cube can be rolled up and later bridged in PVM analysis.
  • Treat MT, e-commerce, institutional and exports with channel-specific drivers and recognition rules.

Practice Questions

Q1. Primary from secondary with stock days [L2]

Secondary sales of soap in GT (lakh units) for March–July 2027 are 40, 46, 48, 44 and 38. Opening distributor stock on 1 April 2027 is 36 lakh units. Target closing stock is 25 days of next month's secondary for April, May and July and 20 days for June (destocking ahead of monsoon). Compute primary sales for April–June 2027 using a 30-day month convention.

💡 Hint: Closing stock each month depends on the following month's secondary sales.

Q2. Designing the cube grain [L2]

A personal care company has 420 SKUs, 28 states, 6 channels and 4 customer classes. Propose the budget grain for each dimension, estimate the number of rows in your structured table, and explain what you would aggregate and why.

Q3. Growth decomposition [L2]

FY 2026-27 secondary volume of a tea brand is 520 lakh packs. Category volume growth is forecast at 4%; the brand plans distribution gains adding 1.5% share of category within the brand's footprint (equivalent to 26 lakh packs), a price increase that reduces volume by 1.2%, and a channel shift that moves 10 lakh packs from GT to quick commerce with no change in total. Build the FY 2027-28 volume bridge.

Q4. Channel loading review [L3]

A regional head's budget shows primary growth of 15% and secondary growth of 8%, with distributor stock rising from 24 to 34 days by March 2028. Assess the implications for revenue quality, distributor ROI, receivables and returns, and write the challenge you would raise in catchball.
