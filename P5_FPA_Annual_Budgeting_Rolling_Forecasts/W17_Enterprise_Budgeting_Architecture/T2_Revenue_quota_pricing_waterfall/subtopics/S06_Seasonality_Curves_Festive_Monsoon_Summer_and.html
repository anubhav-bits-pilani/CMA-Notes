Seasonality Curves: Festive, Monsoon, Summer and Period-End Loading

Scope: Seasonality curves: festive (Diwali/Q3), monsoon, summer categories, month-end/quarter-end loading

Why it matters

Indian consumer demand is strongly seasonal, and the seasons differ by category. Diwali and the wedding season lift Q3 (October–December) for gifting, sweets, apparel, durables, hair oil and home care; summer (March–June) lifts beverages, ice cream, talc, deodorants and soaps; the monsoon (June–September) depresses outdoor consumption and rural movement but lifts tea, coffee and some health products; winter lifts skin creams and chyawanprash. On top of consumer seasonality sits trade loading: primary sales spike at month-end and quarter-end as sales teams chase targets. A monthly budget that ignores seasonality creates false variances every month and misstates working capital and borrowing needs — the revolver peak in a festive business is typically in September–October.

Core notes

The Diwali date moves between mid-October and mid-November, shifting volume between October and November year to year. When building indices from history, align by Diwali date (e.g., index by "weeks before Diwali") rather than calendar month, then map onto next year's calendar.

Computing seasonality indices

Using \(n\) years of monthly sales history (at least three, preferably de-trended), for each month \(m\):

\[ I_m = \frac{\bar{S}_m}{\bar{S}}, \qquad \sum_{m=1}^{12} I_m = 12 \]

where \(\bar{S}_m\) is the average sales in month \(m\) across years and \(\bar{S}\) the average monthly sales across all months (c03). If history has strong growth, de-trend first (divide each month by its year's monthly average, then average the ratios across years) so later months are not overstated simply because they come later in a growing year.

Monthly budget:

\[ B_m = B_{\text{annual}} \times \frac{I_m}{12} \]

Adjustments before applying:

  • Price increase timing: if the index is built on value, remove past price effects; better to build indices on volume and apply price separately.
  • Diwali shift: move the festive share between October and November according to next year's date.
  • Launches and one-offs: exclude launch pipeline fills, one-time institutional orders and stock-outs from the history.
  • Re-normalise so indices still sum to 12 after adjustments.

Illustration

Detergent monthly volume history (lakh units):

Month FY25 FY26 FY27 LE Average Raw index
April 22.0 23.5 24.0 23.17 0.93
October 28.0 29.5 30.0 29.17 1.17
Annual total 290 300 310 300 12.00

Average monthly sales = 300 ÷ 12 = 25.0; October index = 29.17 ÷ 25.0 ≈ 1.17 before rounding and Diwali adjustment, set at 1.20 because Diwali FY28 falls in late October (more festive buying inside the month). FY 2027-28 budget of 330 lakh units: October = 330 × 1.20 ÷ 12 = 33.0 lakh units.

Month-end and quarter-end loading

Primary sales often show 35–50% of the month's invoicing in the last week, and March may be 10–20% above a normal month because of annual-target pressure. For the monthly budget, decide whether to:

  • Replicate the loading (realistic if behaviour will continue; important for receivable and cash timing), or
  • Normalise it as a management initiative (e.g., moving to secondary-based targets to reduce loading), in which case the budget shows smoother primary sales and lower March receivables.

For a weekly cash forecast (Topic 4), intra-month loading matters: collections from last-week invoicing arrive in the next month.

Seasonality for costs and working capital

Seasonality is not only for revenue. Apply corresponding curves to production (subject to capacity — production may be smoothed ahead of the peak, building FG inventory), RM purchases (seasonal crops such as copra, tea leaf, mentha — bought at harvest), freight, sales incentives and trade schemes (festive schemes are larger). The working capital budget then shows inventory peaking before the festive season and receivables peaking during it.

Excel implementation

Store indices in an assumptions table by category × channel × month, with a check that each row sums to 12:

=ROUND(SUM(tblSeason[@[Apr]:[Mar]]),6)=12
=[@Annual_Volume]*INDEX(tblSeason[[Apr]:[Mar]],MATCH([@Category],tblSeason[Category],0),F$2)/12

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Too little history or structural change (new channel like quick commerce): use category-level or channel proxy indices and revise after two quarters.
  • Capacity-constrained peaks: if the plant cannot produce the October peak, the sales budget may still show it (demand), but the production budget must pre-build — check inventory and storage capacity.
  • Weather variability: a delayed monsoon can shift summer categories; keep the budget on normal seasonality and handle weather in forecasts and scenarios.
  • Regional festivals (Onam in Kerala in August–September, Pongal in January) require state-level indices for national companies.
  • Trading days: months with more Sundays or holidays have fewer selling days; for large daily-rate businesses (quick commerce, beverages), adjust indices for trading days.

Key takeaways

  • Indian seasonality is category-specific: festive Q3, summer, monsoon, winter and harvest cycles.
  • Indices = average month ÷ average monthly sales, sum to 12; build on volume, de-trend, align for Diwali, exclude one-offs, re-normalise.
  • Monthly budget = annual × index ÷ 12.
  • Decide explicitly whether to replicate or normalise month-end and March loading.
  • Apply seasonality to production, purchases, freight, schemes and working capital, not just revenue.
  • Check that index rows sum to 12 and are linked to the right category and channel.

Practice Questions

Q1. Computing seasonality indices [L2]

Hair oil volume (lakh units) for April–March: FY25 — 11, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 17, 15, 13, 12; FY26 — 12, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 19, 18, 16, 13, 12. Compute indices by simple averaging and by de-trended ratio averaging, compare them and phase an FY 2027-28 budget of 180 lakh units using the de-trended indices.

💡 Hint: For the de-trended method, divide each month by its own year's monthly average before averaging across years.

Q2. Diwali shift adjustment [L2]

In history, Diwali fell in early November and the indices are October 1.10 and November 1.35. In FY 2027-28 Diwali falls in late October, and management estimates 40% of the November festive uplift (above an index of 1.0) moves into October. Compute the adjusted October and November indices and confirm the total still sums to 12.

Q3. Normalising March loading [L3]

Primary sales history shows March at an index of 1.25 versus secondary of 1.02. Management intends to cap distributor stock at 25 days from FY 2027-28. Explain how you would adjust the primary seasonality curve, and the effects on revenue, receivables at 31 March 2028 and the cash budget.

Q4. Seasonality beyond revenue [L2]

A coconut-oil based hair oil maker buys copra mainly in March–June when prices are lowest. Explain how you would reflect seasonal RM buying, a festive sales peak and a flat production plan in the inventory and revolver budget, and what checks you would build.
