Subtopic note

Launches, Cannibalisation, Distribution Gains and Price Elasticity

Scope: New product launches, cannibalisation, distribution gains (numeric/weighted distribution) and price elasticity

Why it matters

The base volume build (s01) projects the existing business. Growth beyond market growth comes from four layers that FP&A must model explicitly: new product launches, net of cannibalisation of existing products; distribution gains — more outlets stocking the product; and price changes, whose volume effect depends on price elasticity. These are exactly the items BU heads use to justify ambitious plans in catchball, and the items most often overstated. Modelling them with explicit drivers lets you test the claims and later track delivery.

Core notes

New product launches

A launch volume build has three components:

  1. Pipeline fill — initial stocking of distributors and retailers (primary sales without consumer offtake). Typically 3–6 weeks of expected secondary sales per stocking point. It is one-off and must not be extrapolated.
  2. Distribution build-up — outlets stocking the product over time (numeric distribution curve, e.g., 0% → 25% of target outlets in six months).
  3. Rate of sale (ROS) — units per stocking outlet per month, often starting high (trial) and settling lower (repeat), sometimes with a dip after launch advertising stops.

\[ \text{Launch secondary}_m = \text{Outlets stocking}_m \times \text{ROS}_m \]

\[ \text{Launch primary}_m = \text{Launch secondary}_m + \Delta\text{Channel stock}_m \]

Add launch costs (A&P, sampling, listing fees, new moulds/artwork capex, slotting) and a gross margin that may be lower in the first year (low scale, trial packs). Many companies apply a launch success haircut in the budget (e.g., include 70% of the brand team's volume) or include launches only once gate approval is obtained.

Cannibalisation

Cannibalisation is the share of a new product's volume taken from the company's existing products rather than from competitors or category growth:

\[ \text{Incremental volume} = \text{Launch volume} \times (1 - c) \]

where \(c\) is the cannibalisation rate. Measure in a comparable unit (tonnes, ml, consumption occasions) when pack sizes differ. Estimates come from test markets, consumer research, analogues (previous launches) and the positioning: a premium variant of an existing brand may cannibalise 40–60%; a new segment entry 10–20%. Model cannibalised volume as a reduction in the parent SKU's volume and value it at the parent's net price — the revenue and margin impact can differ greatly if the new product has a different price per unit.

Distribution: numeric and weighted

  • Numeric distribution (ND) — the percentage of all outlets in the universe (e.g., all grocery outlets in a town class) that stock the product. 30% ND = 30 out of every 100 outlets.
  • Weighted distribution (WD) — the percentage of total category sales accounted for by outlets that stock the product. 50% WD = outlets that stock it account for half of category sales.

\[ \text{ND} = \frac{\text{Outlets stocking}}{\text{Outlets in universe}}, \qquad \text{WD} = \frac{\sum_{\text{stocking}} \text{Category sales}_j}{\sum_{\text{all}} \text{Category sales}_j} \]

WD > ND means the product is in the larger, higher-selling outlets; WD < ND means it is mainly in small outlets. Distribution gains drive volume through the outlet count (c06):

\[ \text{Distribution-led volume} = \text{Outlets reached} \times \text{ROS} \times \text{Months} \]

In India, distribution expansion — "direct reach" in rural markets, Project Shakti-style rural networks, van sales — is a major growth lever. Budget the cost to serve (additional salesmen, distributor margins, van costs) alongside the volume. Note that new outlets usually have lower ROS than existing ones (smaller, rural), so apply a separate ROS.

Data sources: NielsenIQ retail audit (ND, WD, share), the company's DMS (outlets billed), sales force automation (SFA) apps.

Price elasticity

Price elasticity of demand (c05):

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P} \]

typically negative. Volume impact of a price increase:

\[ \Delta V = V_0 \times \varepsilon \times \%\Delta P \]

Revenue impact:

\[ \Delta R = V_0 P_0 \left[(1 + \%\Delta P)(1 + \varepsilon \times \%\Delta P) - 1\right] \]

Elasticities are estimated from history (regressions of volume on relative price, controlling for promotions and seasonality), RGM studies, conjoint research or analogues. Indian FMCG elasticities for staples are commonly in the −0.3 to −0.8 range in the short run; discretionary and premium categories, and products with close competitors at the same price point, can be above −1 (elastic). Relative price matters: if competitors raise prices simultaneously (common with commodity inflation), the effective elasticity is much lower than the own-price elasticity.

Illustration — combined layers

Hair oil base volume 180 lakh units at NSV ₹95 per unit. (a) Price +3% from October (6 months, base volume 95 lakh units in Oct–Mar); elasticity −0.4: volume loss = 95 × (−0.4) × 3% = −1.14 lakh units. (b) Distribution: 20,000 new outlets reached from July at ROS 12 units/month for 9 months = 21.6 lakh units. (c) Launch of a 100 ml variant: secondary 30 lakh units with 35% cannibalisation of the 200 ml (in ml terms: 30 lakh × 100 ml × 35% = 1.05 lakh litres = 5.25 lakh units of 200 ml).

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Pipeline fill in March: a launch in March shows large primary with little offtake — exclude it from growth assessments and watch for returns.
  • Cannibalisation from price-pack changes: a new LUP may cannibalise larger packs at a lower price per gram, reducing gross margin even if volume grows.
  • Distribution quality: counting outlets billed once as "reached" overstates distribution; use outlets billed at least once in each of the last three months (active outlets).
  • Elasticity asymmetry: consumers react more to visible MRP increases than to grammage reductions; and price decreases may not raise volume as much as increases reduce it.
  • Competitive response: model at least a downside scenario where competitors do not follow the price increase.

Key takeaways

  • Model launches as pipeline fill + distribution build-up × rate of sale, with a success haircut and launch costs.
  • Incremental launch volume = launch volume × (1 − cannibalisation), measured in comparable units and valued at each product's net price.
  • Numeric distribution counts outlets; weighted distribution weights them by category sales; both drive outlet-led volume.
  • Distribution-led volume = outlets reached × ROS × months, with lower ROS for new outlets and the cost to serve budgeted.
  • Elasticity converts price changes into volume changes; use relative elasticity when competitors also move.
  • Present growth as a layered bridge — base, price, distribution, launch, cannibalisation — for catchball and PVM.

Practice Questions

Q1. Launch volume and cannibalisation [L2]

A 75 g premium soap launches in August 2027. Outlets stocking: 50,000 in August, rising by 25,000 a month to 2,00,000 in February and flat in March. ROS: 30 units/outlet/month in the first two months after an outlet is stocked, then 18 (assume a simple average ROS of 22 for all months). Pipeline fill is 4 weeks of secondary sales on each new outlet. Cannibalisation of the existing 100 g soap is 40% in grams. Compute monthly launch primary and secondary volume, cannibalised 100 g units, and incremental grams.

💡 Hint: Pipeline fill applies only to newly added outlets each month.

Q2. ND vs WD interpretation [L1]

Brand A has ND 62% and WD 78%; Brand B has ND 70% and WD 55% in the same category. Interpret each and recommend the distribution priority for each brand.

Q3. Elasticity-based price decision [L2]

A tea brand sells 80 lakh kg a year at NSV ₹380 per kg with a contribution margin of 32%. Management considers a 6% price increase. Own-price elasticity is −0.9; if competitors follow, effective elasticity is −0.35. Compute the revenue and contribution impact under both cases and the break-even elasticity at which contribution is unchanged.

Q4. Challenging distribution claims [L3]

A BU's budget includes 18% volume growth driven by "adding 1 lakh outlets" with ROS equal to the existing average of 45 units per month and no additional sales costs. Write the challenge you would raise, the data you would ask for, and a revised estimate structure.
