Key concept

Splitting Semi-Variable Costs: High-Low and Regression

Concept: High-low/regression to split semi-variable cost: b = Δcost ÷ Δactivity; a = Total cost − b × activity

Statement

Cost model: \(Y = a + bX\).

High-low method:

\[ b = \frac{Y_H - Y_L}{X_H - X_L}, \qquad a = Y_H - b X_H \; (= Y_L - b X_L) \]

Least-squares regression:

\[ b = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2}, \qquad a = \bar{Y} - b\bar{X} \]

where \(Y\) is total cost for a period, \(X\) the activity level, \(a\) the fixed element per period, \(b\) the variable cost per unit of activity, \(H\) and \(L\) the periods with the highest and lowest activity (not cost), \(\bar{X}\) and \(\bar{Y}\) means over \(n\) observations. In Excel: =SLOPE(Y,X) for \(b\), =INTERCEPT(Y,X) for \(a\), =RSQ(Y,X) for \(R^2\), and =LINEST(Y,X,TRUE,TRUE) for full statistics.

Explanation

Flexible budgets need the fixed and variable elements of mixed costs. High-low is quick and uses only two observations — the highest and lowest activity points — so it is sensitive to outliers and ignores the rest of the data. Regression uses all observations and minimises squared errors; \(R^2\) shows how much of the cost variation activity explains, and the standard error of \(b\) shows its reliability.

Practical conditions:

  • Choose H and L by activity, not by cost.
  • Clean the data: remove months with one-offs (annual overhaul, tariff arrears, accounting reclassifications) or adjust them.
  • Same price level: restate costs at constant rates if tariffs changed during the sample.
  • Relevant range: the intercept \(a\) is an extrapolation; it is the fixed cost only within the observed range, not necessarily the cost at zero activity.
  • Plausibility: a negative intercept or a variable rate inconsistent with engineering estimates signals a poor model — consider another driver or multiple regression (LINEST with several \(X\) columns: e.g. machine hours and batches).
  • Sample size: at least 12–24 monthly observations for regression to be meaningful.

Illustration

Power cost vs machine hours for six months:

Month Machine hours (X) Power cost ₹ lakh (Y)
Apr 3,800 15.2
May 4,600 17.3
Jun 3,200 13.9
Jul 5,000 18.4
Aug 4,200 16.1
Sep 4,400 17.0

High-low: H = Jul (5,000; 18.4), L = Jun (3,200; 13.9).

\[ b = \frac{18.4 - 13.9}{5{,}000 - 3{,}200} = \frac{4.5}{1{,}800} = 0.0025 \text{ lakh} = ₹250 \text{ per hour}, \qquad a = 18.4 - 0.0025 \times 5{,}000 = ₹5.9 \text{ lakh} \]

Flexed power for 4,700 hours = 5.9 + 0.0025 × 4,700 = ₹17.65 lakh. Regression on all six months (via SLOPE/INTERCEPT) will give a slightly different \(b\) and \(a\); compare both and check \(R^2\) before choosing.

Practice Questions

Q1. High-low [L1]

Maintenance cost (₹ lakh) and machine hours for five months: 9.8 (2,100), 11.6 (2,800), 8.9 (1,700), 12.5 (3,100), 10.4 (2,400). Using high-low, compute the variable rate and fixed element, and the flexed allowance for 2,650 hours.

Q2. Regression vs high-low [L2]

Using the power data in the illustration, compute (or set up in Excel) the regression estimates of \(a\) and \(b\) and \(R^2\). Compare with high-low and recommend which to use in the budget, stating your criteria.

💡 Hint: Compute means first; the regression slope uses deviations from the means for all six points.

Q3. Data cleansing [L2]

Twelve months of power data include one month with a ₹3.5 lakh arrears bill for the previous year and a tariff increase of 8% from October. Explain how each issue would distort high-low and regression estimates and how you would adjust the data before estimating the cost function.
