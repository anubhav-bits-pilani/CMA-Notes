Subtopic note

Activity-Based Flexible Budgets with Multiple Cost Drivers

Scope: Activity-based flexible budgets using multiple cost drivers (machine hours, batches, orders)

Why it matters

Traditional flexible budgets flex overheads on a single volume measure — units, labour hours or machine hours. In modern consumer goods operations a large share of overhead is driven not by volume but by complexity: number of SKUs, production batches and changeovers, purchase orders, customer orders, deliveries, quality inspections. A plant producing the same tonnage in twice as many small batches incurs much more set-up, cleaning and QC cost; a distribution centre shipping the same cases in more, smaller orders incurs more picking and dispatch cost. A volume-only flexible budget then shows "adverse efficiency" that is really a mix or complexity effect. Activity-based flexible budgets flex each cost pool on its own driver, giving managers fair allowances and giving commercial teams visibility of the cost of complexity (cost-to-serve, SKU rationalisation).

Core notes

Concept

An activity-based flexible budget (ABFB) applies the activity-based costing (ABC) cost hierarchy to budgeting:

Level Driver examples Cost examples
Unit-level Units, machine hours, kg Power for running machines, consumables per unit
Batch-level Number of batches / set-ups / changeovers Set-up labour, cleaning (CIP), changeover scrap, batch QC tests
Product-sustaining Number of SKUs, formulation changes Artwork and packaging development, regulatory registrations, SKU master maintenance
Customer/order-level Number of customer orders, deliveries, invoices Order processing, picking, dispatch, credit control
Facility-sustaining None (fixed) Plant management, security, insurance, depreciation

Each activity cost pool has a budgeted fixed element and a budgeted rate per unit of its driver:

\[ C^{\text{flex}} = \sum_{j} \left( a_j + b_j \times X^{\text{act}}_j \right) \]

where \(j\) indexes activity cost pools, \(a_j\) is the fixed part of pool \(j\), \(b_j\) the budgeted variable rate per unit of driver \(j\), and \(X^{\text{act}}_j\) the actual driver quantity (machine hours, batches, orders, etc.).

Building an ABFB

  1. Identify activities that consume significant overhead (interviews with plant, supply chain, customer service; time studies).
  2. Assign resource costs to activity pools (salaries by % time, power by metering, consumables by usage).
  3. Select a driver per pool — the measurable event that causes the cost. Prefer transaction drivers (count of batches) where cost per event is similar; duration drivers (set-up hours) where events vary greatly.
  4. Separate fixed and variable within each pool (capacity-related resources vs consumed resources).
  5. Compute budgeted rates = variable pool cost ÷ budgeted driver volume.
  6. Capture actual drivers monthly from systems: SAP PP (process orders/batches), QM (inspection lots), SD (sales orders, deliveries), WMS (picks), TMS (trips).
  7. Flex and compare: flexed pool cost vs actual pool cost; driver volume vs budget driver volume.

Variances in an ABFB

For each pool:

\[ \text{Spending variance}_j = (a_j + b_j X^{\text{act}}_j) - \text{Actual cost}_j \]

\[ \text{Activity volume variance}_j = b_j \times (X^{\text{bud,for act output}}_j - X^{\text{act}}_j) \]

where \(X^{\text{bud,for act output}}_j\) is the driver quantity that should have been required for the actual output (e.g. standard batches for actual production given standard batch size). The activity volume variance tells you whether more batches or orders were consumed than the output justified — the complexity effect.

Illustration

Plant budget for a month: 12,000 tonnes in 300 batches (standard batch 40 tonnes); 4,800 machine hours; 1,500 customer orders.

Pool Driver Fixed (₹ lakh) Rate Static (₹ lakh)
Machine running Machine hours 6.0 ₹450/h 27.6
Set-up and cleaning Batches 2.0 ₹6,000/batch 20.0
Quality testing Batches 3.0 ₹2,500/batch 10.5
Order processing and dispatch Customer orders 4.0 ₹800/order 16.0
Facility — 30.0 — 30.0
Total 104.1

Actual: 12,000 tonnes (same as budget), 4,900 machine hours, 380 batches (smaller batches due to more SKUs), 1,900 orders; actual overhead ₹113.6 lakh.

Volume-only flexible budget (flex everything except facility on tonnes): flexed = 104.1 (tonnes unchanged) → variance ₹9.5 lakh A, all labelled "spending/inefficiency".

ABFB flexed:

Pool Flexed (₹ lakh)
Machine running: 6.0 + 0.0045 × 4,900 28.05
Set-up: 2.0 + 0.06 × 380 24.80
QC: 3.0 + 0.025 × 380 12.50
Orders: 4.0 + 0.008 × 1,900 19.20
Facility 30.00
Total 114.55

Against actual ₹113.6 lakh, the plant is ₹0.95 lakh favourable on spending. The ₹10.45 lakh increase in allowance (114.55 − 104.1) is driven by complexity: 80 extra batches (₹6.8 lakh) and 400 extra orders (₹3.2 lakh), plus 100 extra machine hours (₹0.45 lakh). The issue is commercial (SKU proliferation, smaller order sizes), not plant efficiency — a very different management conversation.

Excel / Power BI implementation

Maintain an ActivityPools table (Pool, Driver, Fixed, Rate) and a monthly ActualDrivers table (Month, Driver, Quantity). Flexed cost per pool:

=[@Fixed] + [@Rate] * SUMIFS(tblActDrv[Qty], tblActDrv[Driver], [@Driver], tblActDrv[Month], SelMonth)

In Power BI, a driver dimension linking pools and actual driver quantities supports a "complexity bridge" visual: static → volume effect → batch effect → order effect → spending → actual.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Driver data availability: if batch counts are not reliably captured in the ERP, the ABFB will be disputed. Fix data capture first.
  • Too many pools: 5–10 well-chosen pools typically capture most of the insight; 50 pools become unmaintainable.
  • Capacity vs consumption: fixed elements within pools (QC lab staff) don't fall when batches fall — unused capacity should be reported (as in time-driven ABC) rather than hidden.
  • Behavioural response: plant managers may resist smaller batches; commercial teams may resist being charged for complexity. Use the ABFB for transparency and pricing/cost-to-serve decisions, not punishment.
  • Interaction with Ind AS 2: batch-level costs are production overheads and are inventoriable; order-processing and dispatch are selling costs and are not.
  • Time-driven ABC (Kaplan and Anderson) simplifies by estimating capacity cost per minute and minutes per activity — useful when activities are many and data are sparse.

Key takeaways

  • Flex each overhead pool on the driver that causes it: unit, batch, product, order or facility level.
  • ABFB separates complexity effects (more batches, more orders) from genuine spending variances.
  • Rates come from budgeted pool costs and driver volumes; actual drivers come from ERP/WMS data.
  • Activity volume variances signal SKU proliferation and cost-to-serve issues for commercial teams.
  • Keep the number of pools manageable and report unused capacity explicitly.

Practice Questions

Q1. Building an ABFB [L2]

A distribution centre budget: picking ₹12 lakh fixed + ₹4 per case picked (budget 6 lakh cases); dispatch ₹5 lakh fixed + ₹350 per delivery (budget 4,000 deliveries); returns handling ₹1 lakh fixed + ₹120 per return line (budget 2,500); facility ₹20 lakh. Actual: 6.2 lakh cases, 4,900 deliveries, 3,400 return lines; actual cost ₹93.5 lakh. Prepare the static and flexed budgets and analyse the variance by pool.

Q2. Batch-size economics [L2]

In the illustration, the plant could revert to 40-tonne batches only if SKU count is reduced from 140 to 110. Estimate the monthly overhead saving from fewer batches and describe what other information (lost sales, inventory, product-sustaining costs) you would need to recommend the SKU cut.

💡 Hint: Batch-level rates are in the activity pool table; product-sustaining costs may also fall.

Q3. Choosing drivers [L2]

For a contract-manufacturing-heavy FMCG company, propose activity pools and drivers for the in-house plant, the QC function, the order-to-cash team and the logistics function. Classify each pool in the ABC hierarchy and state where the driver data would come from.

Q4. Persuading the plant and sales heads [L3]

The plant head welcomes the ABFB; the sales head objects that "complexity costs are the plant's problem". Draft the key points of a one-page note to the CFO explaining how the ABFB should be used in monthly reviews, pricing of small orders, and the AOP target-setting for SKU count and minimum order quantities.
