Subtopic note

Overhead Flexing: Variable OH Spending/Efficiency, Fixed OH Spending/Volume

Scope: Overhead flexing: variable overhead spending/efficiency, fixed overhead spending/volume

Why it matters

Manufacturing overheads — power, fuel, consumables, maintenance, plant salaries, depreciation — are often 15–30% of conversion cost in consumer goods plants. Unlike materials, they are not consumed per unit by a bill of materials, so flexing requires an activity base (machine hours, labour hours, batches). The classic four-way overhead analysis splits overhead variances into spending and efficiency for variable overheads, and spending (expenditure) and volume for fixed overheads. The fixed overhead volume variance only exists under absorption costing and has a direct link to Ind AS 2 inventory valuation: under-absorbed overheads at low production must be expensed, not carried in inventory. FP&A uses these variances to explain plant conversion cost performance in monthly packs.

Core notes

Set-up: standard overhead rates

Choose an activity base (say machine hours) that drives variable overhead. From the budget:

\[ \text{SVOR} = \frac{\text{Budgeted variable OH}}{\text{Budgeted machine hours}}, \qquad \text{SFOR} = \frac{\text{Budgeted fixed OH}}{\text{Budgeted (normal) machine hours}} \]

Standard hours per unit (SH/u) come from engineering standards. For actual output \(Q^{\text{act}}\), standard hours allowed are \(\text{SH} = Q^{\text{act}} \times \text{SH/u}\).

Notation: AH = actual hours, AR = actual variable overhead rate per hour (actual variable OH ÷ AH), SR = standard variable OH rate, BH = budgeted hours.

Variable overhead variances

\[ \text{VOH spending} = (\text{SR} - \text{AR}) \times \text{AH} = \text{SR} \times \text{AH} - \text{Actual VOH} \]

\[ \text{VOH efficiency} = (\text{SH} - \text{AH}) \times \text{SR} \]

\[ \text{Total VOH variance} = \text{SH} \times \text{SR} - \text{Actual VOH} = \text{Flexed VOH (on output)} - \text{Actual VOH} \]

Interpretation: spending captures the price paid per hour of activity (power tariff, consumable prices, and also usage of overhead resources per hour); efficiency captures whether more or fewer machine hours were used than standard for the output — it is really a measure of the efficiency of the activity base, not of the overhead itself.

Note the two flexed budgets for variable overhead: flexed on actual hours (SR × AH) for spending, and on standard hours for actual output (SR × SH) for the total. A flexible budget built only on actual hours misses efficiency.

Fixed overhead variances

\[ \text{FOH spending (expenditure)} = \text{Budgeted FOH} - \text{Actual FOH} \]

\[ \text{FOH volume} = (\text{SH} - \text{BH}) \times \text{SFOR} = \text{Absorbed FOH} - \text{Budgeted FOH} \]

where absorbed FOH = SH × SFOR. The volume variance can be split:

\[ \text{FOH efficiency} = (\text{SH} - \text{AH}) \times \text{SFOR}, \qquad \text{FOH capacity} = (\text{AH} - \text{BH}) \times \text{SFOR} \]

Interpretation: the volume variance is not a cash cost — fixed overhead spending did not change because of volume. It reflects over- or under-absorption: producing less than normal capacity leaves fixed overhead unabsorbed. Under marginal costing there is no FOH volume variance; the entire budgeted fixed overhead is a period cost and only the spending variance exists. That is why the flexible budget does not flex fixed overhead, while the absorption-based report shows a volume variance.

Link to Ind AS 2

Ind AS 2.13 requires fixed production overheads to be allocated on normal capacity. If actual production is abnormally low, unallocated overhead is expensed in the period; if production is abnormally high, the absorption rate is reduced so inventories are not measured above cost. The standard costing FOH volume variance is therefore not deferred in inventory at year-end when it reflects below-normal capacity — budget models should mirror this.

Illustration

Budget for the month: 10,000 units; SH/u 0.5 machine hours → BH 5,000 h. Budgeted variable OH ₹15 lakh (SR = ₹300/h); budgeted fixed OH ₹25 lakh (SFOR = ₹500/h).

Actual: 9,200 units; AH 4,900 h; actual variable OH ₹15.68 lakh; actual fixed OH ₹25.6 lakh.

SH = 9,200 × 0.5 = 4,600 h. AR = 15.68 lakh ÷ 4,900 = ₹320/h.

Variance Computation ₹ lakh
VOH spending (300 − 320) × 4,900 0.98 A
VOH efficiency (4,600 − 4,900) × 300 0.90 A
Total VOH 4,600 × 300 − 15.68 lakh = 13.80 − 15.68 1.88 A
FOH spending 25.0 − 25.6 0.60 A
FOH efficiency (4,600 − 4,900) × 500 1.50 A
FOH capacity (4,900 − 5,000) × 500 0.50 A
FOH volume (efficiency + capacity) (4,600 − 5,000) × 500 2.00 A
Total FOH (absorbed 23.0 − actual 25.6) 2.60 A

Commentary: total overhead variance ₹4.48 lakh A. Of this, ₹2.0 lakh is under-absorption from producing 8% below budget (not a spend issue); ₹1.58 lakh is genuine overspend (₹20/h higher VOH rate and ₹0.6 lakh fixed overspend); ₹0.9 lakh is machine-hour inefficiency (300 extra hours), which also drives ₹1.5 lakh of the FOH volume variance.

Excel structure

Inputs: budget units, SH/u, BH, budget VOH and FOH, actual units, AH, actual VOH and FOH. Calculations as above in one block per month, with checks that spending + efficiency = total VOH variance and spending + volume = total FOH variance.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Choice of base: if variable overhead is driven by batches (set-ups, cleaning in FMCG changeovers), a machine-hour base produces misleading efficiency variances — use activity-based flexing (s06).
  • Three-way vs four-way analysis: some textbooks combine VOH and FOH spending into one spending variance (three-way); management packs often show two-way (controllable vs volume). Choose one and be consistent.
  • Normal vs budgeted capacity: if SFOR uses budgeted rather than normal capacity, the volume variance measures deviation from budget, not from normal — this matters for Ind AS 2 inventory valuation.
  • Seasonal plants: monthly volume variances swing with seasonality even when the annual plan is on track; report YTD and annual projections.
  • Depreciation in FOH changes when capitalisation dates slip (Topic 4 s05), creating spending variances unrelated to plant management.
  • Energy tariffs with time-of-day pricing can show spending variances driven by production scheduling, not purchasing.

Key takeaways

  • VOH spending = (SR − AR) × AH; VOH efficiency = (SH − AH) × SR.
  • FOH spending = budgeted − actual FOH; FOH volume = (SH − BH) × SFOR, splittable into efficiency and capacity.
  • The FOH volume variance is an absorption artefact, not a cash overspend; it does not exist under marginal costing.
  • Ind AS 2.13 normal-capacity absorption means below-normal under-absorption is expensed.
  • Present controllable (spending, efficiency) separately from volume effects in commentary.

Practice Questions

Q1. Four-way overhead analysis [L1]

Budget: 24,000 units, 0.25 labour hours per unit, variable OH ₹21.6 lakh, fixed OH ₹36 lakh. Actual: 22,800 units, 5,900 labour hours, variable OH ₹21.0 lakh, fixed OH ₹36.8 lakh. Compute VOH spending and efficiency, FOH spending and volume (with efficiency and capacity split), and reconcile to total overhead variance.

Q2. Marginal vs absorption reporting [L2]

Using Q1, prepare the overhead section of an operating statement under marginal costing and under absorption costing. Explain why profit differs between the two if inventory levels change, and which presentation you would use for the plant head.

💡 Hint: Under marginal costing, budgeted fixed overhead is a period cost — compare only spending.

Q3. Ind AS 2 year-end implication [L2]

A plant with normal capacity of 1,20,000 machine hours and budgeted fixed overhead of ₹18 crore produced output equivalent to 84,000 standard hours due to a demand slowdown. Closing inventory is 15% of the year's production. Compute the fixed overhead absorbed into closing inventory under Ind AS 2 and the amount expensed, and explain why carrying the full budgeted fixed overhead in inventory would be wrong.

Q4. Choosing the overhead base [L3]

A personal care plant runs 40 SKUs with frequent changeovers. Variable overheads are 60% changeover-related (cleaning, set-up power, QC) and 40% run-time related. The current machine-hour-based flexible budget shows persistent adverse efficiency variances in months with many small batches. Diagnose the issue and propose a revised flexible budget design.
