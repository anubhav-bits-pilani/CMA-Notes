Subtopic note

Flexing Revenue, Variable Costs and Step Costs to Actual Volume

Scope: Flexing revenue, variable costs and step costs to actual volume

Why it matters

The flexed budget is the benchmark for every flexible budget variance; if it is built wrongly, every variance below it is wrong. Flexing is mechanically simple for pure variable costs, but practical budgets contain revenue with GTN deductions, costs with multiple drivers, semi-variable costs and step costs with thresholds. FP&A must flex each line on its own driver, at budgeted rates, with step costs re-evaluated at the actual activity level — then present the result so that business owners accept it as fair.

Core notes

Flexing revenue

At its simplest, flexed revenue = actual volume × budgeted net price:

\[ R^{\text{flex}} = \sum_i Q^{\text{act}}_i \times p^{\text{bud}}_i \]

summed over products \(i\), with \(p^{\text{bud}}\) the budgeted net price after GTN deductions (Topic 2). Points of judgement:

  • Product-level flexing preserves the actual mix at budget prices; flexing on total units at average budget price assumes budget mix. The difference is the sales mix variance — part of the PVM bridge (Week 19).
  • GTN elements: some deductions are proportional to volume (per-case schemes), some to value (% trade discounts), some fixed (listing fees, annual rebates with slabs). Flex each on its driver: a fixed listing fee does not flex; a volume-slab rebate is a step function.
  • Sales volume definition: primary (to distributors) for revenue recognised by the company; do not mix with secondary sales (Topic 2 pitfall).

Flexing variable costs

\[ C^{\text{flex}}_j = v^{\text{bud}}_j \times X^{\text{act}}_j \]

where \(v^{\text{bud}}_j\) is the budgeted variable rate for cost \(j\) per unit of its driver \(X_j\). Each cost should use its own driver — materials on production by SKU (BOM), freight on case-km, commissions on net revenue, power on machine hours. When production and sales differ (inventory change), production costs flex on production volume and selling costs on sales volume.

Flexing semi-variable costs

\[ C^{\text{flex}} = a^{\text{bud}} + b^{\text{bud}} \times X^{\text{act}} \]

Only the variable element flexes; the fixed element stays at budget.

Flexing step costs

Re-evaluate the number of steps required at actual volume:

\[ C^{\text{flex}}_{\text{step}} = c^{\text{bud}} \times \left\lceil \frac{X^{\text{act}}}{k} \right\rceil \]

where \(c^{\text{bud}}\) is budgeted cost per step and \(k\) capacity per step. Judgement points:

  • Timing: a step triggered mid-month (third shift started on the 18th) should be pro-rated.
  • Management decision: steps are often discretionary. If actual volume crosses a threshold but management chose not to add a shift (using overtime instead), the flexed allowance still includes the step — the overtime cost then shows as a spending variance, offset by the step not incurred. Present this explicitly.
  • Hysteresis: once a step is added (e.g. a warehouse lease signed), it may not reverse when volume falls. Flex downwards only if the step can genuinely be shed in the period.

Fixed costs

Fixed costs are not flexed — the flexed budget carries the static amount. Flexing fixed costs with volume is a named pitfall: it gives managers extra allowance in high-volume months for costs that did not need to rise, and penalises them in low-volume months.

Illustration: multi-line flexed budget

Budget: 50,000 cases; actual 58,000 cases (sales = production).

Line Behaviour / driver Budget rates Static (₹ lakh) Flexed (₹ lakh)
Net revenue ₹820 per case — 410.0 475.6
Materials Variable, ₹430 per case — 215.0 249.4
Freight-out Variable, ₹38 per case — 19.0 22.04
Power Semi-variable: ₹4.0 lakh + ₹22 per case — 15.0 16.76
Supervisors Step: ₹0.9 lakh per supervisor; 1 per 12,000 cases 5 steps budget 4.5 4.5
Shift operators Step: ₹3.2 lakh per shift; 1 shift per 20,000 cases 3 shifts 9.6 9.6
Plant overheads Fixed — 30.0 30.0

Supervisors: \(\lceil 58{,}000 \div 12{,}000 \rceil = 5\) — same as budget (\(\lceil 50{,}000 \div 12{,}000 \rceil = 5\)), so no flex. Shifts: \(\lceil 58{,}000 \div 20{,}000 \rceil = 3\) — no change. Had actual volume been 61,000 cases, shifts would step to 4 (₹12.8 lakh) and supervisors to 6 (₹5.4 lakh).

Power flexed = 4.0 + 0.00022 × 58,000 = ₹16.76 lakh.

Flexed contribution after variable and semi-variable costs and steps = 475.6 − 249.4 − 22.04 − 16.76 − 4.5 − 9.6 = ₹173.3 lakh; flexed profit after fixed overheads = ₹143.3 lakh.

Model implementation

A flexing engine needs: (1) a cost behaviour table (line, behaviour type, driver, fixed element, variable rate, step size, step cost); (2) actual driver volumes by month; (3) a single formula that handles all types:

=SWITCH([@Type],
  "Fixed", [@Fixed],
  "Variable", [@Rate]*XLOOKUP([@Driver],tblAct[Driver],tblAct[Vol]),
  "Semi", [@Fixed]+[@Rate]*XLOOKUP([@Driver],tblAct[Driver],tblAct[Vol]),
  "Step", [@StepCost]*ROUNDUP(XLOOKUP([@Driver],tblAct[Driver],tblAct[Vol])/[@StepSize],0))

The same table drives budget (with budget volumes) and flexed (with actual volumes), guaranteeing consistency.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Volume outside the relevant range (e.g. 40% below budget due to a plant fire): flexed allowances lose meaning; report separately and consider a revised budget.
  • Price changes are not flexed: the flexed budget uses budget prices; the price effect is part of the flexible budget variance.
  • Inventory changes under absorption costing: flexed COGS uses standard cost of units sold; production volume variance appears separately (s05).
  • Annual rebate slabs: flex using the projected annual slab, not the month's volume alone, or monthly allowances will swing.
  • Seasonality of fixed costs: a static budget may phase fixed costs unevenly (maintenance in June); the flexed column keeps the phased static amount.
  • Driver data quality: if actual drivers come from a different source than budget drivers (e.g. budget cases from S&OP, actual from billing), reconcile definitions before flexing.

Key takeaways

  • Flex revenue at budgeted net price by product to preserve actual mix.
  • Flex each variable cost on its own driver; only the variable element of semi-variable costs flexes.
  • Re-evaluate step costs at actual volume with ceiling logic; consider timing and management decisions.
  • Never flex fixed costs.
  • Use a single cost behaviour table and formula for budget and flexed columns.

Practice Questions

Q1. Flexed budget with steps [L2]

Using the illustration's rates, prepare the flexed budget at actual volumes of 44,000 cases and 63,000 cases. Identify which lines change step at each level and comment on the flexed profit per case.

Q2. GTN flexing [L2]

Budget net revenue for a month: 2,00,000 cases × ₹600 list = ₹12.0 crore gross; trade discount 8% of gross; per-case scheme ₹15; fixed listing fee ₹18 lakh; volume rebate 1.5% of gross if monthly volume ≥ 2,10,000 cases (budget assumes not achieved). Actual volume 2,25,000 cases. Prepare flexed gross revenue, each deduction and flexed net revenue.

💡 Hint: Each GTN element has its own driver; the rebate is a threshold function.

Q3. Overtime vs shift step [L2]

Actual volume of 61,000 cases (illustration rates) required a fourth shift per the step rule, but management ran three shifts with overtime costing ₹2.6 lakh. Prepare the flexed allowance and actual cost for operators and explain how you would present the variance.

Q4. Flexing engine design [L3]

Design an Excel flexing engine for a plant with 40 cost lines, five drivers (cases, machine hours, batches, kWh, case-km) and monthly actuals from SAP. Specify the tables, the formula logic, data validation, and the checks that confirm budget-at-budget-volume equals the static budget.
