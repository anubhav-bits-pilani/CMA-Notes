Subtopic note

Decomposing the Static Budget Variance: Volume vs Flexible

Scope: Decomposing static budget variance into sales volume variance and flexible budget variance

Why it matters

Every month-end, FP&A answers one question for the CFO: "Why is profit different from budget?" The first split — how much is volume, and how much is everything else — is the most important, because it routes the conversation to the right owner: sales and marketing for volume, operations and procurement for price and efficiency. This decomposition is the opening bar of every EBITDA bridge and the basis of the PVM analysis in Week 19. It is also a staple of the CMA Final cost and management accounting syllabus and of FP&A interviews, where candidates are expected to value the volume variance correctly (at contribution, not price).

Core notes

The identity

\[ \underbrace{\text{Actual} - \text{Static}}_{\text{Static budget variance}} = \underbrace{(\text{Flexed} - \text{Static})}_{\text{Sales volume variance}} + \underbrace{(\text{Actual} - \text{Flexed})}_{\text{Flexible budget variance}} \]

Signs: for profit and revenue, positive = favourable (F); for costs, a higher actual than flexed is adverse (A). State the convention in every report.

Sales volume variance

At the profit level under marginal (variable) costing:

\[ \text{SVV} = (Q^{\text{act}} - Q^{\text{bud}}) \times \text{Budgeted contribution per unit} \]

It captures the profit effect of selling more or fewer units than planned, at budgeted prices and costs. Under absorption costing, it is valued at budgeted (standard) profit per unit — the fixed overhead element is then dealt with through the fixed overhead volume variance (s05).

Line by line: revenue volume effect = \(\Delta Q \times p^{\text{bud}}\); variable cost volume effect = \(\Delta Q \times v^{\text{bud}}\); net = \(\Delta Q \times (p - v)^{\text{bud}}\). Fixed costs have no volume variance. Presenting the revenue line volume effect alone (at selling price) overstates the profit impact — a named pitfall.

For multi-product businesses, the SVV can be split further into sales mix and sales quantity variances:

\[ \text{Mix} = \sum_i (Q^{\text{act}}_i - Q^{\text{act,std mix}}_i) \times c^{\text{bud}}_i, \qquad \text{Quantity} = \sum_i (Q^{\text{act,std mix}}_i - Q^{\text{bud}}_i) \times c^{\text{bud}}_i \]

where \(Q^{\text{act,std mix}}_i\) is total actual quantity allocated in budgeted mix proportions and \(c^{\text{bud}}_i\) budgeted contribution per unit. The quantity variance can be further split into market size and market share variances where market data (Nielsen/NIQ) exist.

Flexible budget variance

\[ \text{FBV} = \text{Actual} - \text{Flexed} \]

At the profit level it combines:

  • Sales price variance: \((p^{\text{act}} - p^{\text{bud}}) \times Q^{\text{act}}\);
  • Variable cost variances: material price and usage, labour rate and efficiency, variable overhead spending and efficiency (s05), freight rate and distance, etc.;
  • Fixed cost spending (expenditure) variance: actual fixed − budgeted fixed.

The FBV is attributable to managers who control prices, rates and efficiency at the actual volume.

Reconciliation statement

A standard operating statement (marginal costing) reconciles budgeted to actual profit:

₹ lakh F / (A)
Budgeted profit X
Sales volume variance (at contribution) ±
= Flexed budget profit X
Sales price variance ±
Material price / usage ±
Labour rate / efficiency ±
Variable overhead spending / efficiency ±
Fixed overhead expenditure ±
= Actual profit X

Illustration

Budget: 80,000 units; price ₹300; variable cost ₹180; fixed costs ₹60 lakh. Actual: 74,000 units; price ₹306; variable costs ₹1.369 crore; fixed costs ₹58.5 lakh.

₹ lakh Static Flexed (74,000) Actual
Revenue 240.0 222.0 226.44
Variable costs 144.0 133.2 136.90
Contribution 96.0 88.8 89.54
Fixed costs 60.0 60.0 58.50
Profit 36.0 28.8 31.04
  • Static budget variance = 31.04 − 36.0 = ₹4.96 lakh A.
  • SVV = (74,000 − 80,000) × ₹120 = ₹7.20 lakh A.
  • FBV = 31.04 − 28.8 = ₹2.24 lakh F, comprising: price (306 − 300) × 74,000 = ₹4.44 lakh F; variable cost 136.90 − 133.2 = ₹3.70 lakh A; fixed cost 60.0 − 58.5 = ₹1.50 lakh F.

Check: −7.20 + 4.44 − 3.70 + 1.50 = −4.96. Commentary: volume shortfall of 7.5% cost ₹7.2 lakh; price realisation recovered ₹4.4 lakh but variable cost overruns (₹5 per unit above standard on actual units) absorbed most of it.

Excel / Power BI

Build the bridge from three measures — Static, Flexed and Actual — with the flexed measure using budget rates × actual volume (s03). In Power BI:

Flexed Contribution = SUMX(VALUES(Product[SKU]), [Actual Units] * ([Budget Price] - [Budget VC per Unit]))
SVV = [Flexed Contribution] - [Budget Contribution]

A waterfall chart (Budget profit → SVV → price → cost variances → Actual profit) is the standard visual.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Mix effects: aggregate SVV on total units at average contribution hides mix; compute by product and sum, or show mix separately.
  • Market vs share: a volume shortfall caused by a market contraction (e.g. weak monsoon reducing rural demand) is not the sales team's fault; split quantity into market size and market share where data permit.
  • Price-volume interaction: a deliberate price increase that reduced volume creates an adverse SVV and a favourable price variance — present them together in commentary.
  • Absorption costing valuation at standard profit gives a different SVV from marginal costing; the difference is fixed overhead per unit × ΔQ, reported in the fixed overhead volume variance (s05).
  • Revenue recognition timing: Ind AS 115 cut-off (dispatch vs delivery) can shift volume between months; check before attributing volume variances.
  • Sign conventions in Excel bridges: costs entered as negatives make "Actual − Budget" consistently F-positive; document it.

Key takeaways

  • Static budget variance = sales volume variance + flexible budget variance.
  • Value the sales volume variance at budgeted contribution per unit (or standard profit under absorption), never at selling price.
  • The flexible budget variance bundles price, rate, efficiency and fixed spending — decompose further for owners.
  • Split SVV into mix and quantity (and market size/share) for multi-product businesses.
  • Always reconcile budgeted to actual profit in an operating statement that adds up.

Practice Questions

Q1. Basic decomposition [L1]

Budget: 25,000 units at ₹1,200, variable cost ₹780, fixed costs ₹48 lakh. Actual: 27,500 units at ₹1,170, variable costs ₹2.20 crore, fixed costs ₹49.5 lakh. Compute the static budget variance, sales volume variance and flexible budget variance (with its components) and prepare an operating statement.

Q2. Mix and quantity [L2]

Two products: A (budget 6,000 units, contribution ₹400) and B (budget 4,000 units, contribution ₹250). Actual: A 5,200, B 5,300. Compute the sales volume variance and split it into mix and quantity variances. Comment on what the sales team achieved.

💡 Hint: First restate total actual units (10,500) in budget mix proportions.

Q3. Market size and share [L2]

Budget assumed a category market of 50 lakh cases with 12% share; actual market was 46 lakh cases with 12.5% share. Budgeted contribution is ₹95 per case. Split the sales quantity variance into market size and market share variances, and explain the commentary for the sales head and the CEO.

Q4. Interview: valuing volume at price [L3]

A colleague's bridge shows "Volume impact ₹18 crore adverse" computed as volume shortfall × budget selling price, and then a large "cost savings ₹11 crore favourable". Explain what is wrong, recompute conceptually how the bridge should look, and describe the consequence for management decisions if left uncorrected.
