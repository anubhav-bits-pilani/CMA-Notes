Subtopic note

Monthly Cash Budget and the 13-Week Direct Cash Flow Forecast

Scope: Monthly cash budget plus 13-week direct cash flow forecast for treasury liquidity

Why it matters

Profit does not pay salaries; cash does. The AOP's monthly cash budget tells the CFO whether the year's plan is fundable — the peak borrowing month, the headroom against bank limits, and when surplus can be deployed. But a monthly budget prepared in January cannot run day-to-day liquidity in September. Treasury therefore runs a 13-week direct cash flow forecast (13WCF): a rolling, week-by-week projection of actual receipts and disbursements, refreshed every week and compared with actuals. Lenders, restructuring advisers and private-equity owners routinely demand a 13WCF; in stressed situations (covenant pressure, a large capex peak, seasonal working capital build before Diwali) it becomes the most important document in the finance function.

Core notes

Two horizons, two purposes

Feature Monthly cash budget (AOP) 13-week cash flow forecast
Horizon 12 months (budget year) Rolling 13 weeks (one quarter)
Granularity Month Week (some treasuries run daily for weeks 1–2)

Monthly cash budget structure

The budget cash schedule should be presented in a direct format even though it is derived from accrual budgets:

Line Source
Opening cash Prior month closing
Receipts from customers (incl. GST) Collections schedule (c02): sales × collection profile, or AR roll-forward
Other receipts (scrap, interest, asset sale, subsidy) Respective schedules
Payments to suppliers (incl. GST) Purchases × payment profile, or AP roll-forward (c03)
Payroll (net salary) Payroll budget net of employee PF/ESI/TDS/PT
Statutory payments: PF, ESI, TDS, PT, GST net, advance tax Statutory calendar (s07)
Opex payments (rent, utilities, marketing) Opex budget with payment terms
Capex payments Capex cash schedule (s04)
Interest and loan repayments Debt schedule
Dividends Board/AGM calendar
Net cash flow before financing Sum
Revolver draw / (repay) Minimum cash logic (c01)
Closing cash Opening + net flow + financing

Because the monthly budget is derived from the P&L and balance sheet, it must tie: closing cash in the cash budget = cash in the budgeted balance sheet = closing cash in the budgeted indirect cash flow statement (Topic 5).

13-week forecast mechanics

Receipts are built from the AR ledger, not from sales:

  • week-by-week expected collection of each open invoice, by customer, using the due date adjusted for each customer's historical days-late behaviour;
  • plus collections from forecast sales invoiced in the next few weeks (for short-credit customers, modern trade, exports with LC);
  • plus known non-trade receipts (GST refund for exporters, insurance claims, inter-company receipts).

Disbursements are built from the AP ledger and known calendars:

  • open supplier invoices by due date, adjusted for the payment-run calendar (e.g. payment runs on Tuesday and Friday) and policy (MSME suppliers within 45 days — see c03);
  • payroll on fixed dates (salary on the last working day or 1st; contractual labour weekly);
  • statutory payments on statutory dates (GST 20th, TDS 7th, PF/ESI 15th, advance tax);
  • capex per milestone and LC maturity schedule;
  • debt service per repayment schedule;
  • rent, utilities, freight per vendor terms.

Ending balance per week is compared with a minimum operating cash and available undrawn limits to compute liquidity headroom:

\[ \text{Headroom}_w = \text{Closing cash}_w - \text{Minimum cash} + \text{Undrawn committed limits}_w \]

where undrawn limits are bounded by drawing power, not just sanctioned limit (see s08).

Variance tracking and accuracy

Each week, last week's forecast is compared with actual by line:

Line Forecast (week 32) Actual Variance Reason
Collections — modern trade ₹14.2 crore ₹11.6 crore (2.6) Key account delayed payment run
Collections — distributors ₹22.0 crore ₹22.9 crore 0.9 Festive pre-loading
Supplier payments ₹(28.5) crore ₹(27.8) crore 0.7 Timing
Statutory ₹(6.1) crore ₹(6.1) crore — —

Two variance types matter: timing (cash moves to next week) and permanent (amount changes). A standard accuracy KPI is forecast accuracy of net cash flow over weeks 1–4, e.g. within ±5% or ±₹X crore. Persistent bias (collections always overestimated) indicates a modelling problem, typically using due dates instead of actual payment behaviour.

Reconciling 13-week and monthly views

At each month-end, the sum of the weeks in the month should be compared with the monthly budget/forecast; differences are explained as timing (week boundaries, payment run dates) or genuine changes. Weeks rarely align with months — use a calendar table with a week-to-month mapping, and decide whether weeks straddling month-end are split by days.

=SUMIFS(tblCash[Amount], tblCash[Category], $A8, tblCash[WeekStart], ">="&F$4, tblCash[WeekStart], "<"&F$4+7)

In Power BI, the same model uses a date table with WeekStart and MonthEnd columns so forecast and actual can be sliced by either.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Month-end loading. FMCG primary sales are heavily loaded in the last week of each month and quarter; collections cluster in the first week of the next month. The intra-month trough is real and only weekly forecasting sees it.
  • Bank holidays and weekends shift statutory and salary payments; GST and TDS due dates falling on holidays are generally extended to the next working day only if notified — plan to pay before.
  • Restricted cash (LC margins, escrow, unpaid dividend accounts under Sec 124) must be excluded from available cash.
  • Cash pooling and multiple banks. A consolidated forecast can hide a shortfall in one entity; forecast by entity and bank account, then aggregate.
  • Forecast ownership. If AR and AP teams do not own their lines, the 13WCF degenerates into treasury guesswork. Assign line owners and track their accuracy.
  • Don't derive weekly from P&L. Weekly forecasts built from accrual P&L and DSO/DPO assumptions (rather than ledgers and calendars) miss timing — one of the topic pitfalls.

Key takeaways

  • The monthly cash budget sizes the annual funding plan; the 13WCF runs operational liquidity.
  • Present both in direct format: receipts and payments by category, then financing.
  • Build the 13WCF from AR/AP ledgers, payment calendars and statutory dates — not from P&L accruals.
  • Measure headroom as closing cash less minimum cash plus undrawn limits capped by drawing power.
  • Track weekly forecast vs actual, separate timing from permanent variances and monitor bias.
  • Reconcile weekly to monthly views with a calendar table.

Practice Questions

Q1. Building a 4-week forecast [L2]

Opening cash ₹3.5 crore; minimum cash ₹2.0 crore. Open AR due: ₹9 crore week 1, ₹7 crore week 2, ₹11 crore week 3, ₹6 crore week 4; historical behaviour shows 70% collected in the due week and 30% one week late. Supplier payments run every Friday: ₹6.5 crore, ₹8.0 crore, ₹7.0 crore, ₹9.5 crore. TDS ₹0.9 crore (7th, week 1), PF/ESI ₹1.1 crore (15th, week 2), GST ₹3.4 crore (20th, week 3), salaries ₹4.2 crore (week 4). Ignore late collections from before week 1. Prepare the forecast and compute the required CC draw each week.

Q2. Forecast accuracy diagnosis [L2]

Over 10 weeks, forecast collections exceeded actual collections in 9 weeks, by an average of 11%; payments were forecast within ±2%. Explain likely causes, how you would test them in the AR data, and the changes to the forecasting method.

💡 Hint: Compare due-date-based forecasting with customer-level historical payment behaviour.

Q3. Reconciling weekly to monthly [L2]

The September monthly forecast shows net operating cash flow of ₹4.0 crore. The sum of weeks starting 1, 8, 15, 22 and 29 September in the 13WCF shows ₹1.2 crore. List the possible reconciling items and describe a calendar-table design in Excel or Power BI that would make this reconciliation automatic.

Q4. Designing a 13WCF for a lender [L3]

A consumer goods company in covenant discussions with its bank is asked to submit a weekly 13WCF. Specify the template (rows, columns, entity split), data sources, owners, refresh calendar, variance commentary format and the headroom metric. Explain how you would keep it consistent with the monthly AOP cash budget.

Q5. Interview: monthly vs weekly cash [L1]

"Why would a company with a perfectly good monthly cash budget also need a 13-week cash forecast?" Give a concise answer with one FMCG-specific example.
