Subtopic note

Milestone-Based Capex Tranches and Post-Investment Review

Scope: Tranche-based release tied to milestones (design, order placement, delivery, commissioning) and post-investment review

Why it matters

Approving a ₹150 crore greenfield project as a single cheque gives management no checkpoint once money starts flowing. Tranche-based release breaks the approved amount into stages that unlock only when the previous milestone is achieved and the business case is still valid. This is how boards and private-equity owners keep optionality on large projects, and how FP&A converts an approved AR into a realistic monthly cash and commitment profile. The post-investment review (PIR) closes the loop: it compares delivered cost, timing and returns with what the AR promised, and its findings calibrate future approvals. Weak PIR discipline is one of the most common observations in internal audit reports on capex.

Core notes

Why tranche the release

  • Option value. Each milestone is a decision point to continue, re-scope, pause or abandon. Abandoning after design costs a few percent of the project; abandoning after equipment delivery costs most of it.
  • Information arrives over time. Firm vendor prices replace estimates at order placement; site conditions are known after civil works begin.
  • Cash discipline. Treasury can plan funding by tranche rather than holding the full amount idle.
  • Accountability. The project manager must demonstrate milestone completion with evidence before more money is released.

Typical milestone structure

Tranche Milestone (gate) Typical % of project cost released Evidence required to unlock next tranche
T0 Concept / feasibility approval 1–3% (studies, consultants) Feasibility report, preliminary estimate (±30%)
T1 Design freeze (basic + detailed engineering) 3–7% Approved layout, equipment specs, refined estimate (±10%), regulatory consents applied
T2 Order placement (PO/contract award) 40–60% committed (cash: advances only) Competitive bids, negotiated contracts, LC opened, updated AR if estimate moved > tolerance
T3 Delivery / erection 25–40% Goods receipt, inspection reports, civil works completion certificates
T4 Commissioning / trial runs and handover 5–10% (incl. retention release later) Performance guarantee test (PG test), commissioning certificate, capitalisation request
T5 Post-investment review — PIR report at 12 and 24 months after commissioning

Two different quantities are tranched: the commitment authority (the right to sign POs) and the cash. Order placement commits most of the project cost even though cash at that point is only the advance. FP&A must track both — commitments drive the capital commitments disclosure and the "remaining to spend" view; cash drives the cash budget.

Gate review mechanics

At each gate, the project steering committee (sponsor, project manager, plant finance, engineering) presents:

  1. Physical progress vs plan (% complete by work package, S-curve).
  2. Cost status: approved budget, committed, actual spent, estimate to complete (ETC), estimate at completion (EAC).
  3. Schedule status: milestone slippage and impact on capitalisation date.
  4. Business case refresh: has volume, price, input cost or FX moved enough to change NPV?
  5. Risks: open issues, change orders, regulatory clearances pending.

\[ \text{EAC} = \text{Actual cost to date} + \text{ETC} \]

\[ \text{Variance at completion} = \text{Approved budget} - \text{EAC} \]

Earned-value metrics can be used for large projects: cost performance index \(\text{CPI} = \text{EV} \div \text{AC}\) and schedule performance index \(\text{SPI} = \text{EV} \div \text{PV}\), where EV is the budgeted cost of work performed, AC actual cost and PV budgeted cost of work scheduled. An alternative forecast is \(\text{EAC} = \text{BAC} \div \text{CPI}\) (BAC = budget at completion).

If EAC exceeds the approved amount by more than the DoA tolerance, a supplementary AR is required before the next tranche.

Modelling tranches in the budget

In the capex schedule, each project row carries milestone dates and release percentages; the budget model converts them into monthly commitments and (via the payment profile in s04) cash.

=SUMPRODUCT((tblMilestone[Project]=$A7) * (EOMONTH(tblMilestone[Date],0)=F$5) * tblMilestone[Pct]) * $C7

where $C7 is the approved project cost and F$5 is the month-end date in the timeline header. Keep milestone dates as inputs so delays can be modelled by shifting a single date.

Post-investment review (PIR)

A PIR (post-completion audit) compares actual outcomes with the approved AR. Typical scope:

  • Cost: final cost vs AR, by component; reasons for overrun (scope change, FX, price escalation, delay).
  • Time: commissioning date vs plan; months of delay and their cost (interest during construction, lost contribution).
  • Performance: capacity achieved vs rated, ramp-up curve vs plan, yields, utilisation.
  • Returns: re-computed IRR/NPV using actuals to date plus updated forecast; payback.
  • Lessons: estimation bias, vendor performance, approval process issues.

Timing: an initial review 6–12 months after commissioning (cost and time) and a returns review at 24–36 months once volumes stabilise. PIR results are reported to the capex committee and, for large projects, the audit committee.

Good practice is to build a PIR database: across projects, average overrun and volume shortfall become calibration factors applied to new ARs ("reference class forecasting").

Illustration

A ₹60 crore new soap line approved in May 2026:

Milestone Planned date Commitment released Cash released (approx.)
Design freeze Jul 2026 ₹3.0 crore ₹2.4 crore
Order placement Sep 2026 ₹42.0 crore ₹8.4 crore (20% advance)
Delivery and erection Jan 2027 ₹12.0 crore ₹28.0 crore
Commissioning Apr 2027 ₹3.0 crore ₹15.0 crore
Retention release Apr 2028 — ₹6.2 crore

At the order placement gate, bids come in at ₹47 crore against ₹42 crore estimate; EAC becomes ₹65 crore (+8.3%). With a 10% tolerance, no supplementary AR is required, but the committee re-tests NPV and documents the variance. Had bids been ₹50 crore (EAC ₹68 crore, +13%), the tranche would be frozen pending a supplementary AR.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Long-lead items (imported presses, boilers) may need orders before design freeze to hold the schedule. Allow an early-release sub-tranche with explicit approval, recognising cancellation charges.
  • Fast-track EPC contracts combine design and build; milestones then sit in the EPC payment schedule rather than internal gates — finance must still insist on internal gate reviews.
  • Sunk-cost bias. At later gates, teams argue "we have already spent ₹30 crore". The gate test should use remaining cost vs remaining value.
  • Abandonment accounting. If a project is abandoned, CWIP is tested for impairment (Ind AS 36) and written down; advances may need provisioning if irrecoverable.
  • PIR independence. A PIR written by the project sponsor tends to explain away overruns. Assign internal audit or a finance team not involved in the project.
  • Incentives. Linking project managers' variable pay partly to PIR outcomes (cost, time, ramp-up) reduces optimism bias at the AR stage.

Key takeaways

  • Release capex in tranches tied to design, order placement, delivery and commissioning milestones.
  • Track commitment and cash separately; order placement commits most of the cost before most of the cash leaves.
  • Each gate refreshes EAC and the business case; overruns beyond tolerance need a supplementary AR before the next tranche.
  • Model milestone dates as inputs so delays flow into commitments, cash and capitalisation.
  • PIRs at 6–12 and 24–36 months close the loop and calibrate future ARs.

Practice Questions

Q1. Commitment vs cash profile [L1]

A ₹25 crore project has: design ₹1 crore (paid on completion, Aug), order placement Oct with 25% advance on a ₹20 crore equipment PO, balance of equipment paid 65% on delivery (Feb) and 10% retention released 12 months after commissioning (commissioning May), and ₹4 crore of civil works paid as incurred evenly Nov–Mar. Prepare a monthly table of commitments and cash for April to March of the budget year.

Q2. EAC and supplementary AR [L2]

An approved ₹80 crore project has actual cost to date ₹32 crore, earned value ₹28 crore and planned value ₹35 crore. ETC per engineering is ₹54 crore. The DoA tolerance is 10% of approved cost. Compute EAC by both the bottom-up and CPI methods, CPI and SPI, and state whether the next tranche can be released.

💡 Hint: Compare both EAC estimates with ₹88 crore; explain which one you would put in front of the committee and why.

Q3. Modelling milestone delays [L2]

Describe how you would design an Excel capex schedule so that a three-month slip in "delivery" automatically moves the delivery payment, the commissioning payment, the capitalisation date and the start of depreciation. Specify the input fields, the lookup/aggregation formulas and the checks.

Q4. Writing a PIR [L3]

A ₹45 crore line approved with IRR 19%, commissioning June 2025 and Year-1 utilisation 70% was actually commissioned in November 2025 at a cost of ₹52 crore and reached 48% utilisation in its first six months. Draft the structure of the PIR, list the data you need, and identify three process changes you would recommend for future ARs.

Q5. Gate decision under sunk cost [L3]

At the delivery gate of a ₹100 crore project, ₹55 crore has been spent. Revised remaining cost is ₹60 crore and the revised present value of future cash inflows is ₹70 crore. The sponsor argues for continuing because "abandoning wastes ₹55 crore". Explain the correct decision framework and what else you would consider (salvage value, cancellation charges, strategic options).
