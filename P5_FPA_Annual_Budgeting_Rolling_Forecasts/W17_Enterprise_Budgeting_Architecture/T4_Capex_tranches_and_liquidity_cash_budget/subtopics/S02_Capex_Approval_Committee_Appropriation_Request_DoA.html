Subtopic note

Capex Approval: Committee, Appropriation Request, DoA and Gates

Scope: Capex approval: capex committee, appropriation request (AR), DoA thresholds, hurdle rate and payback gates

Why it matters

Inclusion of a project in the AOP is not approval to spend. In most Indian corporates the AOP carries a capex "envelope" and a list of identified projects, but money is committed only when an appropriation request (AR) — also called a capital expenditure request (CER) or capex proposal — is approved by the right authority. FP&A sits in the middle: it validates the financial case, checks the project fits the envelope, tracks approved-vs-budgeted capex, and reports commitments to the CFO and audit committee. Interviewers for FP&A and business finance roles frequently ask candidates to walk through a capex approval cycle and the metrics used at each gate.

Core notes

Two-stage logic: budget inclusion vs appropriation

Stage What is approved Who Output
AOP inclusion Envelope and indicative project list Board (as part of AOP) Capex budget by project/pool, phased by month
Appropriation Specific project, scope, cost, timeline Per DoA (plant head → CEO → capex committee → Board) Approved AR with number, commitment authority
Release / tranche Spending against milestones Project sponsor + finance PO release, tranche unlocking (see s03)

A project in the budget without an approved AR cannot raise POs. A project not in the budget (unbudgeted capex) needs either reallocation within the envelope or a higher approval level — commonly one level above the normal DoA.

The capex committee

A capex committee (sometimes the Investment Committee or Capital Allocation Committee) is a management body — typically CEO, CFO, COO, head of strategy and the relevant BU head — that reviews ARs above a threshold. Its role:

  • check strategic fit and alignment with the LRP;
  • challenge the assumptions (volume, price, capacity utilisation, capex cost estimates);
  • confirm the hurdle and payback gates are met, or document why a strategic exception is accepted;
  • rank competing projects when the envelope is binding;
  • recommend to the Board projects above the Board threshold.

Under the Companies Act 2013, Sec 179(3) reserves certain powers to the Board by resolution at meetings, including to borrow money, invest the company's funds and approve financial statements; large capex funded by borrowing therefore reaches the Board. Sec 180(1)(c) requires a special resolution of shareholders for borrowing beyond paid-up capital plus free reserves and securities premium. For listed companies, the Audit Committee under SEBI LODR Reg 18 / Part C of Schedule II reviews utilisation of funds and related party transactions, which matters when capex is placed with group companies.

The appropriation request (AR)

A complete AR contains:

  1. Project description and rationale — problem, options considered (including do-nothing), recommended option.
  2. Classification — maintenance, growth, regulatory/ESG (see s01).
  3. Cost estimate — by component (civil, plant & machinery, electricals, installation, IT, pre-operative expenses, contingency), with quotations or engineering estimates; basis of FX rate for imports; GST and customs duty treatment.
  4. Cash phasing — advances, milestones, retention (see s04).
  5. Financial evaluation — incremental cash flows, NPV at the hurdle rate, IRR, payback, sensitivity (volume ±10%, capex +15%, delay 6 months).
  6. Funding — internal accruals, term loan, ECB, subsidy (state industrial policy incentives, PLI scheme).
  7. Risks and mitigation, regulatory approvals needed (environmental clearance, CTE/CTO from PCB).
  8. Timeline and capitalisation date, P&L impact (depreciation, interest, opex).
  9. Post-investment review plan — KPIs and review dates.
  10. Approvals block — signatures per DoA.

DoA thresholds

A typical DoA matrix for capex:

AR value (₹) Budgeted capex Unbudgeted capex
Up to 25 lakh Plant head + plant finance BU head
25 lakh – 2 crore BU head + BU CFO CEO
2 crore – 25 crore Capex committee Capex committee + CEO
Above 25 crore Board (on committee recommendation) Board

Design principles:

  • thresholds apply to the total project cost, not per PO, to stop splitting;
  • overruns above a tolerance (e.g. 10% or ₹50 lakh, whichever is lower) require a supplementary AR at the level appropriate to the revised total;
  • re-scoping or changing the location of a project is a new approval;
  • finance concurrence (not just business approval) is mandatory at every level.

Hurdle rate gate

The hurdle rate is the minimum acceptable return. Usually:

\[ \text{Hurdle rate} = \text{WACC} + \text{Project risk premium} \]

where WACC is the company's weighted average cost of capital and the risk premium reflects project-specific risk (new geography, new technology). Indian consumer goods companies typically run WACC around 11–13% in nominal rupee terms; hurdle rates of 14–18% for expansion and 12–14% for cost-reduction projects are common. Regulatory capex is usually exempt from the hurdle (it is a licence-to-operate cost).

Decision rules: accept if NPV at the hurdle rate > 0, equivalently IRR > hurdle (for conventional cash flows). Mechanics of NPV, IRR and capital rationing are covered in Week 21.

Payback gate

Payback is the time for cumulative undiscounted (or discounted) cash flows to recover the investment. It is a liquidity and risk gate, not a value measure — it ignores cash after payback. Many companies apply payback ceilings by class: cost-reduction projects < 3 years, debottlenecking < 4 years, greenfield < 6 years.

Excel for simple payback with annual cash flows in C5:L5 and initial outlay in B5 (negative), cumulative in row 6:

=XMATCH(TRUE, C6:L6>=0) - 1 + (-INDEX(C6:L6, XMATCH(TRUE, C6:L6>=0)-1) / INDEX(C5:L5, XMATCH(TRUE, C6:L6>=0)))

(Guard the case where payback falls in year 1 separately.)

Illustration

AR for a ₹18 crore automated packing line:

Item Value
Capex (incl. installation, excl. creditable GST) ₹18.0 crore
Annual post-tax cash savings ₹4.6 crore for 10 years
Hurdle rate 14%
NPV at 14% \(4.6 \times 5.216 - 18.0 = ₹6.0\) crore approx.
Simple payback \(18.0 \div 4.6 \approx 3.9\) years
Company payback ceiling for automation 4 years

DoA band ₹2–25 crore → capex committee. The project passes both gates, but the payback is close to the ceiling — the committee asks for a sensitivity: if savings are 15% lower (₹3.91 crore), payback rises to 4.6 years and the project fails the payback gate while still having positive NPV. This is the classic tension: payback rejects some value-creating projects. The committee may accept with a documented exception.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Strategic overrides. Projects that fail gates may be approved as strategic (entering a category, defending share). Require a written rationale and explicit Board visibility so "strategic" does not become a loophole.
  • Optimism bias. Sponsors overstate volumes and understate cost. Benchmark against post-investment reviews of past projects; some firms apply a standard capex contingency (5–10%) and a volume haircut.
  • GST and duty in the AR. Creditable IGST/CGST/SGST on capital goods is not project cost, but it is a cash outflow until set off. Basic customs duty is not creditable and is capitalised. The AR must show both cost and cash views.
  • Inflation consistency. Use nominal cash flows with a nominal hurdle, or real with real — never mix.
  • Capex for subsidiaries. Funding via loans to a subsidiary triggers Sec 186 limits (loans/investments beyond 60% of paid-up capital, free reserves and securities premium or 100% of free reserves and securities premium, whichever is higher, need a special resolution).
  • Commitments disclosure. Approved, contracted but unexecuted capital commitments must be disclosed (Schedule III, and Ind AS 16.74(c)); the AR register is the source.

Key takeaways

  • AOP inclusion sets the envelope; an approved AR authorises commitment.
  • DoA thresholds apply to total project cost, with supplementary ARs for overruns and higher levels for unbudgeted spend.
  • The AR must show cost (capitalisable) and cash (incl. GST, advances) views, plus sensitivities.
  • Hurdle rate tests value; payback tests liquidity and risk — they can conflict.
  • Strategic exceptions need documentation and Board visibility.
  • The AR register feeds capital commitment disclosures and capex tracking.

Practice Questions

Q1. Routing an AR through DoA [L1]

Using the DoA matrix in these notes, state the approving authority for: (a) a budgeted ₹1.6 crore forklift fleet; (b) an unbudgeted ₹1.6 crore cold room; (c) a budgeted ₹22 crore project whose revised estimate is now ₹27 crore; (d) ten identical ₹20 lakh moulds ordered for the same new SKU.

Q2. Gate evaluation with conflicting results [L2]

A ₹40 crore capacity expansion yields post-tax cash flows of ₹6 crore in year 1, ₹9 crore in years 2–3, and ₹11 crore in years 4–10. Hurdle rate 15%; payback ceiling for greenfield/brownfield expansion 5 years. Compute (or set up in Excel) NPV and simple payback, and recommend what the capex committee should do if the two gates disagree.

💡 Hint: Build cumulative cash flow first; for NPV, discount each year rather than using a single annuity factor.

Q3. Drafting AR sensitivities [L2]

For the ₹18 crore packing line illustration, list the sensitivities you would include in the AR and build a two-way data table specification (savings −20% to +10% against capex −5% to +20%) that outputs NPV. Describe the Excel layout and the cell the table must reference.

Q4. Designing a capex DoA for a mid-sized group [L3]

A ₹1,200 crore revenue NSE-listed FMCG company with three plants and one subsidiary asks you to draft its capex DoA. Specify thresholds, budgeted vs unbudgeted rules, overrun tolerances, finance concurrence, Board/committee roles, and the Companies Act / LODR touchpoints you would reference.

Q5. Interview: "Why not just use NPV?" [L3]

An interviewer asks: "If NPV is the theoretically correct rule, why do Indian companies still impose payback gates and capex envelopes?" Prepare a structured 2-minute answer covering liquidity, risk, estimation error, capital rationing and managerial incentives.
