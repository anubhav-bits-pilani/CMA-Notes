Roadmap topic: CAPEX & Cash Budget Integration: Approving capital expenditure tranches, phasing cash outflows, and building a weekly/monthly Liquidity & Treasury Cash Budget

Overview

This topic connects the two budget components that most directly determine whether an AOP is fundable: the capital expenditure plan and the cash budget. The capex plan decides how much capacity, compliance and productivity the business buys; the cash budget decides whether the company can pay for it while also funding seasonal working capital, salaries, statutory dues and debt service. In Indian mid-corporates these two plans are often built by different teams — projects/engineering for capex, accounts or treasury for cash — and the integration point is exactly where FP&A adds value: converting approved capex into a realistic monthly cash profile, mapping statutory dates into the calendar, and testing the result against bank limits, drawing power and covenants.

The learning sequence runs in three blocks. Capex governance (s01–s03): classify capex by purpose and approval type, route it through the capex committee and DoA via appropriation requests with hurdle and payback gates, and release money in tranches tied to milestones, closing the loop with post-investment reviews. Capex accounting-to-cash (s04–s05): phase cash for advances, milestones, retention and import LCs separately from CWIP accrual, and drive depreciation and borrowing-cost capitalisation from realistic capitalisation dates under Schedule II and Ind AS 23. Liquidity (s06–s08): build the monthly cash budget and the rolling 13-week direct forecast, populate them with the Indian statutory payment calendar, and fund deficits through CC/OD limits constrained by drawing power, a minimum cash buffer and covenants.

The key concepts are the formulas that make this integration mechanical and auditable: the cash roll-forward with revolver logic (c01), collections and supplier payment roll-forwards including GST and the MSME 45-day rule (c02, c03), the advance tax schedule (c04), drawing power (c05) and the cash capex reconciliation (c06). For FP&A and treasury interviews, being able to walk from an approved ₹60 crore project to its month-by-month cash, depreciation and borrowing impact — and to say whether the bank line can carry it in October — is a strong differentiator.

Related concepts to study

NPV, IRR, payback and capital rationing (Week 21)

→ Week 21: NPV, IRR, payback and capital rationing for capex approval

The capex approval gates in s02 assume you can compute NPV, IRR and payback correctly. Week 21 covers the mechanics in depth: incremental cash flows (including working capital investment and terminal value), tax shields on depreciation, the treatment of sunk and allocated costs, multiple IRRs for non-conventional flows, MIRR, and the profitability index used for ranking under capital rationing. When the capex envelope is binding, projects should be ranked by NPV per rupee of constrained capital, not by IRR. The tranche and PIR disciplines in s03 then test whether the approved cash flows materialised.

Debt schedule, revolver and cash sweep (Phase 4)

→ Phase 4: debt schedule, revolver logic and cash sweep in 3-statement models

The revolver logic in c01 is a simplified version of the debt schedule built in Phase 4: term loans with scheduled amortisation, a revolver that draws to maintain minimum cash, and an optional cash sweep that applies excess cash to prepay debt in a defined priority. In 3-statement models, interest on average balances creates circularity, typically handled with a circuit breaker toggle. The budget uses the same architecture at monthly granularity, with the added constraint of drawing power — a borrowing base that leveraged finance models also apply.

Treasury: pooling, liquid funds and FX hedging

Treasury: cash pooling, sweep accounts, short-term deployment in liquid funds, FX hedging of import capex

Cash pooling concentrates balances across accounts or group entities: physical pooling (zero balancing) moves cash; notional pooling offsets balances for interest without moving them (restricted in India, and intra-group lending must respect Sec 185/186 of the Companies Act). Sweep accounts auto-convert surplus into FDs. Surplus is commonly deployed in overnight or liquid mutual funds (SEBI-regulated, generally T+1 redemption, graded exit load in the first week for liquid funds) within an investment policy specifying credit quality and concentration limits. Import capex in USD/EUR is hedged with forwards or options under RBI's FEMA hedging framework; under Ind AS 109 a hedge of a forecast PPE purchase can be a cash flow hedge with a basis adjustment to the asset's cost.

Ind AS 16 components and CWIP ageing

Ind AS 16 component accounting and CWIP ageing disclosure (Schedule III)

Ind AS 16.43 requires each part of PPE with a cost significant to the total to be depreciated separately — a furnace's refractory lining, an aircraft engine, a building's lifts. Schedule II reinforces component accounting. For budgeting, components drive both depreciation and the timing of future replacement capex (which is capitalised with the old component derecognised). Schedule III (2021 amendments) requires CWIP ageing in buckets of less than 1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years and more than 3 years, separately for projects in progress and temporarily suspended, and a completion schedule for projects overdue or exceeding original cost. Realistic capitalisation dates in the budget keep these disclosures credible.

Ind AS 7 direct vs indirect presentation

Ind AS 7 direct vs indirect cash flow presentation

Ind AS 7.18 permits operating cash flows under the direct method (gross receipts and payments) or the indirect method (profit adjusted for non-cash items and working capital changes); Ind AS 7.19 encourages the direct method. Most Indian listed companies report indirect. Treasury forecasts, however, are inherently direct — receipts from customers, payments to suppliers, salaries, taxes. The budget model should produce a direct cash budget for management and an indirect cash flow statement for the budgeted financial statements, and prove they reconcile to the same change in cash. Classification choices (interest paid, CC as cash equivalent under Ind AS 7.8) must match the statutory policy.

Common pitfalls

Capex cash phased equal to capitalisation

Phasing capex cash equal to capitalisation, ignoring advances, retention and credit terms

The mistake: the cash budget's capex line copies the fixed asset additions or, worse, the capitalisation schedule. People do it because the projects team provides one capex number by month, and building payment profiles takes effort. The consequences are wrong peak borrowing months, missed advance payments, and retention outflows from prior-year projects omitted entirely. Correct treatment: model each project's payment terms (advance %, milestones, retention and release lag, LC tenor, duty timing) separately from CWIP accrual and reconcile using the cash capex formula (c06). Detection in review: check that capex creditors and capital advances move in the budgeted balance sheet; if they are flat while capex is large, cash has been equated to accrual.

Receipts and payments budgeted ex-GST

Budgeting receipts and payments ex-GST, understating gross cash flows and GST outflow timing

The mistake: collections are derived from net revenue and supplier payments from purchases at ex-GST values, and the GST payment line is missing or netted away. It happens because P&L budgets are ex-GST and the cash budget is derived from them. Result: gross flows are understated by 12–28%, the 20th-of-month GST payment spike disappears, and the receivables and payables balances in the budgeted balance sheet do not reconcile with DSO/DPO on invoiced values. Correct treatment: gross up credit sales and purchases by the applicable GST rates for AR/AP and cash, and add a GST cash line (output tax − ITC + RCM) paid the following month, by GSTIN. Detection: compare budgeted AR/AP with gross-of-tax invoice values, and look for a GST payment line in the cash budget.

Weekly forecast built from P&L accruals

Weekly cash forecast built from P&L accruals rather than direct receipts/payments

The mistake: the 13-week forecast is created by dividing the monthly P&L by weeks and applying DSO/DPO. It is quick, but it ignores actual invoice due dates, customer payment behaviour, payment-run calendars, salary dates and statutory dates — exactly the timing treasury needs. Correct treatment: build receipts from the open AR ledger (adjusted for customer-specific lateness) plus near-term invoicing, and disbursements from the AP ledger, payroll calendar, statutory calendar, LC maturities and debt schedule. Detection: weekly forecasts that are smooth, with no spikes around the 7th, 15th, 20th and month-end, and persistent forecast-vs-actual bias, signal an accrual-based build.

No minimum cash or revolver logic

Omitting minimum cash balance and revolver logic, producing negative cash in the budget

The mistake: the cash budget simply rolls forward and shows negative cash in deficit months, sometimes with a note "to be funded". It happens when the cash budget is treated as an output rather than a funding plan. Negative cash is not a possible state: in reality the company draws on CC, delays payments or fails. Correct treatment: include a minimum cash input, a revolver with draw/repay logic capped at the lower of sanctioned limit and drawing power, interest on the revolver, and an explicit funding-gap check (c01, c05). Detection: any negative closing cash, or a cash balance below the policy minimum with undrawn limits, or revolver balances exceeding drawing power.

Practice Questions

Q1. Integrated capex-to-cash case [L3]

A consumer goods company plans a packing hall expansion for FY 2026-27 with these components: civil works ₹12 crore (GST 18%, ITC blocked under Sec 17(5)(d) as building), imported machinery USD 3.0 million (budget rate ₹86/USD, 180-day usance LC, bill of lading 15 August 2026, BCD 7.5%, SWS 10% of BCD, IGST 18% creditable), domestic electricals ₹4 crore (GST 18%, 20% advance in June, 70% on delivery in October, 10% retention released 12 months after commissioning). Commissioning is planned for 1 January 2027; the project is funded by a specific ₹30 crore term loan at 9.25% drawn 1 June 2026. Civil works are billed evenly June–November with 5% retention released March 2028. Required: (a) monthly CWIP additions and cash outflows for April 2026–March 2027; (b) capital advances and capex creditors at 31 March 2027; (c) borrowing costs to be capitalised (state whether the asset is a qualifying asset and your assumptions); (d) FY 2026-27 depreciation at a 15-year life and 5% residual for machinery and 30 years for the building; (e) cash capex using the c06 reconciliation.

Q2. Funding the festive peak [L3]

For October–December, pre-financing operating cash flows are −₹14 crore, −₹22 crore and +₹9 crore, and the Q1 project above adds the capex payments you computed. Opening cash on 1 October is ₹6 crore; minimum cash ₹5 crore; CC sanctioned ₹60 crore with ₹35 crore drawn. Drawing power (based on the previous month-end statement) is ₹58 crore, ₹54 crore and ₹63 crore. Advance tax of ₹11 crore is due on 15 December and is not yet in the pre-financing numbers. Required: (a) monthly revolver schedule with funding-gap check; (b) the week-by-week view you would ask treasury for in October and why; (c) three management actions if a gap appears, with their cost and covenant implications.

💡 Hint: Check both limits each month; the binding one changes as drawing power moves.

Q3. Reviewing a draft AOP cash budget [L2]

You are reviewing a plant-level cash budget for an NSE-listed FMCG company. Observations: collections equal net revenue each month; supplier payments equal purchases; there is no GST line; capex cash equals capitalisation; payroll is shown gross of employee PF and TDS; advance tax is spread equally over 12 months; cash is negative in September and October. Write a review memo listing each issue, the correct treatment, the expected direction of impact on monthly cash, and the checks you would add to the model.

Q4. Capex committee and tranche governance [L3]

The capex committee is asked to approve a ₹140 crore greenfield plant (hurdle 15%, payback ceiling 6 years) with NPV ₹22 crore and payback 6.4 years. Design (a) the AR contents you would require; (b) the tranche structure with milestones, release percentages and evidence for each gate; (c) the DoA routing including Board and shareholder approvals relevant to its debt funding; (d) the PIR plan. Explain how you would advise the committee on the payback breach.

Q5. Interview case: monthly vs 13-week cash and covenants [L2]

An interviewer gives you: budgeted FY EBITDA ₹120 crore, net debt ₹290 crore at 31 March, covenant net debt/EBITDA ≤ 2.5×, and a capex peak of ₹45 crore in Q4. Explain (a) whether the budget passes the covenant and what headroom exists; (b) how a slip of ₹15 crore capex from Q3 into Q4 would affect the March test; (c) how the 13-week forecast would help manage the year-end position; (d) two legitimate and two illegitimate ways companies "manage" year-end net debt.
