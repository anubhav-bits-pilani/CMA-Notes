Objective: Design an integrated bottom-up and top-down corporate budgeting framework connecting departmental budgets to the Master Budget.

How this week fits

Week 17 opens Phase 5 (FP&A, Annual Budgeting, Rolling Forecasts & Variance Storytelling). Phase 4 (Weeks 13–16) taught you to build an integrated three-statement model and a DCF/Long Range Plan for a business as a whole. This week turns that model inside out: instead of one analyst projecting the company from the top, dozens of budget owners each submit a slice — sales by territory, payroll by position, marketing by package, capex by project — and FP&A must stitch those slices into a single Master Budget that balances, reconciles to cash and is defensible in front of the Board.

The six topics follow the real sequence of an Indian Annual Operating Plan (AOP) cycle:

  1. AOP workflow (t1) — the governance shell: calendar, LRP-to-AOP envelopes, the target letter, templates, catchball, consolidation, lock and incentive linkage.
  2. Revenue and quota budgeting (t2) — the top line, built from volume × net price with a gross-to-net waterfall and seasonality.
  3. Opex and headcount (t3) — position-level payroll with Indian statutory costs, non-payroll opex, and Zero-Based Budgeting for discretionary spend.
  4. Capex and cash (t4) — capex tranches, cash phasing, the statutory payment calendar and revolver funding.
  5. Master budget consolidation (t5) — rolling everything into a budgeted P&L, balance sheet and cash flow.
  6. Flexible budgeting (t6) — how the locked budget is later used to evaluate performance without blaming managers for volume.

Prerequisites: CMA Final Strategic Cost Management and Performance Evaluation (budgetary control, standard costing), Week 9 (Excel structured tables and lookups), Week 10 (Power Query appends), Weeks 13–16 (three-statement linkage, corkscrews, debt schedules, LRP). What comes next: Week 18 converts this static annual budget into driver-based rolling forecasts; Week 19 uses the same granularity for price-volume-mix and EBITDA bridges; Week 21 deepens capex appraisal and CVP.

Refresh before starting

CMA functional budgets, master budget sequence and flexible budget variance analysis

Recall the CMA sequence: identify the principal budget factor (key/limiting factor — usually sales demand, sometimes capacity, a scarce raw material or skilled labour), then build the sales budget → production budget (sales + closing FG − opening FG) → materials usage and purchase budgets → direct labour → factory overheads → COGS → selling and administration → capex → cash budget → budgeted P&L and balance sheet. The master budget is the summary of all functional budgets. A fixed (static) budget is prepared for one activity level; a flexible budget recognises cost behaviour and restates allowances for the actual activity level. Variance analysis compares actual with the flexed budget for spending/efficiency and isolates the activity effect as a volume variance. Revisit the variance hierarchy: sales price, sales volume (split into mix and quantity), material price/usage (mix/yield), labour rate/efficiency/idle time, variable overhead expenditure/efficiency, fixed overhead expenditure/volume (capacity/efficiency).

Cost behaviour, CVP basics and contribution vs absorption costing

Variable costs move proportionately with the activity driver; fixed costs are constant within a relevant range; semi-variable costs have both elements (split by high-low or regression); step-fixed costs jump at capacity thresholds (one supervisor per shift, one warehouse per region). CVP: contribution per unit = price − variable cost; break-even units = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit; margin of safety = (budget − break-even) ÷ budget; operating leverage = contribution ÷ EBIT. Under marginal (contribution) costing, fixed production overhead is a period cost; under absorption costing, it is absorbed into inventory at a pre-determined rate, so profit moves with production as well as sales. Budgets for management use are usually presented in contribution format; statutory reporting (Ind AS 2) requires absorption of fixed production overheads based on normal capacity.

Indian payroll statutes: PF, ESI, gratuity, bonus, and Labour Codes wage definition

  • EPF (Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952): employer 12% of PF wages, of which 8.33% goes to EPS on wages capped at ₹15,000 (max ₹1,250/month) and the balance to EPF; EDLI 0.5% (on capped wages) and admin charges 0.5% (minimum per establishment). Employee 12%. Deposit by the 15th of the following month.
  • ESI (ESI Act, 1948): covers employees with wages up to ₹21,000/month (₹25,000 for persons with disability); employer 3.25%, employee 0.75%; contribution periods April–September and October–March; due by the 15th.
  • Gratuity (Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972): 15/26 × last drawn basic + DA × completed years (part-year over six months rounds up); eligibility after five years' continuous service (relaxed for death/disablement, and to one year for fixed-term employees under the Code on Social Security); ceiling ₹20 lakh.
  • Bonus (Payment of Bonus Act, 1965): employees earning up to ₹21,000/month; minimum 8.33%, maximum 20% of salary capped at the higher of ₹7,000 or the scheduled minimum wage.
  • Labour Codes: the Code on Wages, 2019, Industrial Relations Code, 2020, Code on Social Security, 2020 and OSH Code, 2020 were brought into force from 21 November 2025. Under the uniform "wages" definition, specified exclusions (HRA, conveyance, overtime, commission, employer PF and the like) above 50% of total remuneration are added back to wages. Rules and transitional notifications continue to evolve — verify current state before finalising a payroll budget.

Working capital cycle (DSO, DIO, DPO) and Ind AS 7 cash flow structure

DSO = receivables ÷ credit sales × days; DIO = inventory ÷ COGS × days; DPO = payables ÷ purchases (or COGS) × days; cash conversion cycle = DSO + DIO − DPO. In a monthly budget, convert days into closing balances using the relevant month's (or rolling months') flows — not annual averages. Ind AS 7 classifies cash flows into operating, investing and financing; operating cash flow may be presented by the direct method (encouraged, Ind AS 7.19) or the indirect method. Interest and dividends paid are classified consistently (for non-financial entities, interest paid is typically financing or operating; dividends paid financing). Tax paid is operating unless specifically identifiable with investing or financing activities.

Excel structured tables, SUMIFS, XLOOKUP, named ranges, data validation

Structured tables (Ctrl+T) give auto-expanding ranges and readable references such as tblRoster[Annual_CTC]. SUMIFS aggregates by multiple criteria (department, month, account) and is the workhorse of roll-ups; XLOOKUP replaces VLOOKUP/INDEX-MATCH with exact-match default, left lookups and an if_not_found argument. Named ranges (Infl_Rate, CircSwitch) make formulas auditable and are essential for a single assumptions sheet. Data validation lists (department, cost centre, account code, start month) force template submitters to pick valid values, which is what makes later SUMIFS roll-ups reliable. Also recall SUMPRODUCT for weighted sums across arrays and EOMONTH for timeline headers.

Cross-cutting concepts

Driver-based design: every budget line traced to a volume, rate or headcount driver

A driver-based budget expresses every material line as driver × rate rather than as a typed number. Revenue = cases × net price per case; freight = tonnes shipped × ₹ per tonne-km; payroll = positions × CTC × months employed; IT licences = users × ₹ per seat; rent = square feet × ₹ per sq ft. The discipline matters for three reasons. First, it makes the budget challengeable: in a catchball round FP&A can ask "why 14% volume growth with only 6% more outlets?" instead of arguing about a total. Second, it makes the budget flexible: when actual volume differs, the same drivers produce the flexed allowance used in Topic 6. Third, it makes rolling forecasts cheap (Week 18): a forecast update changes drivers, not hundreds of cells. Design rules: keep drivers and rates in separate, labelled input rows; choose the driver that actually causes the cost (units, hours, orders, headcount — not revenue by default); document the source of every rate (contract, last-twelve-months actual, vendor quote); and limit drivers to those that are material and controllable, since a model with 400 drivers is as opaque as one with none. Lines that genuinely have no driver (audit fee, a single lease) are flagged as "fixed — contract-based" so reviewers know the absence of a driver is deliberate.

Single source of truth: one assumptions sheet, input–calc–output separation, consistent timeline across all templates

Every template — sales, plant, payroll, marketing, capex — must read the same macro assumptions (inflation, FX budget rates, GST rates, statutory ceilings, increment month, interest rate) from one assumptions sheet, never retype them. Within each template, inputs (blue font, shaded), calculations (black) and outputs (summaries feeding consolidation) live on separate blocks or sheets, following FAST-style conventions. All templates share one timeline: identical column structure for April–March, the same month-end dates generated by EOMONTH, the same flags (budget year, quarter, H1/H2). When departmental files are appended in Power Query (Week 10) or loaded into EPM, an inconsistent timeline or account list is the single most common cause of broken roll-ups. A version stamp (V0 submission, V1 after round 1, V2 final) on the assumptions sheet lets you trace which version of a rate every number was built on. The test: change the payroll increment % in one cell and every department's payroll, the P&L, provisions, cash and the balance sheet update with no manual intervention.

Accrual vs cash phasing: P&L budget, balance sheet movements and cash budget must reconcile monthly

The P&L records revenue and expense when earned or incurred; the cash budget records them when collected or paid; the balance sheet holds the difference. Every month, for every flow, opening balance + accrual − cash = closing balance must hold: receivables (sales incl. GST − collections), inventory (purchases − COGS), payables (purchases incl. GST − payments), GST payable (output − input − cash paid on the 20th), TDS and PF payables (deducted − deposited on the 7th/15th), bonus and gratuity provisions (accrued monthly, paid annually), capex creditors (CWIP additions − capex payments), tax (provision − advance tax instalments). If the budget only reconciles annually, the monthly cash profile — and therefore the revolver requirement — will be wrong, typically understating the peak borrowing in festive build-up months. The reconciliation is proven by a monthly indirect cash flow (PAT + non-cash items ± working capital ± investing ± financing) that equals the change in cash from the direct cash budget.

Governance: owner per line, version history, DoA approvals, documented challenge-round changes

A budget is a commitment, so every line must have a named owner accountable for the number and its drivers (the plant head for conversion cost, the HR head for headcount, the category owner for travel). FP&A owns the process, not the numbers. Maintain version history — V0 bottom-up submission, V1/V2 after each challenge round, final locked — with a change log recording who changed what, why, and on whose approval. The Delegation of Authority (DoA) matrix specifies who approves each line and threshold (e.g., BU head approves departmental opex up to ₹50 lakh variance from envelope; CFO up to ₹5 crore; Board approves the consolidated AOP, capex above ₹25 crore and borrowing limits under Sec 179/180 of the Companies Act, 2013). Challenge-round changes must be documented: "Marketing ATL reduced ₹3.2 crore — package M5 deferred to H2 per CFO review 14 Jan" is auditable; an unexplained overwritten cell is not. Internal auditors increasingly review the budgeting process as part of internal financial controls, so this documentation also protects the finance team.

Exercise needs: consumer goods sales/GTN build, production and RM purchase budgets, payroll roster, marketing ZBB packages, capex tranche phasing, monthly cash with revolver

The week's exercise demands six engines that must interlock. Sales/GTN: volume by category × channel × month using seasonality indices, priced at list price with on-invoice and off-invoice deductions down to net sales value (NSV), with GST computed separately for cash. Production and RM purchases: production = sales + desired closing FG − opening FG; RM purchases = usage + closing RM − opening RM, priced at budget RM rates with import/GST effects. Payroll roster: one row per position (existing, vacancy, new hire, exit) with start/exit month, CTC components, increment month and statutory costs. Marketing ZBB: decision packages ranked by return, funded down to the envelope cut-off. Capex tranches: projects phased by milestone payments (advance, delivery, commissioning, retention) separately from capitalisation and depreciation. Monthly cash with revolver: receipts and payments by month including GST, TDS, PF/ESI and advance tax dates, a minimum cash balance and a revolving credit draw/repay mechanism. Each engine outputs a monthly summary on a common account list that feeds the P&L, balance sheet and cash flow.

Q1. Static vs flexible budget for a packaging plant [L2] (25 marks)

Sudarshan Packaging Ltd budgeted the following for Q2 FY 2027-28: 50,000 cartons at ₹400 each; direct material ₹120, direct labour ₹60 and variable overhead ₹40 per carton; variable selling commission 5% of revenue; fixed production overhead ₹30,00,000; fixed administration ₹18,00,000; supervision is step-fixed at ₹6,00,000 per supervisor, one supervisor per 20,000 cartons or part thereof.
