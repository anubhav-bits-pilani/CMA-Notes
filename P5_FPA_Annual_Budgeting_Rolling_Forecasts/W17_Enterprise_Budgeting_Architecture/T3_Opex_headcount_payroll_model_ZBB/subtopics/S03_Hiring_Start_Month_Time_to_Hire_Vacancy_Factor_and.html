Hiring Start Month, Time-to-Hire, Vacancy Factor and Attrition

Scope: Hiring start month, time-to-hire lag, vacancy factor and attrition assumptions

Why it matters

Payroll cost depends as much on when people are on the payroll as on what they are paid. New hires rarely join on the date the hiring manager imagines; positions vacated by resignations stay empty for months; and attrition in India's corporate and GCC workforce commonly runs at 12–25% a year. A budget that assumes every approved seat is filled all year, or that new hires join on the requested date, will systematically overstate payroll — and then "underspend" every month. The four assumptions in this subtopic — start month, time-to-hire, vacancy factor and attrition — convert the roster into a realistic cost profile without weakening headcount control.

Core notes

Hiring start month

For each new position the budget needs an effective start month — the month the employee is on payroll:

\[ \text{Start month} = \text{Requisition approval month} + \text{Time-to-hire} + \text{Notice period of candidate} \]

The hiring manager typically gives the "need-by" date; HR knows the realistic lag. Use HR's realistic timing for cost, while keeping the approved position from its approval date for headcount control.

Time-to-hire lag

Time-to-hire (requisition opened → offer accepted) plus the candidate's notice period (commonly 30–90 days in India; 90 days is standard in IT/GCCs and senior roles) gives the total lag. Typical totals:

Role type Time-to-offer Notice period Total lag to joining
Plant operators, sales reps 3–6 weeks 15–30 days 1.5–2.5 months
Mid-level corporate roles 6–10 weeks 60 days 3.5–4.5 months
Senior / specialist / tech roles 8–14 weeks 90 days 5–6.5 months

Partial months: if joining is mid-month, cost half the month or round to the next month consistently.

Attrition assumptions

Annual attrition rate:

\[ \alpha = \frac{\text{Exits in year}}{\text{Average headcount}} \]

Segment by function and grade (sales and junior roles higher; senior management lower) and separate voluntary attrition (resignations) from involuntary (terminations, retirements). Monthly rate ≈ \(\alpha / 12\). Use three years' history adjusted for the labour market outlook.

Vacancy factor

The vacancy factor captures the cost effect of positions being empty between an exit and the backfill's joining:

\[ \text{Vacancy factor} \approx \alpha \times \frac{L}{12} \]

where \(L\) is the average backfill lag in months (from exit to replacement joining). Budgeted payroll for existing and backfilled positions is then reduced by this factor:

\[ \text{Budgeted payroll} = \text{Full-occupancy payroll} \times (1 - \text{Vacancy factor}) + \text{Backfill premium} \]

The backfill premium recognises that replacements are often hired at a higher salary than the leaver (e.g., 10–15% higher), and recruitment costs (agency fees, joining bonuses) increase.

Illustration: a 400-person function, full-occupancy payroll ₹96 crore, voluntary attrition 18%, backfill lag 3 months. Vacancy factor = 18% × 3/12 = 4.5% → saving ₹4.32 crore. Backfill premium: 72 exits a year × average CTC ₹24 lakh × 12% premium × average 6 months on payroll in the year ≈ ₹1.04 crore. Recruitment cost: 72 × ₹24 lakh × 8.33% agency fee = ₹1.44 crore (opex, not payroll). Net payroll = 96 − 4.32 + 1.04 = ₹92.72 crore.

Two modelling approaches

  1. Top-down vacancy factor — apply a percentage reduction to payroll (by department) as a single line "vacancy savings". Simple; preserves a clean roster; common for large populations.
  2. Explicit modelling — generate expected exits by month (statistical, not named) and backfill rows with lagged start months. More accurate for small departments and when attrition is concentrated (e.g., post-bonus resignations in April–June after variable pay is paid in June).

Many companies use explicit modelling for known exits and a vacancy factor for unknown attrition. Whatever the method, show vacancy savings as a visible line, not buried in each position, so the CFO can decide whether to hold it centrally.

Seasonality of attrition and hiring

In India, resignations peak after annual increments and bonus payouts (April–July) and after the festive bonus; campus hires join in June–August. Hiring freezes in Q4 to protect the budget are common. Phase exits and joins accordingly rather than evenly.

Excel implementation

=[@Approval_Month]+ROUNDUP(([@Time_to_Offer_Days]+[@Notice_Days])/30,0)
=Full_Payroll*(1-XLOOKUP([@Dept],tblAttr[Dept],tblAttr[Attrition])*XLOOKUP([@Dept],tblAttr[Dept],tblAttr[Lag_Months])/12)

Keep attrition and lag by department in an assumptions table; never type them into the grid.

Headcount vs cost control

Headcount reporting should show approved positions, filled positions and vacancies separately. A vacancy saving in the budget does not reduce approved positions — it is a cost forecast. This distinction prevents managers from treating budgeted vacancy savings as permission to hire beyond the approved count.

Edge cases & judgement calls

  • Small teams (fewer than 20 people): a statistical vacancy factor is unreliable; model known exits explicitly or apply none.
  • Hiring freeze or restructuring changes both the attrition (more voluntary exits) and backfill policy (not all exits backfilled); model the non-backfill share explicitly.
  • Critical roles (plant safety officer, statutory roles such as company secretary) may require immediate interim cover through contractors — budget contract cost during the vacancy.
  • Campus hiring cohorts join together; budget them as a block with a start month and expected first-year attrition.
  • Exit costs — leave encashment payout, gratuity for those with five-plus years, notice pay buyouts — are cash items; accruals already sit in the balance sheet but cash timing must be in the cash budget.

Key takeaways

  • Start month = approval + time-to-offer + notice period; use HR's realistic lag for cost.
  • Notice periods of 60–90 days in India make total lags of 3–6 months common for corporate roles.
  • Vacancy factor ≈ attrition rate × backfill lag ÷ 12; show it as a visible line.
  • Add backfill premiums and recruitment costs so vacancy savings are not overstated.
  • Phase attrition and hiring with Indian patterns (post-increment resignations, campus joining).
  • Keep approved headcount control separate from cost forecasts of vacancies.

Practice Questions

Q1. Computing start months and cost [L1]

Five new positions are approved in March 2027: (a) sales rep — time-to-offer 30 days, notice 15 days; (b) plant engineer — 45 days, 30 days; (c) FP&A manager — 60 days, 60 days; (d) data scientist — 75 days, 90 days; (e) head of procurement — 90 days, 90 days. Annual fixed gross: ₹4.8 lakh, ₹9 lakh, ₹24 lakh, ₹30 lakh, ₹60 lakh. Compute each start month (round up to the next whole month after 1 April 2027) and the FY 2027-28 fixed gross cost.

💡 Hint: Count days from 1 April and convert to months using a 30-day convention.

Q2. Vacancy factor and net payroll [L2]

A 1,200-person GCC has full-occupancy payroll of ₹276 crore, attrition of 22% and an average backfill lag of 3.5 months. Backfills are hired at a 15% premium to leavers' salaries; agency fees are 8.33% of annual CTC for 60% of backfills. Compute the vacancy saving, backfill premium (assume backfills are on payroll for an average of 5 months of the year), recruitment cost and net payroll.

Q3. Explicit vs factor approach [L2]

For a 12-person legal team with one known exit in September and historical attrition of 8%, compare the explicit and factor approaches, and recommend one with reasons.

Q4. Persistent underspend investigation [L3]

Payroll has been 4–6% under budget in each of the last three years while approved headcount was never exceeded. Design the analysis you would run to explain the pattern and propose budget-methodology changes for FY 2027-28, including how you would present the change to department heads who fear losing "their" budget.
