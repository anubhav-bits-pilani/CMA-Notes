Roadmap topic: Opex & Headcount Budgeting: Role-based payroll model (CTC, PF/Gratuity, hiring start month, mid-year appraisal %) plus Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB) for discretionary spend

Overview

Employee cost is typically the largest controllable cost in service companies, GCCs and corporate functions, and the second or third largest in manufacturing and FMCG. In India it is also the most statute-laden: provident fund, ESI, gratuity, statutory bonus, leave encashment and — since November 2025 — the Labour Codes' uniform wage definition all depend on how the CTC is structured and on monthly wage thresholds. A payroll budget built as "last year's total × (1 + increment %)" is wrong in almost every month: it ignores when people join and leave, when increments take effect, which statutory caps apply and how vacancies and attrition really behave. The professional standard is a position-level roster — one row per position — with start and exit months, CTC components, increment timing and statutory cost rules, generating a position × month cost grid.

Non-payroll opex — rent, IT/SaaS licences, travel, professional fees, marketing — needs a different discipline: classify each line by cost behaviour (fixed, variable, step) and driver, and challenge discretionary spend. Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB) is the classic challenge tool: instead of starting from last year's spend, every rupee is justified from zero through decision packages ranked against a funding cut-off. Its modern FMCG variant, Zero-Based Spend with cost-category owners, has been used by Unilever (and HUL in India), Kraft Heinz, AB InBev and Mondelez to make cost discipline repeatable rather than a one-off cut.

The learning sequence: start with the roster (s01) and the CTC build-up with statutory costs (s02); add hiring timing, vacancy factor and attrition (s03) and increment and promotion timing (s04). Then move to non-payroll opex and cost behaviour (s05), classic ZBB mechanics (s06) and ZBB variants with category ownership (s07). The concepts give you the formulas you will use in every payroll model: monthly cost per FTE with flags, employer PF, gratuity, ESI and bonus, the Labour Code 50% wage test, FTE vs headcount, and the ZBB decision package.

Related concepts to study

Ind AS 19 actuarial valuation vs budget proxy

Ind AS 19 actuarial valuation (Projected Unit Credit) vs budget proxy for gratuity/leave (→ Phase 1)

Gratuity is a defined benefit plan and compensated absences (leave encashment) are other long-term benefits; Ind AS 19 requires measurement using the Projected Unit Credit method (para 67), with actuarial assumptions for discount rate (G-sec yields), salary escalation, attrition and mortality. The P&L charge comprises current service cost and net interest; remeasurements of gratuity go to OCI (but leave remeasurements go to P&L). Budgets usually use a proxy — 4.81% of basic for gratuity, days of leave accrued × daily basic — because a full valuation per scenario is impractical. Reconcile the proxy to the actuary's projected service cost for next year (actuarial reports usually give it) and budget OCI separately as nil. Large differences arise in young, high-attrition workforces (proxy overstates) and in older, long-tenure ones (proxy understates).

Activity-Based Budgeting and cost-to-serve

Activity-Based Budgeting and cost-to-serve as alternatives to incremental budgeting

Activity-Based Budgeting (ABB) derives resource budgets from planned activity volumes: forecast output → activities required (orders processed, deliveries, calls, invoices) → resources needed at standard consumption rates → cost. It replaces "last year + x%" with a demand-driven build, and unlike ZBB it is not about justifying existence but about sizing to demand. Cost-to-serve applies activity costing to customers and channels — order handling, delivery, merchandising, claims processing — revealing that a small MT account with frequent small drops may be unprofitable despite high gross margin. In opex budgeting, ABB is ideal for transactional functions (shared services, logistics, customer service); ZBB for discretionary spend; incremental budgeting only for genuinely stable fixed costs.

Excel: SUMPRODUCT, XLOOKUP and structured tables

Excel: SUMPRODUCT, XLOOKUP on roster tables, structured tables for position-level models (→ Week 9)

A roster is a natural structured table (tblRoster) with one row per position and columns for department, grade, status, start month, exit month, fixed gross, basic %, increment %, PF basis. XLOOKUP pulls grade-level assumptions (increment % by rating, medical premium by grade) and statutory parameters from the assumptions sheet. The monthly cost grid uses flags — (F$3>=[@Start])*(F$3<=[@Exit]) — and SUMPRODUCT aggregates without helper columns, e.g., =SUMPRODUCT((tblRoster[Dept]="Sales")*(tblRoster[Start]<=6)*(tblRoster[Exit]>=6)) for June sales headcount. Structured references auto-extend as positions are added, avoiding broken ranges, and SUMIFS on the grid produces department × month summaries for consolidation.

HR metrics

HR metrics: span of control, revenue per FTE, attrition %, cost per hire

These metrics turn the payroll budget into challengeable ratios. Span of control = direct reports per manager; narrow spans (below 5–6 in corporate functions) indicate excess layers. Revenue per FTE (and employee cost as % of revenue) tests productivity against prior years and peers. Attrition % = exits in the period ÷ average headcount; it drives backfill hiring, vacancy savings and recruitment cost. Cost per hire = (recruiter fees, job portals, referral bonuses, joining bonuses, internal recruiter cost) ÷ hires; Indian agency fees typically run 8.33%–15% of annual CTC. Budget recruitment cost from planned hires × cost per hire, and use these metrics in catchball to challenge headcount additions.

EPM workforce planning modules

EPM: Anaplan/Adaptive workforce planning modules with employee-level lists

Anaplan and Workday Adaptive Planning provide workforce planning models with employee-level and position-level lists (existing employees from HRMS, TBH — to-be-hired — positions, and terminations). Each list item carries attributes (cost centre, grade, location, salary, start date), and modules calculate monthly salary, benefits and taxes using rules tables (PF rates, ESI threshold, increment month). Planners add TBH positions, transfer employees between cost centres and set exit dates via forms, with workflow approvals; results post to the P&L by account. Data privacy matters: salary-level data is restricted by role, and summary-level views are given to budget owners. An Excel roster designed with the same fields migrates easily to these tools.

Common pitfalls

Budgeting new hires for 12 months

Budgeting new hires for 12 months instead of from hiring start month, overstating payroll

Approved new positions are often costed at full annual CTC because the template has no start-month field or because HR wants to "protect the budget". A position starting in October costs only six months; at scale, this overstates payroll by 5–10% and creates a predictable underspend that is then reallocated to other spending. Correct treatment: every new position carries a start month (from the hiring plan) plus time-to-hire lag, and the cost grid applies a start flag. Detect it by comparing budgeted FTE with closing headcount — if FTE ≈ closing headcount despite many new hires, start months are missing.

Full-year increments and ignored statutory caps

Applying increment to the full year when effective from mid-year; ignoring statutory cap interactions on PF/ESI

When increments take effect from 1 July, April–June remain at old pay; applying the increment for 12 months overstates cost by about a quarter of the increment cost. Separately, statutory costs are often applied as flat percentages of CTC, ignoring that EPS is capped at ₹15,000 wages, that many employers restrict PF to ₹15,000, that ESI stops once wages exceed ₹21,000 (from the next contribution period) and that bonus eligibility ends above ₹21,000. Increments can push employees across these thresholds. Correct treatment: increment flags by month and component-level statutory calculations with caps (c01–c04). Detect it by recomputing two sample employees by hand and by checking ESI headcount before and after the increment month.

ZBB as a one-off cost cut

Treating ZBB as a one-off cost cut rather than a repeatable process with category ownership

Companies often run ZBB as a crisis exercise — a consultant-led "start from zero" that delivers 10–20% savings in year one, after which spending creeps back as the process lapses. The mistake is confusing a cost-cutting event with a management system. Correct treatment: institutionalise it with cost-category owners who own policies and prices across the company, budget owners who own volumes, recurring decision-package reviews each AOP, KPIs (₹ per FTE for travel, ₹ per user for IT) and monthly tracking (s07). Detect it by checking whether category KPIs, owners and policies exist in the year after the exercise.

Ignoring attrition-to-backfill lag

Ignoring attrition-to-backfill lag, which creates systematic payroll underspend versus budget

A budget that assumes every position is filled all year implicitly assumes zero attrition or instant replacement. In reality, with 15–20% attrition and 2–3 month backfill lags, 3–5% of position-months are vacant at any time. The payroll budget then underspends every month, which looks like good control but is actually a forecasting error — and the "savings" are often spent elsewhere. Correct treatment: apply a vacancy factor or model expected exits and backfill lags explicitly (s03), while keeping the approved position count for headcount control. Detect it by comparing three years of budget vs actual payroll: consistent underspend of 3–6% signals a missing vacancy assumption.

Practice Questions

Q1. Department payroll budget from a roster [L3]

The finance department of a Chennai FMCG company has 14 positions for FY 2027-28: 11 existing (annual fixed gross totalling ₹2.64 crore, basic 40% of fixed gross, nine with PF on actual basic and two capped at ₹15,000), one vacancy to be filled from 1 August (₹14 lakh), one new position from 1 November (₹22 lakh), and one exit on 31 December (₹9.6 lakh) with a backfill after a two-month lag at ₹10.2 lakh. Increment 8.5% effective 1 July for existing staff (excluding the exiting employee). Employer PF 12% + 1% admin/EDLI; gratuity 4.81% of basic; group medical ₹16,500 per head per annum; variable pay 10% of fixed gross paid in June on FY 2026-27 fixed gross for existing staff. (a) Build the position × month grid. (b) Compute annual cost, closing headcount and average FTE. (c) Show the overstatement if all positions were costed for 12 months with increments from April.

💡 Hint: Treat the two capped-PF employees with a ₹1,800 per month employer PF ceiling plus admin/EDLI on capped wages.

Q2. Labour Code impact on a payroll budget [L2]

A 600-employee plant has CTC structures with basic at 30% of total remuneration, HRA at 15%, special allowance at 45% and other exclusions at 10%. Average total remuneration is ₹40,000 per month. Employer PF is paid on actual basic (no cap). Assume special allowance is part of wages. (a) Apply the Code on Wages 50% test. (b) Compute the change in annual employer PF and gratuity accrual for the plant if wages must be at least 50% and the company restructures to exactly 50%. (c) Advise the CFO on budget options.

Q3. ZBB for marketing and indirect spend [L3]

A company's FY 2026-27 indirect spend is ₹84 crore: travel ₹12 crore, consulting ₹15 crore, IT ₹22 crore, facilities ₹18 crore, marketing services ₹17 crore. The CFO wants ZBB for FY 2027-28 with a 12% saving target. (a) Design the decision units, packages and levels of effort for two categories. (b) Propose category owners and the category owner × budget owner matrix. (c) Define KPIs and tracking that make the savings sustainable.

Q4. Opex behaviour classification [L2]

Classify and budget the following for FY 2027-28: office rent ₹6.2 lakh per month with 5% escalation from 1 October; SaaS CRM at ₹2,100 per user per month for 180 users rising to 240 users from 1 September; travel at ₹28,000 per sales trip with 1,050 trips; statutory audit fee ₹45 lakh; warehouse rent of ₹3.5 lakh per month per warehouse, with a second warehouse required when monthly throughput exceeds 2,000 tonnes (throughput 1,600 tonnes April–September and 2,300 tonnes October–March). Identify fixed, variable and step costs and compute annual budgets.
