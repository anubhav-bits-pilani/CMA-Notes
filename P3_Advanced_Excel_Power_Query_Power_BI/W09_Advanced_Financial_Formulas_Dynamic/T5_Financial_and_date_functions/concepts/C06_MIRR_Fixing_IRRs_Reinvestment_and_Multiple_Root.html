Key concept

MIRR: Fixing IRR's Reinvestment and Multiple-Root Problems

Concept: =MIRR(values, finance_rate, reinvest_rate) fixes IRR's reinvestment assumption and the multiple-IRR problem

Statement

=MIRR(values, finance_rate, reinvest_rate)

\[ \text{MIRR} = \left( \frac{\sum_{t:\,CF_t>0} CF_t (1+r_{re})^{\,n-t}}{-\sum_{t:\,CF_t<0} \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r_{fin})^{t}}} \right)^{1/n} - 1 \]

where \(CF_t\) is the flow at period \(t = 0, \dots, n\), \(n\) is the number of periods (number of values − 1), \(r_{re}\) is the reinvestment rate applied to positive flows (compounded to period \(n\)) and \(r_{fin}\) is the finance rate applied to negative flows (discounted to period 0).

Explanation

IRR has two well-known weaknesses:

  1. Implicit reinvestment at the IRR. IRR is the single rate at which all flows discount to zero, which mathematically implies interim inflows earn the IRR until the end. For a project with a 30% IRR, that means assuming every rupee returned can be redeployed at 30% — rarely true. High-IRR, front-loaded projects are flattered.
  2. Multiple or no IRRs. With more than one sign change (e.g. outlay, inflows, then a large decommissioning or restoration outflow), the NPV curve can cross zero more than once. Excel returns whichever root its iteration finds from the guess.

MIRR collapses all negative flows into one PV at t = 0 (using the finance rate — the cost of funding outflows) and all positive flows into one FV at t = n (using a realistic reinvestment rate — often the WACC). With one outflow and one inflow, there is exactly one rate linking them, so MIRR is always unique and its reinvestment assumption is explicit.

Limitations:

  • Equal periods only, like IRR. For dated flows, build a dated version manually with XNPV-style factors.
  • Rate inputs drive the answer. Document why the finance and reinvestment rates were chosen (cost of debt, WACC, treasury yield).
  • Ranking. MIRR improves on IRR but still ignores scale; NPV remains the primary criterion for mutually exclusive projects.
  • MIRR requires at least one positive and one negative value (#DIV/0! otherwise).

Illustration

Conventional project (₹ lakh): −100, 30, 40, 50, 20; finance rate 10%, reinvestment rate 12%.

  • IRR = 15.3%.
  • MIRR: FV of inflows at 12% to year 4 = 30(1.12)³ + 40(1.12)² + 50(1.12) + 20 = 42.15 + 50.18 + 56.00 + 20.00 = 168.32; PV of outflows = 100. MIRR = (168.32/100)^(1/4) − 1 = 13.9%.

MIRR is lower because interim inflows earn 12%, not 15.3%.

Non-conventional project (₹ crore): −100, +230, −132 (two sign changes).

  • NPV = 0 at both 10% and 20%: =IRR(C1:C3, 5%) → 10%, =IRR(C1:C3, 25%) → 20%.
  • MIRR at finance 10%, reinvest 12%: PV of outflows = 100 + 132/1.1² = 209.09; FV of inflows = 230 × 1.12 = 257.60; MIRR = (257.60/209.09)^(1/2) − 1 ≈ 11.0%. One answer.

Q2. Mining closure costs [L3]

A quarry project costs ₹30 crore, generates ₹14 crore a year for 4 years, then requires ₹28 crore of restoration in year 5. Explain why IRR may be unreliable here, how you would detect it in Excel, and what metrics you would present to the investment committee instead.
