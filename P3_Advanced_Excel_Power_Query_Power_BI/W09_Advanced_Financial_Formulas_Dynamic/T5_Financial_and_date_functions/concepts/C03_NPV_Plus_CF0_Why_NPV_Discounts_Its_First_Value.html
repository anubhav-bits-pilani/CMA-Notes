Key concept

NPV Plus CF0: Why NPV Discounts Its First Value

Concept: =NPV(r, CF1:CFn) + CF0: NPV discounts its first value by one full period

Statement

=NPV(r, CF1:CFn) + CF0

\[ \text{Net present value} = CF_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^{t}} \]

where \(CF_0\) is the flow at t = 0 (today, undiscounted), \(CF_t\) the flow at the end of period \(t\), and \(r\) the discount rate per period. Excel's NPV function computes only the summation term: it treats the first value in its range as arriving at the end of period 1.

Explanation

The naming is misleading: Excel's NPV is really "present value of a stream starting one period from now". The finance definition of net present value includes the initial outlay at t = 0, which should not be discounted. Hence the pattern: discount periods 1 to n with NPV, then add CF0 outside.

If you include CF0 in the range — =NPV(r, CF0:CFn) — every flow is pushed one period later, so the result is

\[ \frac{\text{true NPV}}{1 + r} \]

The sign and accept/reject decision are unchanged (both are positive or both negative), which is why the error survives casual review, but the value is understated in magnitude. For a valuation, a lease liability or an impairment test, that is a misstatement of the amount itself.

Related rules:

  • Equal periods. NPV assumes each value is one period apart. Monthly flows need a monthly rate; irregular dates need XNPV (c04).
  • Blanks are skipped, not zero. A blank cell in CF1:CFn makes later flows move one period earlier. Fill zeros.
  • Advance payments. For an annuity due (payments at the start of each period, e.g. rent in advance), the first payment is CF0: =NPV(r, CF2:CFn) + CF1 or =PV(r, n, -pmt, 0, 1).
  • Mid-year convention. NPV cannot do half-periods directly; multiply the NPV result by \((1+r)^{0.5}\) to shift all flows half a period earlier, or use explicit discount factors.
  • IRR is different. IRR treats its first value as t = 0, so =NPV(IRR(range), CF1:CFn) + CF0 returns zero — a useful integrity check. Note that =NPV(IRR(range), range) also returns zero (because it equals true NPV ÷ (1 + r), and 0 ÷ anything is 0), so the IRR test cannot detect the CF0-inside error; test the NPV formula at the hurdle rate against a hand-built discount-factor row instead.

Illustration

Expansion project (₹ lakh): CF0 = −100 at 1-Apr-2025; CF1–CF4 = 30, 40, 50, 20 at each FY-end; r = 12%.

Formula Result Comment
=NPV(12%, C2:C5) + C1 6.97 Correct
=NPV(12%, C1:C5) 6.23 = 6.97 ÷ 1.12, understated
=XNPV(12%, C1:C5, B1:B5) 6.99 Day-count difference only

Lease example: 5 annual payments of ₹24 lakh in advance at 10%. Correct PV of all payments = =NPV(10%, D2:D5) + D1 = 24 + 76.08 = ₹100.08 lakh; the Ind AS 116 lease liability excludes the payment made at commencement, so liability = ₹76.08 lakh.

Connections

Central to s03 (NPV vs XNPV) and s06 (lease liabilities in advance vs arrears). It underpins capital budgeting in Weeks 21–22 and the DCF in Weeks 15–16, where the mid-year convention and stub periods modify the timing. The checklist item "explain NPV vs XNPV" depends on this concept, as does the IRR/NPV consistency check.

Practice Questions

Q1. Correct the capex model [L1]

A capex appraisal shows =NPV(13%, B4:B12), where B4 is the initial investment of −₹45 crore and B5:B12 are eight annual inflows. Rewrite the formula and state the exact relationship between the old and corrected values.

Q2. Advance rentals [L2]

A company receives annual lease rentals of ₹18 lakh in advance for 7 years under a finance lease (lessor accounting). At an implicit rate of 9%, write two different correct formulas for the PV of the rentals and explain why =NPV(9%, all 7 rentals) is wrong.

💡 Hint: One formula uses NPV plus the first flow; the other uses PV with the type argument.

Q3. A test that cannot fail [L2]

A reviewer checks a model by computing =NPV(IRR(C1:C6), C1:C6), gets zero, and concludes the model's NPV convention is correct. Explain why this test cannot detect the CF0-inside error, and design a check (using a discount-factor row) that would detect it.
