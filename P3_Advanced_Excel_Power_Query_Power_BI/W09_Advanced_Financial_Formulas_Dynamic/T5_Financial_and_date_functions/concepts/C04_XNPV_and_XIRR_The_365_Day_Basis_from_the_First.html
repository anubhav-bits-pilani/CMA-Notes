Key concept

XNPV and XIRR: The 365-Day Basis from the First Date

Concept: =XNPV(r, values, dates) and =XIRR(values, dates, [guess]) use a 365-day basis from the first date

Statement

=XNPV(r, values, dates)
=XIRR(values, dates, [guess])

\[ \text{XNPV}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{P_i}{(1+r)^{(d_i - d_1)/365}} \qquad \text{XIRR} = r^{*} \text{ such that } \text{XNPV}(r^{*}) = 0 \]

where \(P_i\) is the cash flow on date \(d_i\), \(d_1\) is the first date in the dates array, \(r\) is an annual effective rate and the exponent is the elapsed time in years on a fixed 365-day basis.

Explanation

Both functions measure time in days from the first date and convert to years by dividing by 365 — always 365, even across leap years. Three consequences follow:

  1. The first date is the anchor. The first flow has exponent 0 and is undiscounted. For a valuation as of a date with no cash flow on that date, insert a zero flow on the valuation date as the first row; otherwise the PV is computed as of the first cash-flow date. No date may be earlier than the first date (#NUM!).
  2. Rates are annual effective. XIRR's output can be compared directly with an annual hurdle or WACC; XNPV's input must be an annual effective rate even if flows are monthly.
  3. Leap-year drift is small but real. A flow exactly one calendar year after a 29 February-spanning anchor is at 366/365 = 1.0027 years. Reconciling an XNPV to an NPV with annual periods will show small differences for this reason.

Practical requirements:

  • values and dates must be the same size; dates must be true dates (text from ERP exports → #VALUE!).
  • XIRR needs at least one positive and one negative value (#NUM! otherwise), and may need a guess for unusual patterns (default 10%).
  • Dates need not be sorted after the first, but sorted data is easier to review.
  • Non-numeric values in values produce #VALUE!; blanks are a problem — use 0.

Typical uses: SIP and mutual fund returns (XIRR is the standard measure on Indian investment platforms), PE/VC fund IRR from capital calls and distributions, project returns with milestone flows, DCFs with stub periods or specific flow dates, deferred consideration, and lease liabilities with irregular payment dates.

Illustration

Investment (₹ lakh), valuation and return at 12%:

Date Flow
15-Jun-2025 −100
31-Dec-2025 30
30-Sep-2026 40
31-Mar-2027 50
30-Jun-2028 20
  • =XNPV(12%, C2:C6, B2:B6) = 17.73, valued as of 15-Jun-2025 (the first date).
  • =XIRR(C2:C6, B2:B6) = 25.2% p.a.
  • Treating the same flows as equally spaced years: NPV = 6.97 and IRR = 15.3%.

Valuing as of 1-Apr-2025 instead: add a first row (1-Apr-2025, 0). Each exponent increases by 75/365, so XNPV falls by a factor of \(1.12^{-75/365}\): 17.73 × 0.9770 ≈ 17.32.

Practice Questions

Q1. Anchor the valuation [L1]

Flows of ₹−50 crore on 10-Aug-2025 and ₹+18, +22, +25 crore on 31-Mar-2026, 31-Mar-2027 and 31-Mar-2028 are to be valued as of 30-Jun-2025 at 11%. Set up the values/dates arrays and the XNPV formula, and explain what value you get if you omit the anchor row.

Q2. PE fund XIRR [L2]

A fund called capital of ₹10 crore (5-May-2021), ₹15 crore (18-Jan-2022) and ₹8 crore (30-Nov-2022), distributed ₹6 crore (12-Mar-2024) and ₹14 crore (20-Sep-2025), and has a NAV of ₹31 crore on 31-Mar-2026. Set up the XIRR calculation, state the sign of each flow, and explain how the NAV is treated.

💡 Hint: The NAV is treated as if it were a distribution on the valuation date.

Q3. Reconcile XNPV and NPV [L2]

An analyst finds that NPV with annual periods gives ₹212.4 crore and XNPV with FY-end dates gives ₹212.9 crore for the same project. Give two reasons for the difference and state which is more defensible for an Ind AS 36 value-in-use calculation.
